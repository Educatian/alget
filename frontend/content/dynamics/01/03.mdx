On final approach, a pilot lines up with a runway but points the nose noticeably to one side. To an observer on the ground the aircraft tracks straight down the centerline, yet the fuselage is "crabbed" into the wind. Nothing is wrong with the controls. The pilot is solving a relative-motion problem in real time: the velocity through the air and the velocity over the ground are two different vectors, and only one of them keeps the wheels on the runway.

All motion is relative. A train can be moving at high speed relative to the ground while a passenger quietly sipping coffee feels almost at rest relative to the seat. In engineering the key question is never just "How fast is it moving?" but rather:

**Moving relative to what frame of reference?**

That one question matters in highway safety, drone navigation, robotics, river crossing, spacecraft docking, and collision analysis. Getting the frame wrong is one of the most common ways a kinematics calculation can be internally consistent yet physically meaningless.

4. **Solve** translating-frame problems such as river crossing, wind correction, and closing rate
5. **Extend** the relative formulation to acceleration between translating frames

## The Core Equation

The velocity of particle B relative to particle A is:

$$
\boxed{\vec{v}_{B/A} = \vec{v}_B - \vec{v}_A}
$$

Read this carefully as "the velocity of B as observed by someone riding on A." Both $\vec{v}_B$ and $\vec{v}_A$ are measured in the same fixed (ground) frame; their difference is what the moving observer on A actually sees.

An equivalent rearrangement is:

$$
\vec{v}_B = \vec{v}_A + \vec{v}_{B/A}
$$

This addition form is the workhorse. It says a ground velocity is the velocity of a moving platform plus the velocity measured relative to that platform. When you chain frames together, the relative subscripts cancel like fractions:

$$
\vec{v}_{B/G} = \vec{v}_{B/A} + \vec{v}_{A/G}
$$

The "$A$" cancels, leaving B relative to the ground. This bookkeeping rule is what keeps multi-frame problems (boat, water, shore or plane, air, ground) from collapsing into nonsense.

> **Key Insight:** Relative motion is vector subtraction, not speed subtraction. Direction is carried at every step, even in one dimension where it appears only as a sign.

## Why Reference Frames Matter

Reference frames are not decorative labels. They change the meaning of the numbers.

- A cyclist moving at $8 \text{ m/s}$ relative to the road is at rest relative to the bicycle seat.
- A boat moving north in still water has a different ground velocity once a river current pushes it east.
- A sensor mounted on a robot arm may see a moving part as stationary relative to one joint and fast-moving relative to the room.

If you mix frames accidentally, the algebra can look correct while the answer is physically wrong. In this chapter we assume every frame is **translating** (no rotation), so velocities simply add and subtract. Rotating frames introduce extra terms (the transport and Coriolis terms) that belong to a later chapter.

| Notation | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| $\vec{v}_A$, $\vec{v}_B$ | Velocities measured in the fixed ground frame |
| $\vec{v}_{B/A}$ | Velocity of B as seen by an observer moving with A |
| $\vec{v}_{A/G}$ | Velocity of A relative to ground (same as $\vec{v}_A$) |

## Relative Motion in 1D

For straight-line motion the equation reduces to signed subtraction. Choose a positive direction first, then attach signs before any arithmetic.

### Worked Example 3.1: Two Cars on a Highway

**Problem:** Car A travels east at $25 \text{ m/s}$; Car B travels west at $20 \text{ m/s}$ on the same road. What is the velocity of B relative to A?

**Step 1: Choose a frame.** Let east be positive, so $v_A = +25 \text{ m/s}$ and $v_B = -20 \text{ m/s}$.

**Step 2: Apply the relative equation.**

$$
v_{B/A} = v_B - v_A = (-20) - (+25) = -45 \text{ m/s}
$$

**Step 3: Interpret.**

$$
v_{B/A} = \boxed{-45 \text{ m/s}}
$$

To the driver of Car A, Car B approaches at $45 \text{ m/s}$ from the east. The negative sign does not mean the speed is "bad"; it tells you the relative motion points opposite the chosen positive direction. This closing speed, not either car's individual speed, is what governs collision energy and required stopping distance.

## Relative Motion in 2D

When motion is planar, treat each component separately:

$$
\vec{v}_{B/A} = (v_{Bx} - v_{Ax})\,\hat{i} + (v_{By} - v_{Ay})\,\hat{j}
$$

The magnitude and direction follow from the components:

$$
|\vec{v}_{B/A}| = \sqrt{(v_{Bx} - v_{Ax})^2 + (v_{By} - v_{Ay})^2}, \qquad \theta = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{v_{By}-v_{Ay}}{v_{Bx}-v_{Ax}}\right)
$$

### Worked Example 3.2: Aircraft in Wind

**Problem:** A plane has airspeed $200 \text{ m/s}$ due north (velocity relative to the air). The wind blows $50 \text{ m/s}$ due east (air relative to ground). Find the plane's ground velocity.

**Step 1: Chain the frames.**

$$
\vec{v}_{plane/ground} = \vec{v}_{plane/air} + \vec{v}_{air/ground} = (50)\hat{i} + (200)\hat{j} \ \text{m/s}
$$

**Step 2: Magnitude.**

$$
|\vec{v}| = \sqrt{50^2 + 200^2} = \boxed{206.2 \text{ m/s}}
$$

**Step 3: Direction.**

$$
\theta = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{50}{200}\right) = \boxed{14.0^\circ \ \text{east of north}}
$$

The plane points straight north through the air but drifts $14^\circ$ off course over the ground. To hold a true northward track the pilot must crab the nose west into the wind, which is exactly the next problem.

### Worked Example 3.3: River Crossing (Drift and Correction)

**Problem:** A boat can move at $4 \text{ m/s}$ relative to the water. A river $200 \text{ m}$ wide flows east at $3 \text{ m/s}$. The pilot first aims the bow straight across (due north). (a) Find the ground velocity, crossing time, and downstream drift. (b) Find the heading needed to land directly opposite the start.

**Part (a): Bow aimed straight across.** Let east be $\hat{i}$ and north be $\hat{j}$.

$$
\vec{v}_{boat/ground} = \vec{v}_{boat/water} + \vec{v}_{water/ground} = (3)\hat{i} + (4)\hat{j} \ \text{m/s}
$$

$$
|\vec{v}| = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \boxed{5.0 \text{ m/s}}
$$

The northward component is unaffected by the east-flowing current, so the crossing time depends only on the $4 \text{ m/s}$ across-stream speed:

$$
t = \frac{200 \text{ m}}{4 \text{ m/s}} = \boxed{50 \text{ s}}
$$

During those 50 s the current carries the boat downstream:

$$
d = (3 \text{ m/s})(50 \text{ s}) = \boxed{150 \text{ m east}}
$$

**Part (b): Land directly opposite.** Now aim the bow at angle $\phi$ west of north so the east component of the boat cancels the current. Setting the net east velocity to zero:

$$
-\,(4)\sin\phi + 3 = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \sin\phi = \frac{3}{4} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \phi = \boxed{48.6^\circ \ \text{west of north}}
$$

The remaining north component gives the crossing speed and time:

$$
v_{north} = 4\cos(48.6^\circ) = 2.65 \text{ m/s}, \qquad t = \frac{200}{2.65} = \boxed{75.6 \text{ s}}
$$

Correcting for drift costs about $26 \text{ s}$ of extra crossing time, because part of the boat's capability is now spent fighting the current instead of advancing across. This trade between landing accuracy and crossing speed is the same one a ferry operator, a swimmer, and a station-keeping ROV all face.

## Relative Acceleration

Differentiating the velocity relation with respect to time gives the matching acceleration rule for translating frames:

$$
\vec{a}_{B/A} = \vec{a}_B - \vec{a}_A
$$

A useful consequence: if two vehicles have the **same** acceleration (for example, both in identical free fall, or both braking at the same rate), then $\vec{a}_{B/A} = 0$ and B appears to move at constant velocity relative to A even though both are accelerating in the ground frame. This is why a dropped object inside a uniformly accelerating elevator still falls in a straight line relative to the cabin, just with an altered apparent gravity.

### Worked Example 3.4: Closing Rate at an Intersection

**Problem:** Car A heads east at $30 \text{ m/s}$; Car B heads north at $40 \text{ m/s}$, approaching the same intersection. Find the velocity of B relative to A.

$$
\vec{v}_{B/A} = \vec{v}_B - \vec{v}_A = (0 - 30)\hat{i} + (40 - 0)\hat{j} = (-30)\hat{i} + (40)\hat{j} \ \text{m/s}
$$

$$
|\vec{v}_{B/A}| = \sqrt{(-30)^2 + 40^2} = \boxed{50 \text{ m/s}}
$$

$$
\theta = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{40}{-30}\right) = \boxed{126.9^\circ \ \text{from east (i.e. north-west)}}
$$

To the driver of A, car B does not merely approach from the left; it sweeps in along a line $126.9^\circ$ from due east at $50 \text{ m/s}$. Collision-avoidance and radar systems compute exactly this relative vector, because a constant relative bearing with nonzero closing speed is the classic signature of an impending collision.

## Practical Engineering Uses

Relative motion shows up whenever two moving systems interact.

| Application | Frames Involved | Why It Matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Autonomous driving | Vehicle vs. surrounding traffic | Closing rate sets braking and avoidance decisions |
| Mobile robotics | Tool tip vs. moving conveyor | Pick-and-place must track the part, not the floor |
| Marine and ferry routing | Hull vs. water vs. shore | Current correction sets heading and crossing time |
| Aircraft navigation | Airframe vs. air vs. ground | Wind correction angle keeps the ground track true |
| Spacecraft docking | Chaser vs. target | Only the relative state vector matters for capture |

A lot of "surprise" failures in motion analysis come from forgetting that the chosen frame quietly changes the answer.

## Common Errors

- **Subtracting speeds instead of velocities:** direction must be included; $|\vec{v}_B| - |\vec{v}_A|$ is almost never $|\vec{v}_{B/A}|$.
- **Switching frames mid-problem:** for example, mixing air-relative and ground-relative values in the same equation.
- **Ignoring signs in 1D:** approaching and separating cases can flip quickly if directions are not defined first.
- **Forgetting the subscript-cancellation rule:** the inner frame must match ($\vec{v}_{B/A}+\vec{v}_{A/G}$), or the chained result is meaningless.

## Summary

### Key Equations

| Concept | Equation |
| --- | --- |
| Relative velocity | $\vec{v}_{B/A} = \vec{v}_B - \vec{v}_A$ |
| Velocity addition (frame chaining) | $\vec{v}_{B/G} = \vec{v}_{B/A} + \vec{v}_{A/G}$ |
| Planar components | $\vec{v}_{B/A} = (v_{Bx}-v_{Ax})\hat{i} + (v_{By}-v_{Ay})\hat{j}$ |
| Relative acceleration | $\vec{a}_{B/A} = \vec{a}_B - \vec{a}_A$ |

### Key Takeaways

- Velocity descriptions depend on the observer's frame; always name the frame first.
- Relative motion is vector subtraction, with direction preserved even in 1D as a sign.
- Frame subscripts cancel like fractions, which keeps multi-frame chains consistent.
- For translating frames the same rule applies to acceleration; equal accelerations cancel.
- Closing rate, not absolute speed, governs collisions, docking, and avoidance.
