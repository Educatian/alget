# Curvilinear Motion: n-t and Polar Coordinates

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this section, you will be able to:

1. **Decide** when normal-tangential ($n$-$t$) coordinates or polar coordinates simplify a curvilinear-motion problem
2. **Decompose** velocity and acceleration in $n$-$t$ coordinates with $a_t = dv/dt$ and $a_n = v^2/\rho$
3. **Compute** polar-coordinate acceleration including the cross terms $a_r = \ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2$ and $a_\theta = r\ddot{\theta} + 2\dot{r}\dot{\theta}$
4. **Identify** the centripetal direction and apply it correctly in force-balance problems

---

## Opening: Why Race Cars Skid on the Exit, Not the Apex

A car can be traveling at perfectly constant speed and still slide off a curve. Drivers feel this every time a highway ramp tightens: the car has zero tangential acceleration (the speedometer is steady) yet a very real sideways pull. That pull is the normal acceleration $a_n = v^2/\rho$, and it grows as the radius $\rho$ shrinks. When the tires cannot supply the friction force $m a_n$ demands, the car skids, regardless of whether the driver is speeding up.

The lesson is that on a curved path, "not accelerating" in the everyday sense (constant speed) is not the same as zero acceleration. Direction is changing, and changing direction requires acceleration toward the center of the turn. Choosing the right coordinate system makes this physics obvious instead of buried in messy $xy$ derivatives.

> **The Big Picture:** Pick coordinates that match the geometry and a curvilinear problem collapses to two lines. Pick poorly and even a simple curve becomes painful algebra.

---

## Why Coordinates Choose Themselves

Most undergraduate dynamics begins with rectilinear motion in Cartesian coordinates. Real motion in machines, vehicles, and orbital mechanics is rarely rectilinear and rarely well-described in xy. The choice of coordinate system is part of the analysis: pick well and the equations simplify; pick poorly and even simple problems become algebraically painful.

Two coordinate systems handle most curvilinear motion problems an undergraduate will see: **normal-tangential (n-t) coordinates** for motion along a known path, and **polar (cylindrical) coordinates** for motion measured from a fixed origin. Hibbeler covers both in chapter 12; Beer and Johnston cover them in chapter 11; Meriam in chapter 2.

This module covers when to choose each, the velocity and acceleration decompositions in each, and the workflow that prevents the most common errors.

---

## When n-t Coordinates Are the Right Choice

Use n-t coordinates when **the path is known and the position of the particle along the path is what you are asked about**. Examples: a bead on a wire, a car on a curved track, a roller coaster, a satellite in a known trajectory whose only unknown is its current arc length.

In n-t coordinates, you fix a unit vector $\hat{u}_t$ tangent to the path (in the direction of motion) and a unit vector $\hat{u}_n$ normal to the path, pointing toward the *center of curvature* (always toward the inside of the turn).

The velocity has only a tangential component:

$$\vec{v} = v\,\hat{u}_t$$

where $v = ds/dt$ is the speed (a scalar).

The acceleration decomposes into two physical components:

$$\vec{a} = a_t\,\hat{u}_t + a_n\,\hat{u}_n$$

with **tangential acceleration** $a_t = dv/dt$ (changes the speed) and **normal acceleration** $a_n = v^2/\rho$ (changes the direction), where $\rho$ is the local radius of curvature of the path.

Two physical insights live in this decomposition. First, tangential acceleration is exactly the rate of change of speed; if speed is constant, $a_t = 0$ and only direction-changing acceleration remains. Second, normal acceleration is always nonzero on a curved path and always points toward the center of curvature (this is the centripetal direction). The faster you go around a tighter curve, the larger $a_n$ must be.

## When Polar Coordinates Are the Right Choice

Use polar coordinates when **the motion is most naturally described by distance and angle from a fixed origin**. Examples: a satellite orbiting Earth, a rotating arm pinned at one end, a projectile tracked from a radar station, a planet in heliocentric motion. The unit vectors are $\hat{u}_r$ (radially outward from the origin) and $\hat{u}_\theta$ (perpendicular, in the direction of increasing $\theta$).

In polar coordinates the position vector is simply

$$\vec{r} = r\,\hat{u}_r$$

The velocity has two components:

$$\vec{v} = \dot{r}\,\hat{u}_r + r\dot{\theta}\,\hat{u}_\theta$$

where $\dot{r}$ is the radial speed and $r\dot{\theta}$ is the transverse speed. Note the $r$ factor; the transverse component is not $\dot{\theta}$ alone; it scales with how far out the particle is.

The acceleration decomposes into four terms, and this is where most students make errors:

$$\vec{a} = (\ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2)\,\hat{u}_r + (r\ddot{\theta} + 2\dot{r}\dot{\theta})\,\hat{u}_\theta$$

The radial acceleration $\ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2$ contains a centripetal-like term $-r\dot{\theta}^2$ that is present even when $\ddot{r} = 0$. The transverse acceleration $r\ddot{\theta} + 2\dot{r}\dot{\theta}$ contains a Coriolis-like term $2\dot{r}\dot{\theta}$ that is present even when $\ddot{\theta} = 0$.

These cross terms exist because $\hat{u}_r$ and $\hat{u}_\theta$ themselves rotate as the particle moves. Differentiating the rotating unit vectors generates the extra terms. This is the central conceptual point: in non-inertial-feeling coordinate systems, the time derivatives of basis vectors do not vanish.

### Deriving the Polar Acceleration (Where the Cross Terms Come From)

The four-term acceleration is not a formula to memorize blindly; it falls straight out of the product rule once you know how the basis vectors turn. The key facts are the time derivatives of the rotating unit vectors. Because $\hat{u}_r$ rotates with angular rate $\dot{\theta}$, its tip sweeps in the $\hat{u}_\theta$ direction, and $\hat{u}_\theta$ in turn sweeps back toward $-\hat{u}_r$:

$$\dot{\hat{u}}_r = \dot{\theta}\,\hat{u}_\theta, \qquad \dot{\hat{u}}_\theta = -\dot{\theta}\,\hat{u}_r$$

Start from position $\vec{r} = r\,\hat{u}_r$ and differentiate once for velocity:

$$\vec{v} = \dot{r}\,\hat{u}_r + r\,\dot{\hat{u}}_r = \dot{r}\,\hat{u}_r + r\dot{\theta}\,\hat{u}_\theta$$

Differentiate again, applying the product rule to every factor and substituting $\dot{\hat{u}}_r$ and $\dot{\hat{u}}_\theta$:

$$\vec{a} = \ddot{r}\,\hat{u}_r + \dot{r}\,\dot{\hat{u}}_r + (\dot{r}\dot{\theta} + r\ddot{\theta})\,\hat{u}_\theta + r\dot{\theta}\,\dot{\hat{u}}_\theta$$

$$\vec{a} = \ddot{r}\,\hat{u}_r + \dot{r}\dot{\theta}\,\hat{u}_\theta + (\dot{r}\dot{\theta} + r\ddot{\theta})\,\hat{u}_\theta - r\dot{\theta}^2\,\hat{u}_r$$

Collecting the $\hat{u}_r$ and $\hat{u}_\theta$ terms gives the result quoted above:

$$\vec{a} = (\ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2)\,\hat{u}_r + (r\ddot{\theta} + 2\dot{r}\dot{\theta})\,\hat{u}_\theta$$

The two "extra" terms now have a clear pedigree. The $-r\dot{\theta}^2$ centripetal term comes from $\dot{\hat{u}}_\theta = -\dot{\theta}\,\hat{u}_r$ acting on the transverse velocity. The $2\dot{r}\dot{\theta}$ Coriolis term is the sum of two equal contributions: one from $\dot{r}$ changing the lever arm of the transverse velocity, and one from $\dot{\hat{u}}_r$ turning the radial velocity sideways. They are equal, so they add to a factor of 2. If you ever cannot remember the signs, redo these two lines; the algebra is short and self-correcting.

## Cylindrical Coordinates: Polar + Vertical

For 3D motion that has cylindrical symmetry (a helix, a particle on a screw, motion of a particle in a vertical pipe with rotation), use **cylindrical coordinates**: polar $(r, \theta)$ in the horizontal plane plus z vertically.

$$\vec{r} = r\,\hat{u}_r + z\,\hat{u}_z, \quad \vec{v} = \dot{r}\,\hat{u}_r + r\dot{\theta}\,\hat{u}_\theta + \dot{z}\,\hat{u}_z$$

$$\vec{a} = (\ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2)\,\hat{u}_r + (r\ddot{\theta} + 2\dot{r}\dot{\theta})\,\hat{u}_\theta + \ddot{z}\,\hat{u}_z$$

The vertical component is a clean Cartesian-style derivative because $\hat{u}_z$ does not rotate. The horizontal components inherit the polar cross terms.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Constant Speed Around a Curve

**Problem:** A car travels at constant speed $v = 30$ m/s around a horizontal curve of radius $\rho = 200$ m. Find its acceleration.

**$n$-$t$ analysis:**

- Tangential acceleration: $a_t = dv/dt = 0$ (constant speed).
- Normal acceleration: $a_n = v^2/\rho = (30)^2/200 = \boxed{4.5 \text{ m/s}^2}$, directed toward the center of curvature.

The centripetal force required to produce this is $F_n = m a_n$, supplied by friction (or by friction plus the banking-normal-force component on a banked road). The dynamics step that follows ($\sum F = m\vec{a}$ in the $n$ direction) gives the friction or banking-angle requirement.

This problem in $xy$-coordinates would require parameterizing position by time and differentiating. In $n$-$t$ the answer is one line.

---

### Example 2: Speeding Up While Turning (Combined $a_t$ and $a_n$)

**Problem:** A car rounds a curve of radius $\rho = 300$ m. At the instant of interest its speed is $v = 25$ m/s and it is accelerating along the path at $a_t = 3$ m/s². Find the magnitude of the total acceleration.

**Step 1: Normal component.**

$$a_n = \frac{v^2}{\rho} = \frac{(25)^2}{300} = 2.083 \text{ m/s}^2 \text{ (toward the center of curvature)}$$

**Step 2: Tangential component** is given: $a_t = 3$ m/s² (along the path).

**Step 3: The two components are perpendicular**, so the magnitude is the hypotenuse:

$$|\vec{a}| = \sqrt{a_t^2 + a_n^2} = \sqrt{(3)^2 + (2.083)^2} = \sqrt{9 + 4.34} = \boxed{3.65 \text{ m/s}^2}$$

The total acceleration tilts forward-and-inward: forward because the car is speeding up, inward because the path is curving. Neither component alone tells the full story, which is exactly why the $n$-$t$ split is so useful.

---

### Example 3: Polar Coordinates with Rotating Basis Vectors

**Problem:** A radar at the origin tracks a drone. At one instant the drone is at radius $r = 10$ m with $\dot{r} = 2$ m/s and $\ddot{r} = 0$; the bearing angle has $\dot{\theta} = 0.5$ rad/s and $\ddot{\theta} = 0.2$ rad/s². Find the velocity and acceleration in polar coordinates.

**Step 1: Velocity components.**

$$v_r = \dot{r} = 2 \text{ m/s}, \qquad v_\theta = r\dot{\theta} = (10)(0.5) = 5 \text{ m/s}$$

$$|\vec{v}| = \sqrt{2^2 + 5^2} = \boxed{5.39 \text{ m/s}}$$

**Step 2: Radial acceleration** (note the $-r\dot{\theta}^2$ term survives even though $\ddot{r} = 0$):

$$a_r = \ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2 = 0 - (10)(0.5)^2 = -2.5 \text{ m/s}^2$$

**Step 3: Transverse acceleration** (the $2\dot{r}\dot{\theta}$ Coriolis-like term is present):

$$a_\theta = r\ddot{\theta} + 2\dot{r}\dot{\theta} = (10)(0.2) + 2(2)(0.5) = 2 + 2 = 4 \text{ m/s}^2$$

**Step 4: Magnitude.**

$$|\vec{a}| = \sqrt{(-2.5)^2 + (4)^2} = \sqrt{6.25 + 16} = \boxed{4.72 \text{ m/s}^2}$$

If you had forgotten the cross terms, you would have reported $a_r = 0$ and $a_\theta = 2$ m/s², missing more than half the true acceleration. The cross terms exist because $\hat{u}_r$ and $\hat{u}_\theta$ rotate as the drone moves; they are never optional.

---

### Example 4: Radius of Curvature from a Path Equation

When the path is given as a curve $y = f(x)$ rather than a circle, the local radius of curvature feeding $a_n = v^2/\rho$ comes from calculus:

$$\rho = \frac{\left[1 + (dy/dx)^2\right]^{3/2}}{\left| d^2y/dx^2 \right|}$$

**Problem:** A test vehicle follows the parabolic track $y = 0.01x^2$ (meters) at a steady speed $v = 20$ m/s. Find the normal acceleration as it passes the point $x = 50$ m.

**Step 1: Slopes from the path.**

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = 0.02x = 0.02(50) = 1.0, \qquad \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = 0.02$$

**Step 2: Radius of curvature.**

$$\rho = \frac{\left[1 + (1.0)^2\right]^{3/2}}{0.02} = \frac{(2)^{3/2}}{0.02} = \frac{2.828}{0.02} = 141.4 \text{ m}$$

**Step 3: Normal acceleration** (speed is constant, so $a_t = 0$):

$$a_n = \frac{v^2}{\rho} = \frac{(20)^2}{141.4} = \boxed{2.83 \text{ m/s}^2}$$

The same vehicle on the flatter part of the track near $x = 0$, where $\rho = 1/0.02 = 50$ m, would feel $a_n = 400/50 = 8.0$ m/s², nearly three times larger. The track straightens as $x$ grows, $\rho$ rises, and the cornering demand falls. This is precisely how highway and rail alignments are laid out: designers shape $\rho$ along the route so that $v^2/\rho$ never exceeds the friction or comfort limit.

---

### Example 5: Sizing a Banked Curve (Kinematics Drives the Design)

**Problem:** A highway exit ramp of radius $\rho = 120$ m is to be banked so that a car traveling at the design speed $v = 25$ m/s needs zero friction to hold the curve. Find the required bank angle $\theta$.

**Step 1: Kinematics sets the target.** The normal acceleration the car must be given is

$$a_n = \frac{v^2}{\rho} = \frac{(25)^2}{120} = 5.208 \text{ m/s}^2 \text{ (horizontal, toward the center of the curve)}$$

**Step 2: Force balance on the frictionless banked surface.** The only forces are weight $mg$ down and the road normal force $N$ perpendicular to the banked surface. The normal force tilts inward by the bank angle, so its horizontal component supplies $m a_n$ and its vertical component supports the weight:

$$N\sin\theta = m a_n, \qquad N\cos\theta = mg$$

**Step 3: Divide to eliminate $N$ and $m$.**

$$\tan\theta = \frac{a_n}{g} = \frac{5.208}{9.81} = 0.5309$$

$$\theta = \arctan(0.5309) = \boxed{27.96^\circ}$$

A bank near $28^\circ$ lets the design-speed car round the ramp with no reliance on tire friction, leaving the full friction budget as a safety margin for rain, speed variation, or a tighter line. Notice the workflow: the kinematic quantity $a_n = v^2/\rho$ from this section is the input, and Newton's second law in the $n$ direction (next chapter) turns it into a buildable angle.

## Common Errors and Their Diagnoses

- **Forgetting the rotating-unit-vector cross terms** in polar acceleration. The full $a_r = \ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2$ and $a_\theta = r\ddot{\theta} + 2\dot{r}\dot{\theta}$ are not optional.
- **Using n-t for non-path-defined motion.** If the path is not known, you cannot identify the tangent direction, and n-t is the wrong tool.
- **Treating $r\dot{\theta}$ as $\dot{\theta}$ alone** in transverse velocity. The factor of $r$ scales the angular rate by the lever arm.
- **Confusing $\hat{u}_n$ direction.** It always points toward the center of curvature, never away. On a circular path, $\hat{u}_n$ points radially inward.
- **Mixing coordinate systems mid-problem.** Pick one and convert at the end if needed.

## Engineering Applications

The choice between $n$-$t$ and polar coordinates is not academic; it tracks how the hardware is actually instrumented and what is held fixed.

| Application | Natural Coordinates | What the Math Sizes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Highway and rail alignment | $n$-$t$ (path known) | Radius of curvature $\rho$ so $v^2/\rho$ stays under friction/comfort limits |
| Banked race or exit ramps | $n$-$t$ then $\sum F = m a_n$ | Bank angle from $\tan\theta = v^2/(g\rho)$ |
| Roller coaster loops | $n$-$t$ (path known) | Minimum top speed so $a_n = v^2/\rho \geq g$ keeps riders on the track |
| Satellite and orbital tracking | Polar / cylindrical | Radial and transverse acceleration from a fixed-Earth origin |
| Radar and missile guidance | Polar | Line-of-sight rate $\dot{\theta}$ and closing rate $\dot{r}$ from a ground station |
| Robotic arm / turntable | Polar (pinned origin) | Coriolis term $2\dot{r}\dot{\theta}$ on a part sliding along a rotating link |

> **Engineering in Action:** A part sliding outward along a spinning robot arm experiences a real transverse force from the $2\dot{r}\dot{\theta}$ Coriolis term. Ignore it and the controller mis-commands the joint torque; the part lags or overshoots its target. The same term governs why winds curve on a rotating Earth.

## Summary

### Key Equations

| Concept | Equation |
| --- | --- |
| $n$-$t$ velocity | $\vec{v} = v\,\hat{u}_t$ |
| $n$-$t$ acceleration | $\vec{a} = \dot{v}\,\hat{u}_t + \dfrac{v^2}{\rho}\,\hat{u}_n$ |
| Polar velocity | $\vec{v} = \dot{r}\,\hat{u}_r + r\dot{\theta}\,\hat{u}_\theta$ |
| Polar acceleration | $\vec{a} = (\ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2)\,\hat{u}_r + (r\ddot{\theta} + 2\dot{r}\dot{\theta})\,\hat{u}_\theta$ |
| Radius of curvature ($y=f(x)$) | $\rho = \dfrac{[1 + (y')^2]^{3/2}}{\lvert y'' \rvert}$ |
| Rotating basis derivatives | $\dot{\hat{u}}_r = \dot{\theta}\,\hat{u}_\theta,\;\; \dot{\hat{u}}_\theta = -\dot{\theta}\,\hat{u}_r$ |

### Key Takeaways

- Use $n$-$t$ coordinates when the path is known; use polar when motion is measured from a fixed origin.
- Tangential acceleration changes speed; normal acceleration $v^2/\rho$ changes direction and always points to the center of curvature.
- Constant speed does not mean zero acceleration on a curve.
- The polar cross terms $-r\dot{\theta}^2$ and $2\dot{r}\dot{\theta}$ come from the rotating basis vectors and are never optional.
- The transverse velocity is $r\dot{\theta}$, not $\dot{\theta}$ alone; the lever arm $r$ matters.
