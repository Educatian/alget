## From Particle Dynamics to Rigid-Body Dynamics

For a particle, all the mass is treated as concentrated at one point. For a rigid body, mass is distributed across space, so forces can do two things:

- translate the center of mass
- rotate the body about its center of mass or another point

That is why rigid-body dynamics needs both force equations and a moment equation.

## Governing Equations in Plane Motion

For a rigid body in plane motion:

$$
\boxed{\sum F_x = m(a_G)_x}
$$

$$
\boxed{\sum F_y = m(a_G)_y}
$$

$$
\boxed{\sum M_G = I_G \alpha}
$$

where:

- $G$ is the center of mass
- $I_G$ is the mass moment of inertia about $G$
- $\alpha$ is angular acceleration

These equations are powerful because they separate translation and rotation cleanly.

## What Mass Moment of Inertia Really Means

Mass moment of inertia tells you how difficult it is to angularly accelerate a body about an axis.

Two bodies can have the same mass but different rotational response if their mass is distributed differently.

| Shape | $I_G$ |
| --- | --- |
| Slender rod about center | $\frac{1}{12}mL^2$ |
| Thin disk or cylinder | $\frac{1}{2}mr^2$ |
| Solid sphere | $\frac{2}{5}mr^2$ |

Bodies with more mass farther from the axis have larger inertia and therefore smaller angular acceleration for the same applied moment.

## The Parallel-Axis Theorem

If the rotation axis is not through the center of mass,

$$
\boxed{I = I_G + md^2}
$$

This is one of the most common sources of mistakes in rigid-body dynamics. Students often remember a tabulated $I_G$ value and forget to shift it to the actual rotation axis.

## A Reliable Solution Strategy

### 1. Draw the rigid-body free body diagram

Include all applied forces, reactions, and known distances.

### 2. Mark the center of mass

If the center of mass location is not explicit, infer it from the geometry.

### 3. Write force equations for translation

These determine the acceleration of the center of mass.

### 4. Write a moment equation

Use a point that makes the rotational balance easiest to evaluate. If you use the center of mass, the standard equation is $\sum M_G = I_G \alpha$.

### 5. Connect translation and rotation when needed

Rolling constraints or kinematic relations may couple $a_G$ and $\alpha$.

## Worked Example

### Example 3.2: Disk with a Tangential Force

**Problem:** A 10 kg disk of radius 0.3 m has a tangential force of 20 N applied at its rim. Find the angular acceleration.

### Step A: Compute the inertia

For a disk about its center:

$$
I_G = \frac{1}{2}mr^2 = \frac{1}{2}(10)(0.3)^2 = 0.45 \text{ kg*m}^2
$$

### Step B: Compute the applied moment

Because the force is tangential,

$$
\sum M_G = Fr = 20(0.3) = 6 \text{ N*m}
$$

### Step C: Apply the rotational equation

$$
\sum M_G = I_G \alpha
$$

$$
6 = 0.45\alpha
$$

$$
\alpha = 13.3 \text{ rad/s}^2
$$

The disk's angular acceleration is determined by both the applied moment and the body's rotational inertia.

## A More Subtle Interpretation

If the same 20 N force acted on a larger-radius but much heavier disk, the larger moment arm might help, but the increased inertia could dominate. That tradeoff is why motor sizing, flywheel design, and rotating-machine startup analysis all depend on the same structure:

- applied torque creates angular acceleration
- inertia resists angular acceleration

## Common Mistakes

- using force instead of moment in the rotational equation
- forgetting the moment arm
- using a tabulated inertia about the wrong axis
- mixing translational acceleration of the center of mass with angular acceleration of the body

## Summary

- Rigid-body dynamics combines translation of the center of mass with rotation about it.
- The force equations predict translational acceleration; the moment equation predicts angular acceleration.
- Mass moment of inertia is the rotational analog of mass in particle dynamics.
- The reference axis matters, which is why the parallel-axis theorem appears so often.
