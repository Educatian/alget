# Work-Energy for Rigid Bodies

> **"A rolling or spinning body stores energy in both translation and rotation, and you lose accuracy if you forget either one."**

## Why Rigid-Body Energy Needs More Care

In particle dynamics, kinetic energy is just $\frac{1}{2}mv^2$. In rigid-body motion, a body can translate and rotate at the same time. That means one energy term is no longer enough.

Examples:

- a wheel rolling downhill
- a flywheel storing energy in a drivetrain
- a yo-yo translating downward while spinning faster
- a robotic arm link sweeping while its center of mass moves

If you omit the rotational term, you generally predict a speed that is too high.

## Total Kinetic Energy of a Rigid Body

For general plane motion:

$$
\boxed{T = \frac{1}{2}mv_G^2 + \frac{1}{2}I_G\omega^2}
$$

The two pieces mean:

- $\frac{1}{2}mv_G^2$: translational kinetic energy of the center of mass
- $\frac{1}{2}I_G\omega^2$: rotational kinetic energy about the center of mass

This split is one of the central ideas of rigid-body dynamics.

## Rolling Without Slipping

For a rolling body,

$$
\boxed{v_G = r\omega}
$$

This is a kinematic constraint, not a second energy term. It lets you rewrite the total kinetic energy in one variable, either $v_G$ or $\omega$.

For example:

$$
T = \frac{1}{2}mv_G^2 + \frac{1}{2}I_G\omega^2
$$

with $\omega = \frac{v_G}{r}$ becomes

$$
T = \frac{1}{2}mv_G^2 + \frac{1}{2}I_G\left(\frac{v_G}{r}\right)^2
$$

That is why rolling bodies usually move more slowly than sliding particles descending the same height: some of the potential-energy drop becomes rotational kinetic energy.

## Work-Energy Balance

The same structure from particle dynamics still works:

$$
\boxed{T_1 + V_1 + W_{nc} = T_2 + V_2}
$$

The only difference is that $T$ now includes both translational and rotational pieces.

## Worked Example

### Example 3.3: Solid Disk Rolling Down a Ramp

**Problem:** A solid disk with mass 5 kg and radius 0.2 m rolls down from a vertical height of 2 m. Find its speed at the bottom.

### Step A: Write the initial and final energies

- Initial kinetic energy: $T_1 = 0$
- Initial potential energy: $V_1 = mgh$
- Final potential energy: $V_2 = 0$

If rolling without slipping and energy loss is neglected:

$$
mgh = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 + \frac{1}{2}I_G\omega^2
$$

### Step B: Insert the disk inertia

For a solid disk,

$$
I_G = \frac{1}{2}mr^2
$$

Using $v = r\omega$:

$$
mgh = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 + \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}mr^2\right)\left(\frac{v}{r}\right)^2
$$

$$
mgh = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 + \frac{1}{4}mv^2 = \frac{3}{4}mv^2
$$

### Step C: Solve for speed

$$
v = \sqrt{\frac{4gh}{3}}
$$

Substitute $g = 9.81$ m/s$^2$ and $h = 2$ m:

$$
v = \sqrt{\frac{4(9.81)(2)}{3}} = 5.11 \text{ m/s}
$$

The disk reaches the bottom more slowly than a frictionless sliding block would, because part of the gravitational energy becomes rotation.

## Compare Against a Sliding Particle

If the same object slid without rotation, the speed would come from

$$
mgh = \frac{1}{2}mv^2
$$

which gives

$$
v = \sqrt{2gh} = 6.26 \text{ m/s}
$$

That comparison is pedagogically useful. It makes the rotational energy cost visible.

## Quantitative Comparison Across Shapes

The same drop height does not produce the same final speed for every rolling body. Shape matters because inertia distribution matters.

For rolling without slipping from the same height $h$, the speed can be written in the form:

$$
v = \sqrt{\frac{2gh}{1 + I_G/(mr^2)}}
$$

Using $h = 2$ m:

| Rolling Body | $I_G/(mr^2)$ | Final Speed |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Hoop | 1.0 | $4.43$ m/s |
| Solid disk | 0.5 | $5.11$ m/s |
| Solid sphere | 0.4 | $5.29$ m/s |

This is an important engineering comparison. A body with more inertia concentrated away from the axis stores more of the gravitational energy in rotation and therefore reaches a lower translational speed.

## Comparative Test Mindset

If an engineer were selecting a wheel for a delivery robot, a simple comparison test might ask:

- Which geometry gives the most stable downhill speed?
- Which reaches the target speed with the least motor demand on level ground?
- Which shape is more forgiving when the surface is slightly rough or when rolling losses increase?

That is where rigid-body energy becomes more than a classroom formula. It supports direct comparison among competing geometries before a prototype is even built.

## Sensitivity Analysis: Effect of Energy Losses

The ideal rolling examples above ignore non-conservative losses. Real wheels do not.

For the same 5 kg solid disk descending from a 2 m height:

$$
mgh - E_{loss} = \frac{3}{4}mv^2
$$

If bearing drag, tire deformation, or surface losses consume part of the available energy, the predicted speed changes as follows:

| Energy Loss | Final Speed |
| ---: | ---: |
| 0 J | 5.11 m/s |
| 5 J | 4.98 m/s |
| 10 J | 4.84 m/s |
| 20 J | 4.54 m/s |

This sensitivity view is often more useful than a single ideal answer. It shows how tolerant the design is to wheel quality, maintenance condition, or manufacturing variability.

## Assumption Breakdown Case: Rolling Without Slipping

One of the most important hidden assumptions in this section is that rolling without slipping actually holds.

That assumption can fail when:

- the available static friction is too low,
- a torque spike exceeds the traction limit,
- the surface becomes wet, dusty, or compliant,
- or braking loads exceed the contact's ability to transmit shear.

Once slipping begins, the clean relation $v = r\omega$ no longer acts as a reliable kinematic constraint. Then:

- static friction may be replaced by kinetic friction,
- part of the energy is dissipated as heat,
- and the simple rolling-energy solution can overpredict retained useful motion.

This is a genuine design boundary. A wheel concept that looks excellent under ideal rolling assumptions may behave very differently once traction margins shrink.

## Common Mistakes

- forgetting the rotational kinetic-energy term
- using the wrong inertia formula for the shape
- treating $v = r\omega$ as optional in rolling problems
- double counting the same motion by adding extra terms after already substituting the rolling constraint

## Summary

- Rigid bodies store kinetic energy in translation and rotation.
- Rolling constraints connect linear and angular motion.
- Energy balances often solve rigid-body speed problems faster than force-moment equations.
- The body's inertia model strongly influences the final answer.
