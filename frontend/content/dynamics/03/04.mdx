# Rigid Body Impulse and Angular Momentum

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this section, you will be able to:

1. **Apply** linear impulse-momentum to the center of mass of a rigid body
2. **Apply** angular impulse-momentum about the center of mass and about a fixed point, choosing the correct moment of inertia
3. **Identify** when linear or angular momentum is conserved during an impact, recognizing impulsive constraint reactions
4. **Solve** eccentric-impact problems using contact-point velocities and the coefficient of restitution along the impact line

---

## Opening: How a Diver Adds a Somersault in Mid-Air

A platform diver leaves the board with a fixed amount of angular momentum, set the instant their feet lose contact. Nothing in the air can change that total. Yet divers routinely speed up to whip through somersaults and then slow down to enter the water clean. They do it by changing their moment of inertia: tucking pulls mass toward the spin axis, which shrinks $I_G$, and since $I_G\,\omega$ is conserved, $\omega$ shoots up. Opening into a layout reverses it.

That is angular momentum conservation made visible, and it is the heart of this section. When external forces act only briefly or have no moment about a chosen point, the time-integrated form of the laws of motion, impulse and momentum, becomes the cleanest tool available. It is the only convenient framework for collisions, impacts, and the short, violent forces that ordinary $F = ma$ cannot capture.

> **The Big Picture:** Impulse-momentum is the third leg of the kinetics triangle. It turns "an unbounded force acting for an instant" into a finite, solvable change in momentum.

---

## Why Rigid Body Impulse-Momentum Completes the Kinetics Trio

For particles, dynamics offers three equivalent kinetics frameworks: Newton's second law (force-acceleration), work-energy (energy bookkeeping), and impulse-momentum (time-integrated force). Each one shines for a different class of problem. The rigid-body extension of impulse-momentum is the missing leg of the triangle for rotating and translating bodies — and is the only convenient tool for problems involving **collisions, impacts, and short-duration forces** in rigid-body dynamics.

Hibbeler's chapter 19, Beer/Johnston's chapter 17, and Meriam's chapter 6/7 cover this material. This module assembles the linear and angular impulse-momentum principles for rigid bodies, the principles of conservation, and a worked workflow for impact problems.

## Linear Impulse-Momentum: From Particle to Rigid Body

For a particle, $\int_{t_1}^{t_2} \vec{F}\,dt = m\vec{v}_2 - m\vec{v}_1$. For a rigid body, the same equation holds **for the center of mass**:

$$\int_{t_1}^{t_2} \sum \vec{F}\,dt = m\vec{v}_{G,2} - m\vec{v}_{G,1}$$

where $\vec{v}_G$ is the velocity of the center of mass G. The linear impulse from external forces equals the change in linear momentum of the center of mass. Internal forces between rigid-body particles cancel by Newton's third law.

This is the same principle as for a particle, applied to the center-of-mass motion. The rigid-body extension is the next equation.

---

## Angular Impulse-Momentum About the Center of Mass

A rigid body's angular momentum about its center of mass G is

$$\vec{H}_G = I_G \vec{\omega}$$

where $I_G$ is the centroidal mass moment of inertia and $\vec{\omega}$ is the angular velocity. The principle:

$$\int_{t_1}^{t_2} \sum \vec{M}_G\,dt = I_G \vec{\omega}_2 - I_G \vec{\omega}_1$$

The angular impulse from external moments **about the center of mass** equals the change in angular momentum about the center of mass. Internal moments cancel.

For planar motion this reduces to scalar form: $\sum \int M_G\,dt = I_G(\omega_2 - \omega_1)$.

---

## Angular Impulse-Momentum About a Fixed Point

When a rigid body rotates about a fixed point O (e.g., a pinned wheel, a pendulum), the principle has a cleaner form using the moment of inertia about O:

$$\int_{t_1}^{t_2} \sum \vec{M}_O\,dt = I_O \vec{\omega}_2 - I_O \vec{\omega}_1$$

where $I_O = I_G + md^2$ by the parallel-axis theorem and d is the distance from G to O. This works specifically when O is fixed; for moving reference points, use the centroidal form above.

---

## Conservation Laws

When the system has no external linear impulse, **linear momentum is conserved**:

$$m\vec{v}_{G,1} = m\vec{v}_{G,2}$$

When the system has no external angular impulse about a chosen point, **angular momentum is conserved about that point**:

$$\vec{H}_{O,1} = \vec{H}_{O,2}$$

These two principles handle the entire family of impact and short-duration-force problems where impulsive forces dominate and ordinary forces (gravity, friction over a long arm) contribute negligibly during the impact interval.

The classic example: a spinning ice skater pulls in their arms. No external angular impulse acts; angular momentum about the vertical axis is conserved; pulling arms in reduces $I_G$, so $\omega$ must increase to keep $I_G \omega$ constant. The same logic explains the rotational dynamics of pirouettes, satellites changing configuration, and gymnasts on the high bar.

## Eccentric Impact

When two rigid bodies collide along a line that does not pass through both centers of mass, the impact is **eccentric**. The impact line is the line of action of the impulsive contact force. Two principles structure the analysis:

**Conservation of momentum along the impact line.** No external impulse along the line during impact (gravity acts perpendicular over the brief duration); linear momentum components along the impact line for the system are conserved.

**Coefficient of restitution along the impact line.**

$$e = \frac{(v'_{B,n} - v'_{A,n})}{(v_{A,n} - v_{B,n})}$$

where the primes denote post-impact, the n subscript denotes the impact-line component, and e is the coefficient of restitution (e = 1 perfectly elastic, e = 0 perfectly plastic). For real materials, e is typically 0.4-0.9.

**Velocities perpendicular to the impact line** are unchanged by an idealized impact (no friction during the impulsive interaction, by assumption).

For each body, the velocity of the **contact point** (not the center of mass) enters the restitution equation, because that is where the impact force acts. For a rotating body, the contact-point velocity equals the center-of-mass velocity plus the rotation contribution $\vec{\omega} \times \vec{r}_{G\to{\rm contact}}$.

This last point is the most common error in eccentric impact problems: students use the center-of-mass velocity in the restitution equation instead of the contact-point velocity.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Pinned Rod Struck by a Ball (Symbolic)

A uniform rod of mass $M$ and length $L$ is pinned at its top end. A ball of mass $m$ moving horizontally with speed $v$ strikes the rod at distance $d$ below the pin. What is the rod's angular velocity immediately after a perfectly plastic impact?

**Step 1: Identify the conserved quantity.** The pin exerts an impulsive reaction during impact. Linear momentum is **not** conserved (the pin reaction has a horizontal component). Angular momentum **about the pin** is conserved (the pin's reaction has zero moment about itself).

**Step 2: Initial angular momentum about the pin** (only the ball contributes; the rod is at rest):

$$H_{O,1} = m v d$$

**Step 3: After the plastic impact** the ball sticks at the strike point. The combined system rotates about the pin with $\omega'$. Moment of inertia about the pin:

$$I_O = I_{O,\text{rod}} + m d^2 = \frac{1}{3}ML^2 + md^2$$

**Step 4: Conservation** $H_{O,1} = H_{O,2} = I_O\,\omega'$ gives

$$\boxed{\omega' = \frac{m v d}{\frac{1}{3}ML^2 + md^2}}$$

This problem in $F = ma$ form would require integrating impulsive forces over an indefinite short interval. Impulse-momentum gives the answer in four lines.

---

### Example 2: Pinned Rod Struck by a Ball (Numbers)

**Problem:** A uniform rod of mass $M = 6$ kg and length $L = 1.2$ m is pinned at its top. A ball of mass $m = 0.5$ kg moving horizontally at $v = 20$ m/s strikes and sticks to the rod at $d = 0.9$ m below the pin. Find the angular velocity immediately after impact.

**Step 1: Moment of inertia about the pin.**

$$I_O = \frac{1}{3}ML^2 + md^2 = \frac{1}{3}(6)(1.2)^2 + (0.5)(0.9)^2 = 2.88 + 0.405 = 3.285 \text{ kg·m}^2$$

**Step 2: Conservation of angular momentum about the pin.**

$$m v d = I_O\,\omega' \;\Rightarrow\; \omega' = \frac{(0.5)(20)(0.9)}{3.285} = \frac{9.0}{3.285}$$

$$\boxed{\omega' = 2.74 \text{ rad/s}}$$

The combined rod-plus-ball system swings up about the pin at 2.74 rad/s. If you had wrongly used $I_G$ of the rod (about its own center) instead of $I_O$ about the pin, you would have badly overstated $\omega'$, because the rod's mass also has to swing about the offset pin.

---

### Example 3: Spinning Skater (Conservation of Angular Momentum)

**Problem:** A skater spins on near-frictionless ice with arms extended, moment of inertia $I_1 = 4.0$ kg·m² and angular velocity $\omega_1 = 2.0$ rad/s. The skater pulls their arms in, reducing the moment of inertia to $I_2 = 1.5$ kg·m². Find the new angular velocity.

**Step 1: No external angular impulse** about the vertical spin axis (ice is frictionless, gravity has no moment about the vertical axis), so angular momentum is conserved:

$$I_1\,\omega_1 = I_2\,\omega_2$$

**Step 2: Solve.**

$$\omega_2 = \frac{I_1\,\omega_1}{I_2} = \frac{(4.0)(2.0)}{1.5} = \boxed{5.33 \text{ rad/s}}$$

The angular velocity more than doubles, purely from redistributing mass. This is the diver's tuck of the opening, and the same principle that lets a collapsing star spin up into a pulsar.

> **Workflow Reminder:** Choose a point about which the troublesome (impulsive or constraint) forces have zero moment, write angular momentum before and after, and set them equal. Use $I_O$ about a fixed point, $I_G$ about the mass center.

## Common Errors and Their Diagnoses

- **Using F=ma for impulsive forces.** Impulsive forces produce finite impulses over zero (idealized) time intervals. F itself is unbounded; only the impulse $\int F\,dt$ is meaningful. Use impulse-momentum, not Newton's second law in instantaneous form.
- **Forgetting that the pin reaction is impulsive.** When a rigid body has a pinned constraint and is struck, the pin exerts an impulsive reaction. Linear momentum is not conserved; angular momentum about the pin is.
- **Using center-of-mass velocity in the restitution equation.** Restitution operates on the velocities of the contact points, not the centers of mass. For rotating bodies, contact-point velocity includes the $\omega \times r$ term.
- **Choosing the moment-of-inertia axis incorrectly.** When using angular impulse-momentum about a fixed point O, use $I_O$, not $I_G$. The parallel-axis theorem connects them.

## Summary

### Key Equations

| Concept | Equation |
| --- | --- |
| Linear impulse-momentum | $\displaystyle\int \sum \vec{F}\,dt = m\vec{v}_{G,2} - m\vec{v}_{G,1}$ |
| Angular (about G) | $\displaystyle\int \sum M_G\,dt = I_G(\omega_2 - \omega_1)$ |
| Angular (about fixed O) | $\displaystyle\int \sum M_O\,dt = I_O(\omega_2 - \omega_1)$ |
| Parallel-axis | $I_O = I_G + Md^2$ |
| Coefficient of restitution | $e = \dfrac{v'_{B,n} - v'_{A,n}}{v_{A,n} - v_{B,n}}$ |

### Key Takeaways

- Impulse-momentum is the right tool for impacts and short-duration forces, where $F = ma$ fails.
- Linear momentum of the center of mass changes by the external linear impulse; internal forces cancel.
- For a struck pinned body, linear momentum is not conserved (impulsive pin reaction) but angular momentum about the pin is.
- Use $I_O$ about a fixed point and $I_G$ about the mass center; the parallel-axis theorem connects them.
- In eccentric impact, the coefficient of restitution uses contact-point velocities, which include the $\omega \times r$ term for rotating bodies.
