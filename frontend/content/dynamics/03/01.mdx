## Why Rigid-Body Kinematics Feels Different

Particle kinematics asks how one point moves. Rigid-body kinematics asks how an entire shape moves while keeping the distance between all of its points fixed.

That difference matters in engineering:

- the hub and rim of a wheel do not have the same speed
- the tip of a robot arm moves faster than a point near the motor
- a door handle has a much larger linear speed than a point near the hinge

So even if a rigid body is "one object," different points on it can have different velocities and accelerations.

## Three Motion Types

| Type | Description | Key idea |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Translation | All points move with the same velocity and acceleration | Orientation does not change |
| Rotation about a fixed axis | All points move in circles about the axis | Angular quantities control the motion |
| General plane motion | Translation and rotation happen together | One point can be used as a reference for others |

### Translation

If a rigid body translates without rotating, all points share the same velocity and acceleration vectors.

### Rotation about a fixed axis

Every point traces a circle centered on the axis. Points farther from the axis move faster linearly for the same angular speed.

### General plane motion

This is the most common real case: a wheel rolling, a connecting rod swinging, or a tool arm sweeping through space while the base moves.

## Angular Position, Velocity, and Acceleration

$$
\omega = \frac{d\theta}{dt}, \qquad \alpha = \frac{d\omega}{dt}
$$

For constant angular acceleration:

$$
\omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t
$$

$$
\theta = \theta_0 + \omega_0 t + \frac{1}{2}\alpha t^2
$$

These look just like particle kinematics with $x, v, a$, but now the rotational variables are $\theta, \omega, \alpha$.

## Relating Angular and Linear Motion

For a point at distance $r$ from the axis:

$$
\boxed{v = r\omega}
$$

$$
\boxed{a_t = r\alpha}
$$

$$
\boxed{a_n = r\omega^2}
$$

Interpretation:

- $v$ is tangent to the circular path
- $a_t$ changes the speed
- $a_n$ points inward and changes the direction of velocity

Students often remember the formulas but forget the geometry. Tangential and normal directions are different and should not be combined carelessly.

## Relative Motion on a Rigid Body

If A and B are points on the same rigid body,

$$
\vec{v}_B = \vec{v}_A + \vec{\omega} \times \vec{r}_{B/A}
$$

This equation says:

- start with the known velocity of point A
- add the rotational contribution caused by the body's angular velocity
- the position vector from A to B determines the direction and magnitude of that extra term

For acceleration, a similar idea appears, but with both tangential and normal rotational effects.

## Worked Example

### Example 3.1: Point on a Rotating Disk

**Problem:** A disk rotates at $\omega = 12$ rad/s and has angular acceleration $\alpha = 4$ rad/s$^2$. Find the speed, tangential acceleration, and normal acceleration of a point 0.25 m from the center.

### Step A: Linear speed

$$
v = r\omega = 0.25(12) = 3.0 \text{ m/s}
$$

### Step B: Tangential acceleration

$$
a_t = r\alpha = 0.25(4) = 1.0 \text{ m/s}^2
$$

### Step C: Normal acceleration

$$
a_n = r\omega^2 = 0.25(12^2) = 36.0 \text{ m/s}^2
$$

The normal acceleration is much larger here because it scales with $\omega^2$ rather than $\alpha$.

## Common Misreads

- assuming all points on a rigid body have the same speed
- confusing angular velocity with tangential velocity
- forgetting that $a_n$ depends on $\omega^2$, not $\alpha$
- using $v = r\omega$ for a point that is not measured from the correct axis

## Summary

- Rigid-body motion can be translational, rotational, or combined.
- Angular variables describe how the body rotates; linear variables describe how specific points move.
- The relation $v = r\omega$ is geometric and depends on the correct radius.
- Relative-motion equations let you connect the motion of different points on the same body.
