## Why Engineers Switch to Energy

Newton's second law is local. It tells you what acceleration is happening at an instant. Energy methods are cumulative. They tell you how the system state changes between two positions.

That is powerful when:

- the motion path is known but time is not important
- several forces act over a distance
- you care about final speed more than intermediate acceleration
- springs, gravity, and friction all interact in one system

In those cases, work and energy often reduce a multi-step force analysis to one well-structured equation.

## Work Done by a Force

$$
\boxed{W = \int \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r}}
$$

For a constant force acting at angle $\theta$ to the displacement:

$$
\boxed{W = Fd\cos \theta}
$$

The sign of work matters:

- positive work adds kinetic energy
- negative work removes kinetic energy
- zero work means the force does not change the body's speed through that displacement

## Common Work Models

| Force type | Useful expression | Sign intuition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Constant force | $W = Fd\cos \theta$ | Positive if force helps motion |
| Gravity | $W_g = -mg\Delta h$ | Negative when moving upward |
| Spring | $W_s = \frac{1}{2}k(x_1^2 - x_2^2)$ | Depends on compression change |
| Kinetic friction | $W_f = -f_k d = -\mu_k N d$ | Always resists sliding |

## Kinetic Energy

$$
\boxed{T = \frac{1}{2}mv^2}
$$

Kinetic energy depends on speed, not direction. A car moving east at 20 m/s and one moving west at 20 m/s have the same kinetic energy.

## The Work-Energy Theorem

$$
\boxed{W_{net} = \Delta T = T_2 - T_1}
$$

Expanded for a particle:

$$
W_{net} = \frac{1}{2}mv_2^2 - \frac{1}{2}mv_1^2
$$

This is the energy version of cause and effect:

- forces do work
- net work changes kinetic energy
- changed kinetic energy changes speed

## Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces

### Conservative forces

These are forces whose work depends only on the start and end states, not the exact path.

Examples:

- gravity
- ideal spring force

For these forces, we introduce potential energy $V$ and write:

$$
W_c = -\Delta V
$$

### Non-conservative forces

These depend on the actual path or on energy dissipation mechanisms.

Examples:

- kinetic friction
- drag
- active propulsion losses

For these, it is often clearer to keep them as explicit work terms such as $W_{nc}$.

## Two Useful Energy Forms

When only conservative forces act:

$$
\boxed{T_1 + V_1 = T_2 + V_2}
$$

When non-conservative work is present:

$$
\boxed{T_1 + V_1 + W_{nc} = T_2 + V_2}
$$

This second equation is usually the safer default in engineering problems because real systems often include friction, damping, or external actuators.

## Assumption Callout: What Energy Methods Hide

Energy methods are powerful because they compress motion into a before-and-after statement. That efficiency also means they often hide the detailed time history.

When you use work and energy, you are usually assuming that:

- the start and end states are the main quantities of interest,
- the chosen work terms capture the dominant energy transfers,
- and neglected losses are small enough not to change the design decision materially.

That is why energy methods are excellent for speed, height, compression, and stopping-distance estimates, but less informative when you care about peak force, transient acceleration, or exact timing.

## Worked Example

### Example 2.2: Block Sliding Down an Incline with Friction

**Problem:** A 5 kg block slides 3 m down a $30^\circ$ incline with $\mu_k = 0.2$. Starting from rest, find the final speed.

### Step A: Define the initial and final states

- Initial kinetic energy: $T_1 = 0$ because the block starts from rest
- Final potential energy can be taken as zero at the bottom
- Initial height change:

$$
h = 3\sin 30^\circ = 1.5 \text{ m}
$$

### Step B: Compute the gravitational potential drop

$$
V_1 - V_2 = mgh = 5(9.81)(1.5) = 73.6 \text{ J}
$$

### Step C: Compute friction work

First find the normal force:

$$
N = mg\cos 30^\circ = 42.5 \text{ N}
$$

Then the friction force:

$$
f_k = \mu_k N = 0.2(42.5) = 8.50 \text{ N}
$$

Work of friction:

$$
W_f = -f_k d = -(8.50)(3) = -25.5 \text{ J}
$$

### Step D: Write the energy equation

$$
T_1 + V_1 + W_f = T_2 + V_2
$$

$$
0 + 73.6 - 25.5 = \frac{1}{2}(5)v_2^2 + 0
$$

$$
48.1 = 2.5v_2^2
$$

$$
v_2 = \sqrt{19.24} = 4.39 \text{ m/s}
$$

The speed is lower than the frictionless case because some of the gravitational energy was dissipated by friction.

## What Students Usually Miss

- They forget that friction work is negative.
- They mix up height change and path length.
- They use $mgh$ with the full incline length instead of the vertical drop.
- They assume "energy is conserved" even when friction is present, rather than writing the more general balance with $W_{nc}$.

## Engineering Trade-Off: Detailed Force History Versus State-to-State Clarity

Work-energy methods often outperform force-balance methods when the path is known and the final state matters most. But that convenience comes with a modeling trade-off.

Energy analysis is strong when you need:

- final speed,
- required spring compression,
- climb or descent capability,
- or total dissipation over a travel distance.

It is weaker when you need:

- peak actuator load,
- contact force history,
- instantaneous acceleration,
- or control-oriented timing.

In practice, engineers often use both views together: energy to check feasibility quickly, and force-time analysis to refine the loads that control design.

## Comparative Example: Same Ramp, Two Design Choices

Suppose two 12 kg delivery carts descend the same 4 m ramp segment with a 1.2 m vertical drop.

- **Cart A:** conventional wheels, effective non-conservative loss of 18 J
- **Cart B:** improved bearings and lower rolling resistance, effective non-conservative loss of 8 J

Using the energy balance,

$$
T_1 + V_1 + W_{nc} = T_2 + V_2
$$

and assuming both start from rest:

$$
mgh + W_{nc} = \frac{1}{2}mv^2
$$

### Cart A

$$
(12)(9.81)(1.2) - 18 = \frac{1}{2}(12)v_A^2
$$

$$
141.3 - 18 = 6v_A^2
$$

$$
v_A = 4.53 \text{ m/s}
$$

### Cart B

$$
(12)(9.81)(1.2) - 8 = 6v_B^2
$$

$$
v_B = 4.71 \text{ m/s}
$$

The difference is small in equation count but meaningful in design interpretation. Lower dissipation improves speed efficiency, but it may also increase stopping demand downstream. Energy methods make that trade-off visible very quickly.

## Summary

- Work is the energy transfer caused by a force acting through a displacement.
- Net work changes kinetic energy.
- Conservative forces can be converted into potential-energy terms.
- Friction and other dissipative effects should remain explicit unless you are modeling a truly conservative system.
