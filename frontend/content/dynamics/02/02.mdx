## Opening: The Crumple Zone

When a car hits a barrier at 25 m/s, the driver survives not because the crash is gentle but because the front of the car is engineered to do a very specific amount of work. A modern crumple zone deliberately deforms, spreading the change in the car's kinetic energy over roughly half a meter of crush distance instead of a few millimeters of rigid contact. Because force times distance must equal the kinetic energy removed, lengthening the distance lowers the peak force on the occupant. A rigid bumper would stop the same car in a centimeter and multiply the deceleration force by a hundred.

That is the entire philosophy of energy methods in one example. Instead of tracking the violent, millisecond-by-millisecond force history of the collision, an engineer can write a single statement: the kinetic energy at impact equals the work absorbed by the structure. The same idea sizes elevator buffers, ski-lift catch springs, regenerative brakes, and pile drivers.

> **The Big Picture:** Newton's second law answers "what is happening right now." Work and energy answer "what changed between here and there." For many design questions, the second is the cheaper and clearer question.

## Why Engineers Switch to Energy

Newton's second law is local. It tells you what acceleration is happening at an instant. Energy methods are cumulative. They tell you how the system state changes between two positions.

That is powerful when:

- the motion path is known but time is not important
- several forces act over a distance
- you care about final speed more than intermediate acceleration
- springs, gravity, and friction all interact in one system

In those cases, work and energy often reduce a multi-step force analysis to one well-structured equation.

## Work Done by a Force

$$
\boxed{W = \int \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r}}
$$

For a constant force acting at angle $\theta$ to the displacement:

$$
\boxed{W = Fd\cos \theta}
$$

The dot product is the key idea: only the component of force along the displacement does work. A force perpendicular to motion (such as the normal force on a sliding block, or the tension in a string holding a ball in circular motion) does zero work no matter how large it is.

The sign of work matters:

- positive work adds kinetic energy
- negative work removes kinetic energy
- zero work means the force does not change the body's speed through that displacement

## Common Work Models

| Force type | Useful expression | Sign intuition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Constant force | $W = Fd\cos \theta$ | Positive if force helps motion |
| Gravity | $W_g = -mg\Delta h$ | Negative when moving upward |
| Spring | $W_s = \frac{1}{2}k(x_1^2 - x_2^2)$ | Depends on compression change |
| Kinetic friction | $W_f = -f_k d = -\mu_k N d$ | Always resists sliding |

The spring expression deserves a note, because students often misremember its sign. The force a linear spring exerts is $F = -kx$, opposing the stretch. Integrating that force from $x_1$ to $x_2$,

$$
W_s = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} (-kx)\, dx = \tfrac{1}{2}k\left(x_1^2 - x_2^2\right).
$$

So a spring that relaxes from a compression of $x_1$ to its free length ($x_2 = 0$) does positive work $\tfrac{1}{2}kx_1^2$ on whatever it pushes, which is exactly the energy it stored while being compressed.

## Kinetic Energy

$$
\boxed{T = \frac{1}{2}mv^2}
$$

Kinetic energy depends on speed, not direction. A car moving east at 20 m/s and one moving west at 20 m/s have the same kinetic energy.

## The Work-Energy Theorem

$$
\boxed{W_{net} = \Delta T = T_2 - T_1}
$$

Expanded for a particle:

$$
W_{net} = \frac{1}{2}mv_2^2 - \frac{1}{2}mv_1^2
$$

### Where the Theorem Comes From

The theorem is not a separate law; it is Newton's second law integrated over distance. Start from $\sum \vec{F} = m\vec{a}$ along the path and take the dot product with $d\vec{r}$:

$$
\sum \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r} = m\,\vec{a} \cdot d\vec{r}.
$$

Using $\vec{a} = \dfrac{d\vec{v}}{dt}$ and $d\vec{r} = \vec{v}\,dt$, the right side becomes $m\,\vec{v} \cdot d\vec{v}$. Integrating from state 1 to state 2,

$$
\int_1^2 \sum \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r} = m \int_1^2 \vec{v} \cdot d\vec{v} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv_2^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}mv_1^2.
$$

The left side is exactly the net work, so $W_{net} = T_2 - T_1$. This derivation explains why the theorem is so general: it inherits the validity of Newton's second law and adds no new assumptions.

This is the energy version of cause and effect:

- forces do work
- net work changes kinetic energy
- changed kinetic energy changes speed

## Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces

### Conservative forces

These are forces whose work depends only on the start and end states, not the exact path.

Examples:

- gravity
- ideal spring force

For these forces, we introduce potential energy $V$ and write:

$$
W_c = -\Delta V
$$

### Non-conservative forces

These depend on the actual path or on energy dissipation mechanisms.

Examples:

- kinetic friction
- drag
- active propulsion losses

For these, it is often clearer to keep them as explicit work terms such as $W_{nc}$.

## Two Useful Energy Forms

When only conservative forces act:

$$
\boxed{T_1 + V_1 = T_2 + V_2}
$$

When non-conservative work is present:

$$
\boxed{T_1 + V_1 + W_{nc} = T_2 + V_2}
$$

This second equation is usually the safer default in engineering problems because real systems often include friction, damping, or external actuators.

## Assumption Callout: What Energy Methods Hide

Energy methods are powerful because they compress motion into a before-and-after statement. That efficiency also means they often hide the detailed time history.

When you use work and energy, you are usually assuming that:

- the start and end states are the main quantities of interest,
- the chosen work terms capture the dominant energy transfers,
- and neglected losses are small enough not to change the design decision materially.

That is why energy methods are excellent for speed, height, compression, and stopping-distance estimates, but less informative when you care about peak force, transient acceleration, or exact timing.

## Worked Examples

### Example 2.1: Spring-Launched Slider with Friction

**Problem:** A 0.5 kg slider rests against a spring of stiffness $k = 800$ N/m on a horizontal track. The spring is compressed 0.15 m and released. After leaving the spring, the slider crosses a rough patch 0.6 m long with $\mu_k = 0.25$ before reaching a sensor. Find (a) the launch speed as the slider leaves the spring, and (b) the speed at the sensor.

**Free body diagram (rough patch).** Picture the slider on the horizontal track: weight $W = mg$ down, normal force $N$ up, and kinetic friction $f_k$ pointing backward against the motion. There is no vertical motion, so $N = mg$.

**Step 1: Work done by the spring.** The spring relaxes from $x_1 = 0.15$ m to $x_2 = 0$:

$$
W_s = \tfrac{1}{2}k\left(x_1^2 - x_2^2\right) = \tfrac{1}{2}(800)(0.15^2 - 0) = 9.00 \text{ J}
$$

**Step 2: Launch speed (part a).** On the smooth section the only horizontal work is the spring's, and the slider starts from rest, so $W_s = \tfrac{1}{2}mv_1^2$:

$$
v_1 = \sqrt{\frac{2W_s}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(9.00)}{0.5}} = \boxed{6.00 \text{ m/s}}
$$

**Step 3: Friction work on the rough patch.** First the normal force, then friction:

$$
N = mg = (0.5)(9.81) = 4.905 \text{ N}
$$

$$
f_k = \mu_k N = (0.25)(4.905) = 1.226 \text{ N}
$$

$$
W_f = -f_k d = -(1.226)(0.6) = -0.736 \text{ J}
$$

**Step 4: Speed at the sensor (part b).** Apply the work-energy theorem across the whole motion, from rest to the sensor:

$$
W_s + W_f = \tfrac{1}{2}mv_2^2
$$

$$
9.00 - 0.736 = \tfrac{1}{2}(0.5)v_2^2 = 0.25\,v_2^2
$$

$$
v_2 = \sqrt{\frac{8.264}{0.25}} = \boxed{5.75 \text{ m/s}}
$$

The slider arrives a little slower than its launch speed because the rough patch removed 0.74 J of the 9.00 J the spring delivered.

### Example 2.2: Block Sliding Down an Incline with Friction

**Problem:** A 5 kg block slides 3 m down a $30^\circ$ incline with $\mu_k = 0.2$. Starting from rest, find the final speed.

**Free body diagram (on the incline).** Tilt the axes with the slope: weight $mg$ down, normal force $N$ perpendicular to the surface, and friction $f_k$ up the slope opposing the downhill slide. The component of weight along the slope drives the motion; the component perpendicular to the slope sets the normal force.

### Step A: Define the initial and final states

- Initial kinetic energy: $T_1 = 0$ because the block starts from rest
- Final potential energy can be taken as zero at the bottom
- Initial height change:

$$
h = 3\sin 30^\circ = 1.5 \text{ m}
$$

### Step B: Compute the gravitational potential drop

$$
V_1 - V_2 = mgh = 5(9.81)(1.5) = 73.6 \text{ J}
$$

### Step C: Compute friction work

First find the normal force:

$$
N = mg\cos 30^\circ = 42.5 \text{ N}
$$

Then the friction force:

$$
f_k = \mu_k N = 0.2(42.5) = 8.50 \text{ N}
$$

Work of friction:

$$
W_f = -f_k d = -(8.50)(3) = -25.5 \text{ J}
$$

### Step D: Write the energy equation

$$
T_1 + V_1 + W_f = T_2 + V_2
$$

$$
0 + 73.6 - 25.5 = \frac{1}{2}(5)v_2^2 + 0
$$

$$
48.1 = 2.5v_2^2
$$

$$
v_2 = \sqrt{19.24} = \boxed{4.39 \text{ m/s}}
$$

The speed is lower than the frictionless case (which would give $v_2 = \sqrt{2gh} = 5.42$ m/s) because some of the gravitational energy was dissipated by friction.

### Example 2.3: Stopping Distance of a Braking Car

**Problem:** A 1200 kg car travels at 25 m/s when the driver locks the brakes on dry pavement, $\mu_k = 0.7$. Using energy methods, find the stopping distance.

**Free body diagram.** On level ground: weight $mg$ down, normal force $N = mg$ up, and friction $f_k$ backward, opposing motion. Friction is the only force doing work along the road.

**Step 1: Initial kinetic energy.**

$$
T_1 = \tfrac{1}{2}mv_0^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(1200)(25^2) = 375{,}000 \text{ J}
$$

**Step 2: Friction force.**

$$
f_k = \mu_k mg = (0.7)(1200)(9.81) = 8240 \text{ N}
$$

**Step 3: Work-energy balance.** The car ends at rest, so all kinetic energy is removed by friction over distance $d$:

$$
-f_k d = 0 - T_1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad d = \frac{T_1}{f_k} = \frac{375{,}000}{8240} = \boxed{45.5 \text{ m}}
$$

Notice the design lesson hidden in the algebra: $d = \dfrac{v_0^2}{2\mu_k g}$ scales with the **square** of speed. Doubling the speed quadruples the stopping distance, which is exactly why posted speed limits dominate crash severity.

## What Students Usually Miss

- They forget that friction work is negative.
- They mix up height change and path length.
- They use $mgh$ with the full incline length instead of the vertical drop.
- They assume "energy is conserved" even when friction is present, rather than writing the more general balance with $W_{nc}$.

## Engineering Trade-Off: Detailed Force History Versus State-to-State Clarity

Work-energy methods often outperform force-balance methods when the path is known and the final state matters most. But that convenience comes with a modeling trade-off.

Energy analysis is strong when you need:

- final speed,
- required spring compression,
- climb or descent capability,
- or total dissipation over a travel distance.

It is weaker when you need:

- peak actuator load,
- contact force history,
- instantaneous acceleration,
- or control-oriented timing.

In practice, engineers often use both views together: energy to check feasibility quickly, and force-time analysis to refine the loads that control design.

## Comparative Example: Same Ramp, Two Design Choices

Suppose two 12 kg delivery carts descend the same 4 m ramp segment with a 1.2 m vertical drop.

- **Cart A:** conventional wheels, effective non-conservative loss of 18 J
- **Cart B:** improved bearings and lower rolling resistance, effective non-conservative loss of 8 J

Using the energy balance,

$$
T_1 + V_1 + W_{nc} = T_2 + V_2
$$

and assuming both start from rest:

$$
mgh + W_{nc} = \frac{1}{2}mv^2
$$

### Cart A

$$
(12)(9.81)(1.2) - 18 = \frac{1}{2}(12)v_A^2
$$

$$
141.3 - 18 = 6v_A^2
$$

$$
v_A = 4.53 \text{ m/s}
$$

### Cart B

$$
(12)(9.81)(1.2) - 8 = 6v_B^2
$$

$$
v_B = 4.71 \text{ m/s}
$$

The difference is small in equation count but meaningful in design interpretation. Lower dissipation improves speed efficiency, but it may also increase stopping demand downstream. Energy methods make that trade-off visible very quickly.

## Engineering Applications

| Application | How work and energy are used |
| --- | --- |
| Vehicle crumple zones | Crush distance is sized so absorbed work limits peak deceleration force |
| Regenerative braking | Kinetic energy is captured as stored charge instead of friction heat |
| Elevator buffer springs | Spring work $\tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$ must absorb the car's kinetic energy on overtravel |
| Pile drivers and forging | Dropped-mass potential energy converts to plastic work in the workpiece |
| Roller coasters | Energy balance sets minimum hill clearance and loop entry speed |

> **Engineering in Action:** Whether the device stores energy (a launch spring), dissipates it (a brake), or recovers it (a regenerative drive), the design question is the same accounting statement: where does the kinetic energy go, and over what distance.

## Summary

### Key Equations

| Concept | Equation |
| --- | --- |
| Work (general) | $W = \int \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r}$ |
| Work (constant force) | $W = Fd\cos\theta$ |
| Kinetic energy | $T = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ |
| Work-energy theorem | $W_{net} = T_2 - T_1$ |
| Spring work | $W_s = \tfrac{1}{2}k(x_1^2 - x_2^2)$ |
| Gravity work | $W_g = -mg\Delta h$ |
| Friction work | $W_f = -\mu_k N d$ |
| Energy balance with losses | $T_1 + V_1 + W_{nc} = T_2 + V_2$ |

### Key Takeaways

- Work is the energy transfer caused by a force acting through a displacement.
- Net work changes kinetic energy, and the theorem is just $\sum F = ma$ integrated over distance.
- Conservative forces can be converted into potential-energy terms.
- Friction and other dissipative effects should remain explicit unless you are modeling a truly conservative system.
- Stopping distance scales with the square of speed, a direct consequence of the energy balance.
