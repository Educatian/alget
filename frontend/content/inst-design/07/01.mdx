## Designing Beyond the "Average" Learner

For much of the 20th century, instructional systems and physical systems were designed around an imaginary average user. The problem with that logic is simple: **the average learner is a statistical abstraction, not a real person**. Human variability is not noise around the edge of design. It is the central design condition.

This insight became especially influential when the US Air Force discovered in the 1950s that no single pilot matched all the average body measurements used to design cockpit seats. When engineers designed for the average, they ended up designing for almost nobody. Instructional design faces the same trap when it assumes that all learners read at the same pace, process media in the same way, express competence in the same format, or stay engaged under the same conditions.

In education, this realization led to **Universal Design for Learning (UDL)**, developed by CAST as a framework for designing flexible learning environments from the beginning rather than retrofitting accommodations after barriers appear. UDL is not a special add-on for a small subgroup. It is a proactive design philosophy that treats variability as normal.

## Why UDL Matters for Instructional Designers

A strong instructional designer does not ask only, "Is this content accurate?" They also ask:

- Who might be excluded by this format?
- Where does this activity assume background knowledge or physical ability?
- Which parts of the experience create unnecessary friction that is unrelated to the learning goal?
- How can learners reach the same objective through multiple valid pathways?

UDL matters because many barriers in learning are not caused by the learner. They are caused by the design.

Consider a module that teaches ethical decision-making through dense legal prose only. A learner with dyslexia may appear to "struggle with ethics" when the actual barrier is decoding heavy text. A learner who understands the content well but has executive-function challenges may fail a timed written response even though they can explain the reasoning perfectly in speech. In both cases, the learner is not the primary defect. The design is.

That distinction changes responsibility. Instead of waiting for a learner to request special treatment, UDL asks the designer to anticipate predictable variability and build options in advance.

## The Three Brain Networks in UDL

UDL often maps its design guidance to three broad brain networks involved in learning.

### 1. Recognition Networks: The "What" of Learning

These systems help learners perceive, interpret, and categorize information.

- Design implication: provide multiple means of **representation**
- Typical barrier: relying on one mode, such as dense text or audio only
- Better move: pair key explanations with visuals, transcripts, labels, examples, and definitions

If a learner cannot perceive the information clearly, they cannot even begin the cognitive work.

### 2. Strategic Networks: The "How" of Learning

These systems support planning, responding, and demonstrating understanding.

- Design implication: provide multiple means of **action and expression**
- Typical barrier: requiring one narrow output format unrelated to the true objective
- Better move: allow essays, presentations, annotated diagrams, simulations, or recorded explanations when the objective permits

If the goal is "demonstrate causal reasoning," the assessment should not accidentally become a test of typing speed or handwriting fluency.

### 3. Affective Networks: The "Why" of Learning

These systems shape motivation, persistence, relevance, and emotional investment.

- Design implication: provide multiple means of **engagement**
- Typical barrier: one motivational style imposed on everyone
- Better move: offer choice, relevance, challenge tuning, self-pacing, collaboration options, and low-risk entry points

Some learners thrive under public competition. Others shut down when performance is made visible too early. Good design creates more than one path into engagement.

## UDL and Accessibility Are Related but Not Identical

UDL is often discussed alongside accessibility standards such as WCAG, but they are not the same thing.

- **Accessibility** asks whether the experience is technically usable by people with disabilities.
- **UDL** asks whether the learning environment is flexibly designed so that learners with varied needs can meaningfully participate and succeed.

Accessibility is a minimum threshold. UDL is a broader design stance.

For example:

- Captioning a video supports accessibility.
- Offering a transcript, chapter markers, a visual summary, and a replayable worked example reflects UDL.

The first prevents exclusion. The second supports flexible learning.

## Technical Accessibility: WCAG as Design Constraint

In digital learning environments, UDL often depends on concrete technical accessibility standards, most notably the **Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG)**.

WCAG is not just a compliance checklist for developers. It has direct instructional consequences because usability barriers distort learning evidence. If a learner cannot navigate a module, the system cannot validly interpret their performance.

Key WCAG-related design checks include:

- **Color contrast:** body text and controls must meet contrast thresholds such as 4.5:1 for standard text
- **Keyboard navigation:** all learning tasks should be completable without a mouse
- **Semantic structure:** headings, buttons, landmarks, and form labels must be machine-readable
- **Alt text and media alternatives:** images, charts, and audio should have equivalent access pathways

If a screen reader user cannot identify the Next button, the instructional sequence breaks. If a drag-and-drop interaction has no keyboard equivalent, success becomes a motor-control test rather than a learning task.

## A Practical Tradeoff Matrix for UDL Decisions

Designers often misunderstand UDL as "make everything available in every format." That is rarely realistic. The better approach is to make deliberate tradeoffs.

Use this planning matrix:

- **Keep fixed:** the core learning objective and success criteria
- **Make flexible:** representation formats, pacing supports, practice pathways, and expression modes
- **Audit carefully:** any tool or task that introduces sensory, language, motor, or executive barriers
- **Document clearly:** which options are equivalent and which are optional supports

For example, if the objective is to interpret classroom assessment data:

- You might keep the requirement that learners justify decisions with evidence.
- You might allow them to submit a written memo, narrated slides, or an annotated dashboard review.
- You should avoid requiring a complex interface interaction unless that interaction is itself part of the target skill.

This is the core UDL move: preserve rigor, reduce irrelevant barriers.

## Common Design Mistakes

When teams first adopt UDL, they often make one of four mistakes:

- They confuse flexibility with lowered standards.
- They provide multiple media formats but keep only one narrow assessment pathway.
- They add accommodations after launch instead of designing options from the start.
- They treat accessibility as a legal checkbox instead of a validity issue for learning evidence.

A learner who fails because the interface is inaccessible has not truly failed the content. The system has failed to measure learning fairly.

## Designer Checklist for UDL Reviews

Before approving a lesson or course, ask:

- Can learners perceive the essential information in more than one way?
- Can learners demonstrate understanding without being trapped in one arbitrary response format?
- Are there parallel pathways for social, independent, and paced participation?
- Do the assessments measure the intended skill rather than incidental barriers?
- Would a screen reader user, keyboard-only user, or multilingual learner still have a legitimate route to success?

If several of those answers are "no," the course may still function for some learners, but it is not yet designed for learner variability.
