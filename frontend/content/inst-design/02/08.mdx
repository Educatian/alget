## Why Online Learning Is Not Just Classroom Learning Recorded

The most expensive mistake in online instructional design is to treat the digital course as a classroom course delivered through a different pipe. Two reasons that fails. First, the **temporal and spatial structure** of online learning is fundamentally different — learners are not synchronized in time, are not co-located, and are not under the same continuous social pressure that an in-room cohort produces. Second, the **modes of interaction** that the classroom takes for granted — eye contact, hallway questions, the instructor noticing a confused face — disappear unless the design replaces them with something deliberate.

Online learning design therefore has its own theory base. Two frameworks dominate the literature: **Moore's three types of interaction** and the **Community of Inquiry (CoI) model** by Garrison, Anderson, and Archer. Both are foundational in Reiser and Dempsey, in Smith and Ragan, and in nearly every online-learning chapter of the canonical ID texts.

This module covers the design moves that distinguish a defensible online course from a recorded lecture series.

## Moore's Three Types of Interaction

Michael Moore (1989) argued that any instructional system can be analyzed by which interactions it supports and how strongly. He named three:

**Learner–content interaction.** The learner reads, watches, queries, manipulates, or otherwise engages the subject matter directly. This is the only type that exists in a self-paced, asynchronous, instructor-absent course. Strong learner–content design pays attention to retrieval practice, worked examples, segmenting, and embedded checks (Gagné Event 6 lives here).

**Learner–instructor interaction.** The learner receives direct feedback, coaching, or correction from a content expert. Online forms include graded feedback on submissions, instructor-led webinars, threaded forum responses, and one-to-one coaching messages. The frequency and specificity of this interaction predict perceived course quality more strongly than any other variable in the empirical literature.

**Learner–learner interaction.** Learners exchange ideas, work, or critique with peers. Online forms include forum discussion, peer review, group projects, and synchronous collaborative work. Empirical effects are more variable than the other two types: well-structured peer interaction has strong effects on retention and transfer; loose, unmoderated forums often have negligible effect.

A balanced online course intentionally allocates **all three** types. A course that only has learner–content interaction is a textbook with a video player. A course that only has learner–instructor interaction is a tutoring relationship. A course that only has learner–learner interaction is a discussion club. None of those alone is a course.

## The Community of Inquiry Model

Garrison, Anderson, and Archer's Community of Inquiry framework (2000, refined many times since) describes the conditions under which deep learning emerges in online environments. They name three overlapping presences.

**Cognitive presence** is the extent to which learners construct meaning through sustained reflection and discourse. It moves through four phases: a triggering event raises a question, exploration gathers relevant information, integration synthesizes ideas, and resolution commits to a defensible answer. Online courses fail at cognitive presence when discussion stops at exploration and never reaches integration or resolution.

**Social presence** is the degree to which participants experience each other as real people, not just usernames. It includes affective expression, open communication, and group cohesion. Without social presence, learner–learner interaction becomes transactional and shallow.

**Teaching presence** is the design and direction of the educational experience. It includes course architecture, facilitation of discourse, and direct instruction. Teaching presence is the variable an instructional designer most directly controls — it lives in the syllabus, the prompts, the rubrics, the announcements, and the timing of feedback.

The intersection of all three is where deep learning happens. A course strong in only one or two presences may feel functional but rarely produces durable transfer.

## Asynchronous, Synchronous, Hybrid: Choosing the Mode

The mode question is not "which is better" but "which fits the objective."

**Asynchronous** (learners engage on their own time) excels at deep reading, reflective writing, complex problem solving, and learners who need time to formulate. It struggles with real-time skill demonstration, quick clarification, and the social cohesion that synchronous time builds. Defensible asynchronous design uses pacing scaffolds — weekly checkpoints, due dates, peer-review windows — to prevent procrastination and isolation.

**Synchronous** (learners engage at the same time) excels at real-time discourse, demonstration, and rapid feedback loops. It struggles with timezone-distributed cohorts, learners who process slowly under verbal pressure, and content that benefits from rewatching. Defensible synchronous design budgets time deliberately, uses breakout structures rather than long lectures, and records sessions for asynchronous reuse.

**Hybrid / blended** combines the modes intentionally — for example, asynchronous pre-work, a synchronous discourse session, asynchronous follow-up. The mistake here is to layer modes additively rather than design for their interaction. Each mode should do what it does best; redundancy across modes wastes learner time.

## Designing for Self-Regulation

Online learners must regulate their own attention, pacing, and persistence to a degree classroom learners rarely do. Three design moves support self-regulation.

**Make pacing visible.** Show the learner where they are in the course, what is upcoming, and how their pace compares to expected pace. Hidden progress correlates with disengagement.

**Build in low-stakes checkpoints.** Frequent, short formative checks anchor attention and create natural breaks. They also generate the data the adaptive system needs.

**Surface learning strategies, not just content.** Tell the learner *how* to study the material — which prompts to retrieve, which mistakes to expect. Self-regulated learners exist on a spectrum, and the design should not assume the strong end.

## Common Online-Learning Design Anti-Patterns

A short audit of frequent mistakes:

- **Recorded-lecture saturation.** Long uninterrupted videos with no embedded checks. Working memory fades within minutes; the learner is not retrieving.
- **Forum-as-grading.** Requiring forum posts purely for participation credit, with no teaching presence directing the discourse. Cognitive presence stays at exploration and never integrates.
- **Feedback latency.** Submission turnarounds longer than a week destroy formative value. Feedback that arrives after the learner has moved on functions as a grade, not a learning signal.
- **Tool sprawl.** Different modules using different tools, each with its own login. Cognitive load from interface variability becomes a hidden tax on learning.
- **Synchronous mass lectures.** Treating webinars as broadcasts. The synchronous mode wasted on one-way content delivery is the most expensive mode for the lowest-value purpose.

Each of these signals a missing presence, a mode-purpose mismatch, or a self-regulation gap.

---

## Quality Audit for an Online Course

Before launch:

- Does each module include all three Moore interactions, weighted intentionally?
- Does the course architecture make teaching presence visible (clear sequencing, rubrics, expectations)?
- Are cognitive presence prompts designed to push past exploration into integration and resolution?
- Are social presence opportunities built in (introductions, low-stakes peer interactions, named instructor voice)?
- Is mode (asynchronous/synchronous/hybrid) chosen per objective rather than as a default?
- Are pacing, formative checkpoints, and learning-strategy guidance explicit?

If several answers are "no," the course will run, but it will under-perform its potential.
