## Opening: One Process Did Not Fit

A learning-and-development lead at a hospital network was asked to build two things in the same quarter. The first was a 6-month certification course for nurses on a new infusion-pump protocol, where a single wrong instruction could harm a patient and where state auditors would later inspect the assessment. The second was a 20-minute refresher on a new scheduling app, where the requirements were vague, the deadline was three weeks out, and nobody could say in advance what "good" looked like.

The lead's instinct was to run both projects through the same trusted process, ADDIE, executed front to back. The certification course came out strong. The scheduling refresher came out late, over-documented, and still wrong, because the team spent two of their three weeks writing an analysis report for a problem that only became clear once a rough prototype was in front of real users.

> **The Big Picture:** The failure was not ADDIE. It was treating one process model as the answer to every kind of project. Different projects carry different risks (accuracy, speed, ambiguity, feasibility), and the right process model is the one that attacks the dominant risk first.

This module compares the major instructional design process models and gives you a defensible way to choose among them.

---

By the end of this section, you will be able to:

4. **Explain** why mature designers run an alignment model and a delivery model at the same time.

## Why a Single Model Is Not Enough

When most people first meet instructional design, they meet ADDIE: Analyze, Design, Develop, Implement, Evaluate. Five clean phases, often drawn as a linear arrow. ADDIE is a useful shared vocabulary, but treating it as the only ID model produces brittle designers. Real projects rarely march left to right through the five letters. They iterate, skip back, and run phases in parallel.

Instructional design models are best understood as **lenses**, not as recipes. Each one foregrounds a different concern: systematic alignment, learner-centered design, rapid iteration, classroom feasibility, or organizational performance. Picking the right model, or knowing when to mix several, is part of professional judgment.

A helpful way to organize the field is along two axes. The first axis is **how much the model front-loads specification** (heavy analysis and documentation up front versus learning by building). The second axis is **how the model treats change** (change as a defect to be controlled versus change as the expected mode of progress). Where a model sits on those two axes tells you the kind of project it serves well.

| Axis | Front-loaded / Control change | Build-to-learn / Embrace change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Models** | Dick & Carey, Smith & Ragan | SAM, Agile/Lean |
| **Strength** | Traceability, defensibility, alignment | Speed, responsiveness, learner signal |
| **Risk it attacks** | Wrong objectives/assessment slipping through | Building the wrong thing in detail |
| **Fails when** | Requirements are still unknown | Accuracy must be guaranteed pre-release |

Kemp sits in the middle: structured like the alignment models, but explicitly non-linear like the iterative ones.

## Dick, Carey, and Carey: The Systems View

The **Dick, Carey, and Carey model** (often shortened to "Dick and Carey") is the most explicitly systems-oriented model in mainstream ID. Its signature move is to treat instruction as a tightly coupled system in which a change to one component requires recalculation of the others.

The model has nine interacting components:

1. Identify instructional goals
2. Conduct instructional analysis (including subordinate skills)
3. Analyze learners and contexts
4. Write performance objectives
5. Develop assessment instruments
6. Develop instructional strategy
7. Develop and select materials
8. Design and conduct formative evaluation
9. Revise instruction (then conduct summative evaluation)

The strong contribution of Dick and Carey is **alignment discipline**. Objectives, assessment, and strategy are designed in lockstep, and any drift between them is treated as a defect. Teams that struggle with assessment-objective drift, mismatched item types, or vague performance criteria benefit most from this model. The deliberate ordering, objectives (step 4) before assessment (step 5) before strategy (step 6), is what forces backward design: you cannot write a test before you know the objective, and you cannot pick a teaching strategy before you know what the test will demand.

Its weakness is also its strength: the model can feel heavy for short-cycle work. A 30-minute microlearning module rarely needs a full subordinate-skills hierarchy, and forcing one wastes the budget on documentation nobody will read.

---

## The Kemp Model: Continuous and Non-Linear

The **Kemp model** (also associated with Morrison, Ross, Kalman, and Kemp) reorganizes ID work into nine elements arranged in an oval rather than a sequence. The elements include instructional problems, learner characteristics, task analysis, instructional objectives, content sequencing, instructional strategies, message design, instructional delivery, and evaluation instruments. Surrounding the oval are continuous activities such as planning, project management, support services, and formative evaluation.

The Kemp model captures something honest about real ID work:

- You can start at any element where you have new information.
- You revisit earlier elements whenever a downstream decision exposes a gap.
- Project management, budgeting, and stakeholder communication run continuously, not as a separate phase.

Kemp is especially well suited for **classroom and instructional designers in K-12 and higher education**, where the work is rarely linear and where the designer often also teaches the course. A teacher redesigning a unit might begin at "content sequencing" because the textbook changed, then loop back to "instructional objectives" when the new sequence reveals a missing prerequisite. Kemp legitimizes that loop instead of treating it as a process violation.

## Smith and Ragan: A Designer-Friendly Decomposition

The **Smith and Ragan model** is closer in spirit to ADDIE, but with a sharper focus on the cognitive psychology that justifies design decisions. Their three macro phases are Analysis, Strategy, and Evaluation, and within each they expose concrete decision points the designer must defend.

For example, under Strategy, Smith and Ragan distinguish between:

- **Organizational strategy** (sequencing and structuring of content)
- **Delivery strategy** (medium, mode, and grouping of learners)
- **Management strategy** (pacing, learner control, and progress monitoring)

This separation matters because many novice designers conflate all three into one decision. They pick "a video course" and treat that single choice as if it answered sequencing, medium, and pacing at once. Smith and Ragan explicitly tell you to design the three separately and then verify their interaction, because a delivery choice (self-paced video) can quietly undermine an organizational choice (tightly scaffolded sequence) if pacing is left entirely to the learner.

The model is widely used in graduate ID programs because it scaffolds **why** decisions are made, not just what to do.

## SAM: The Successive Approximation Model

The **Successive Approximation Model (SAM)**, developed by Michael Allen and colleagues, was created in direct response to perceived rigidity in ADDIE. SAM has three phases:

1. **Preparation**: quick gathering of background, goals, and constraints
2. **Iterative Design**: short cycles of prototyping, review, and revision
3. **Iterative Development**: implementation cycles that continue to refine the working product

The cultural shift behind SAM is large. SAM expects that:

- Stakeholders cannot fully specify what they want before they see something working.
- Early prototypes will be wrong, and that is the point.
- Decisions are made by testing artifacts, not by signing off on documents.

SAM is the right choice when **requirements are unclear, time is short, and rework is cheaper than over-specification**. It is a poor choice when high-stakes accuracy must be guaranteed before any learner sees the material, for example regulated medical or aviation training where a wrong prototype released too early creates real risk. The cure for that limitation is to keep SAM's iteration but route prototypes through SME and compliance review before any learner exposure.

## Agile and Lean ID

A growing body of practice borrows from software development. **Agile ID** treats the course as a set of small, deliverable learning chunks shipped in sprints. **Lean ID** treats every design decision as a hypothesis to be validated by learner data.

Agile and Lean share several commitments:

- **Working learning experiences over comprehensive design documents.**
- **Learner feedback loops over expert speculation.**
- **Continuous deployment of small improvements over big-bang releases.**

These approaches do not replace classical ID. They reframe it. The analysis still happens, but it is broken up and validated chunk by chunk. The evaluation still happens, but it is continuous rather than reserved for the end.

Agile is most powerful in **digital learning at scale** where instrumentation is cheap, iteration is cheap, and learner cohorts are large enough to detect signal. With only 12 learners, an A/B test of two lesson versions cannot reach a reliable conclusion; with 12,000, it can, and Lean ID becomes genuinely data-driven rather than data-decorated.

## A Side-by-Side Comparison

| Model | Defining emphasis | Best-fit project | Main limitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ADDIE | Shared phase vocabulary | Coordinating mixed teams | Linear if taken literally |
| Dick & Carey | Systems alignment | High-stakes, auditable content | Heavy for short cycles |
| Kemp | Non-linear, continuous | Classroom/higher-ed redesign | Less prescriptive for novices |
| Smith & Ragan | Justified strategy decisions | Teaching design reasoning | Still front-loaded |
| SAM | Rapid prototyping | Unclear stakeholder needs | Risky for regulated content |
| Agile / Lean | Validated iteration | Large-scale digital products | Needs telemetry and scale |

## Worked Example: Selecting a Model Under Real Constraints

**The brief.** A learning-and-development team must redesign a 4-week leadership pipeline for a regulated healthcare provider. Stakeholders cannot agree on requirements. The final assessment must be defensible to external auditors. The team has 6 weeks and a small pool of subject-matter experts (SMEs). Which model, or combination, should they use?

Work the decision the way a mature designer would, one constraint at a time. Predict each step before revealing it.

**Step 1: Name the dominant risks.**
Two risks compete. (a) *Accuracy/defensibility:* a regulated assessment that auditors will inspect. (b) *Ambiguity:* stakeholders cannot specify requirements. A single pure model serves only one of these well.

**Step 2: Map each risk to its natural model.**

| Risk in this project | Model that attacks it |
| --- | --- |
| Defensible objective-to-assessment alignment | Dick & Carey (or Smith & Ragan) |
| Requirements that only emerge by building | SAM (iterative prototyping) |

**Step 3: Resolve the conflict instead of picking one.**
The defensibility requirement is non-negotiable (auditors), so the *assessment and objectives* must be developed with alignment discipline. The ambiguity requirement is real but lives mostly in the *experience design* (activities, scenarios, sequencing), where iteration is safe. So split the project: govern the assessment with Dick and Carey, and run the experience design as SAM iterations.

**Step 4: Sequence under the time budget (6 weeks).**

- Weeks 1-2: Dick and Carey steps 1-5. Lock instructional goals, instructional analysis, learner/context analysis, performance objectives, and the assessment blueprint. This produces the auditable backbone early.
- Weeks 2-5: SAM iterative design over the *instructional strategy and materials*, three short prototype-review-revise cycles with the small SME pool, each cycle checked against the locked objectives so iteration cannot drift out of alignment.
- Week 6: Formative evaluation pass plus a compliance review of the final assessment before any learner sees it.

**Step 5: State the answer and why.**
Use **Dick and Carey as the alignment/governance model and SAM as the delivery model**, with the objective-and-assessment backbone frozen before iteration begins. This satisfies the auditors (traceable alignment) without paying the full front-loaded cost on the parts of the design that are genuinely uncertain. Pure SAM would risk an indefensible assessment; pure Dick and Carey would burn the 6-week budget specifying activities nobody had validated yet.

> **Why this is the textbook move:** The small SME pool is the hidden constraint. SAM's short cycles ration scarce SME attention better than a heavy linear process that needs SMEs available continuously for deep documentation review.

## Choosing a Model: A Quick Decision Frame

No model is universally best. Use this frame to choose:

- **High-stakes accuracy, regulated content** -> Dick and Carey or Smith and Ragan
- **Classroom or higher-ed redesign with shifting requirements** -> Kemp
- **Corporate microlearning with unclear stakeholder needs** -> SAM
- **Large-scale digital product with telemetry** -> Agile or Lean
- **Cross-team shared vocabulary, mixed audience** -> ADDIE as a coordination layer, with one of the above as the working model underneath

Mature designers usually run **two models at once**: a defensible alignment model (often Dick and Carey or Smith and Ragan) for evidence and accountability, and a flexible delivery model (SAM or Agile) for execution speed. The worked example above is exactly that pattern in action.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Treating ADDIE as a linear waterfall.** Writing a complete analysis document before any design, then never revisiting it, recreates the slow front-loaded failure even when the project demanded iteration.
- **Choosing a model by familiarity, not by risk.** "We always use X" ignores the project's dominant risk. Diagnose the risk first, then pick the model.
- **Iterating without an alignment anchor.** SAM and Agile drift if there is no frozen objective-and-assessment backbone to check each prototype against. Iteration is not an excuse to skip alignment.
- **Over-modeling small jobs.** A subordinate-skills hierarchy for a 20-minute refresher is wasted budget. Match the model's weight to the project's stakes.
- **Confusing the model with the deliverable.** The model organizes the work; it is not the course. A beautiful process diagram does not teach anyone anything.

---

## Why Comparing Models Matters

A designer who only knows ADDIE will treat every project the same way. They will write a long Analysis document for a two-week microlearning sprint. They will skip iterative prototyping for an unclear stakeholder. They will leave alignment between objectives and assessment to the end of Development, where it is most expensive to fix.

A designer who knows several models can match the model to the project's risk, audience, and uncertainty. That is the central professional skill of instructional design: **selecting and combining methods**, not loyally executing one.

## Key Takeaways

<concept-map concepts='["id_models","dick_and_carey_model","kemp_model","smith_and_ragan_model","successive_approximation_model","agile_id"]'></concept-map>

- ID process models are **lenses**, not recipes; each foregrounds a different concern.
- Two axes organize the field: **how much is specified up front** and **how change is treated**.
- **Dick and Carey** and **Smith and Ragan** maximize alignment and defensibility; **SAM** and **Agile/Lean** maximize iteration speed and learner signal; **Kemp** is the structured-but-non-linear middle.
- Choose the model that **attacks the project's dominant risk first** (accuracy, speed, ambiguity, or feasibility).
- Mature practice **combines** an alignment model with a delivery model, freezing the objective-and-assessment backbone before iteration begins.
