## Opening: The Course That Everybody Loved

A regional hospital rolled out a four-hour patient-safety course. The end-of-session surveys were glowing: 4.8 out of 5, comments praising the facilitator, near-perfect post-test scores. The training team celebrated and the budget was renewed.

Six months later, the infection-control office pulled hand-hygiene compliance logs from the wards. Compliance had not moved. The course that everybody loved had changed almost no behavior on the floor.

Nothing in that story is unusual. It is the single most common failure in instructional evaluation: collecting evidence that is easy to gather (smile sheets, quiz scores) while quietly assuming it stands in for evidence that is hard to gather (real behavior change, organizational results). Evaluation, done well, is the discipline that keeps a design team honest about the difference.

> **The Big Picture:** Evaluation is the empirical backbone of instructional design. It is the only way to show stakeholders that a training intervention actually closed the performance gap identified in analysis and justified the cost of design, development, delivery, and maintenance.

Evaluation is traditionally viewed as the "final" phase of the ADDIE model. However, strong instructional designers understand that evaluation must be continuous. If you only measure at the very end, you have no data to correct course during development, and no baseline against which to claim a change later.

### A Designer's First Reframe

Evaluation is not only about asking whether learners liked the course. It is about deciding:

- what counts as evidence,
- when evidence should be collected,
- how performance should be interpreted,
- and whether observed change can reasonably be attributed to the instruction.

That last point, attribution, is what separates a persuasive story from a defensible claim. That is why evaluation belongs in design conversations early, not just in end-of-course reporting.

## Formative vs. Summative Evaluation

- **Formative evaluation:** happens during design and development. It helps teams improve the instruction before full rollout.
- **Summative evaluation:** happens after implementation. It helps teams judge whether the instruction produced meaningful results.

A useful mnemonic from Robert Stake: when the cook tastes the soup, that is formative; when the guests taste the soup, that is summative.

| Dimension | Formative | Summative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Timing | During design and development | After implementation |
| Purpose | Improve the instruction | Judge the instruction |
| Audience | Design team, SMEs | Stakeholders, sponsors |
| Typical question | "What is confusing or broken?" | "Did it work, and was it worth it?" |
| Risk if skipped | Costly defects ship to learners | No evidence of value or impact |

Typical formative methods include usability tests, pilot runs, SME reviews, cognitive walkthroughs, and think-aloud sessions with representative learners.

Typical summative methods include pre and post assessments, transfer checks on the job, learner surveys, manager observations, and business-performance indicators.

## The Kirkpatrick Model and What Each Level Really Means

Kirkpatrick's Four Levels remain one of the most influential frameworks in instructional design because they help separate weak evidence from stronger evidence.

### Level 1: Reaction

This level asks whether learners found the experience engaging, relevant, and usable.

- useful signals: satisfaction, relevance, confidence, usability feedback
- common tools: surveys, ratings, comments, short reflections
- limitation: high enjoyment does not prove learning

### Level 2: Learning

This level asks whether learners actually acquired targeted knowledge, skills, or attitudes.

- useful signals: pre/post gains, scored demonstrations, rubric performance
- common tools: tests, scenarios, oral defenses, simulations
- limitation: strong performance in training does not guarantee transfer

### Level 3: Behavior

This level asks whether learners apply what they learned in real performance settings.

- useful signals: observed transfer, workflow changes, performance logs
- common tools: manager observation, peer review, delayed tasks, field audits
- limitation: workplace conditions often interfere with transfer

### Level 4: Results

This level asks whether behavior change affected meaningful organizational outcomes.

- useful signals: quality gains, fewer incidents, faster completion, reduced cost
- common tools: KPI dashboards, operational metrics, longitudinal comparison
- limitation: many outside variables may influence the result

This progression helps designers avoid overclaiming. A strong Level 1 result does not substitute for Level 3 evidence. A strong Level 2 result does not automatically imply Level 4 impact. Notice that effort and cost rise as you climb the levels, while the directness of business value also rises. Most organizations stop at Level 1 and 2 precisely because they are cheap, which is exactly why so many programs are renewed without proof that they changed anything that matters.

## A More Practical Selection Guide

When choosing evaluation methods, start with the decision the organization needs to make, not with the instrument you happen to have.

| If the team needs to... | Prioritize | Primary evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Improve the course before launch | Formative methods | Usability, think-alouds, SME review |
| Prove learning happened | Level 2 | Pre/post gains, scored demonstrations |
| Justify rollout or renewal | Levels 3 and 4 | Transfer observation, KPI shifts |
| Compare two versions | Baseline + comparison window | Controlled A/B with matched groups |

This framing helps the designer choose evidence that is decision-relevant rather than merely easy to collect. The guiding rule: the stronger the consequence of the decision, the stronger the evidence should be.

## Phillips ROI and the Isolation Problem

Jack Phillips added a fifth layer focused on return on investment. The logic is appealing: translate impact into money, compare it to program cost, and calculate ROI.

$$ ROI (\%) = \frac{\text{Net Program Benefits}}{\text{Program Costs}} \times 100 $$

where net program benefits equal monetized benefits minus program costs.

But this raises the central methodological problem in evaluation: **isolation**. If sales increase after sales training, what caused the increase: the training, a new marketing campaign, a seasonal trend, a change in incentives, or a weaker competitor?

Phillips' practical answer is the **isolation factor**: ask credible sources (managers, participants, analysts) to estimate what fraction of the improvement the training is responsible for, together with how confident they are. The raw benefit is then discounted before any ROI is reported.

$$ \text{Adjusted Benefits} = \text{Raw Benefits} \times (\text{Attribution \%}) \times (\text{Confidence \%}) $$

Drag the attribution and confidence factors and watch the defensible ROI separate from the naive headline. Notice how fast a plausible-sounding 200% headline collapses once you discount for what the training truly caused.

### Worked Example: ROI With and Without Isolation

**Problem.** A leadership program costs **\$60,000** to design and deliver. After rollout, the business attributes **\$180,000** in monetized improvement (faster project delivery, lower turnover) to the cohort. Managers estimate the program is responsible for about **70%** of that gain. Compute the naive ROI and the isolation-adjusted ROI.

**Step 1: Naive net benefit.**

$$ \text{Net} = \$180{,}000 - \$60{,}000 = \$120{,}000 $$

**Step 2: Naive ROI.**

$$ ROI = \frac{120{,}000}{60{,}000} \times 100 = \boxed{200\%} $$

This is the number an unrigorous report would headline: every dollar returned three.

**Step 3: Apply the isolation factor.** Only 70% of the \$180,000 is credibly the program's doing.

$$ \text{Adjusted Benefits} = \$180{,}000 \times 0.70 = \$126{,}000 $$

$$ \text{Adjusted Net} = \$126{,}000 - \$60{,}000 = \$66{,}000 $$

**Step 4: Adjusted ROI.**

$$ ROI_{adj} = \frac{66{,}000}{60{,}000} \times 100 = \boxed{110\%} $$

The program is still a good investment, but the defensible figure (110%) is nearly half the headline figure (200%). Reporting the 200% would not be a lie so much as a failure of attribution, and the first sharp CFO will dismantle it. Without control groups, baselines, delayed follow-up, triangulation across sources, and explicit acknowledgment of confounders, ROI claims sound persuasive but remain analytically weak.

## Advanced Evaluation: Item Response Theory

In digital assessment systems, percent-correct scoring is often not enough. Two learners can both score 7 out of 10 yet have very different ability if one got the hard items right and the other got the easy items right. **Item Response Theory (IRT)** improves measurement by modeling both learners and items on the same latent scale.

In a 2-parameter logistic (2PL) model, each question is described by:

- **Difficulty (b):** how much latent ability a learner needs to have a 50 percent chance of success
- **Discrimination (a):** how sharply the item separates lower-ability and higher-ability learners

$$ P(X_i = 1 \mid \theta, a_i, b_i) = \frac{e^{a_i(\theta - b_i)}}{1 + e^{a_i(\theta - b_i)}} $$

where $\theta$ is learner ability, $a_i$ is item discrimination, $b_i$ is item difficulty, and $P(X_i = 1)$ is the probability of a correct response.

Manipulate the 2PL item curve directly. Set a learner ability, then watch the probability of a correct response as difficulty and discrimination change. Push discrimination toward zero and notice how the probability crowds toward 0.5, which is exactly the signature of an item that cannot tell strong learners from weak ones.

<parameter-explorer config='{"title":"2PL item characteristic curve","sliders":[{"name":"theta","label":"Learner ability (theta)","min":-3,"max":3,"step":0.1,"unit":"","default":1},{"name":"a","label":"Discrimination (a)","min":0,"max":2.5,"step":0.1,"unit":"","default":1.2},{"name":"b","label":"Difficulty (b)","min":-3,"max":3,"step":0.1,"unit":"","default":0.5}],"outputs":[{"label":"P(correct)","unit":"","expr":"1 / (1 + 2.71828^(0 - a*(theta - b)))"}],"plot":{"x":"theta","output":0}}'></parameter-explorer> Ability and difficulty are on the same scale, so the quantity that drives the probability is the gap $(\theta - b_i)$: a learner well above an item's difficulty is likely to pass it; a learner well below is likely to miss it.

### Worked Example: Reading an Item With IRT

**Problem.** An item has discrimination $a = 1.2$ and difficulty $b = 0.5$. Find the probability of a correct response for a strong learner at $\theta = 1.0$ and a weaker learner at $\theta = 0.0$.

**Step 1: Strong learner, $\theta = 1.0$.** Compute the exponent.

$$ a(\theta - b) = 1.2 \,(1.0 - 0.5) = 0.6 $$

**Step 2: Apply the logistic function.** Using $e^{0.6} \approx 1.822$,

$$ P = \frac{1.822}{1 + 1.822} = \frac{1.822}{2.822} = \boxed{0.646} $$

**Step 3: Weaker learner, $\theta = 0.0$.**

$$ a(\theta - b) = 1.2\,(0.0 - 0.5) = -0.6, \qquad e^{-0.6} \approx 0.549 $$

$$ P = \frac{0.549}{1 + 0.549} = \boxed{0.354} $$

**Interpretation.** The same item gives a 64.6% chance of success to the stronger learner and only a 35.4% chance to the weaker one. That spread is what a healthy discrimination value buys you: the item meaningfully separates ability levels. If you reran this with a low discrimination such as $a = 0.2$, the two probabilities would crowd toward 0.5 and the item would barely tell strong and weak learners apart, which is a signal to revise or retire it.

IRT helps designers do more than score learners. It helps them inspect the **quality of the assessment itself**. A poorly written item may confuse experts, reward guessing, or depend on language complexity unrelated to the intended construct. If designers never examine item quality, they may misdiagnose learner performance when the actual problem lies in the assessment instrument.

## Common Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Why it happens | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Treating Level 1 as proof of impact | Smile sheets are cheap and flattering | Pre-commit to at least one Level 3 measure |
| Headline ROI with no isolation | The big number is persuasive | Apply attribution and confidence factors |
| Measuring only at the end | Evaluation framed as the last ADDIE phase | Build formative checkpoints into development |
| No baseline | Nobody captured "before" | Define baseline and comparison window in design |
| Scoring learners but never items | Percent-correct feels objective | Use IRT or item analysis to audit the instrument |

### Key Takeaways

- Evaluation is continuous, not a final step; formative work prevents costly defects and creates the baseline summative work depends on.
- Match the evidence to the decision; climb Kirkpatrick's levels as the stakes rise.
- A glowing Level 1 result tells you nothing reliable about Level 3 behavior or Level 4 results.
- ROI is only as honest as its isolation; always discount raw benefits for attribution and confidence.
- IRT evaluates the test as well as the test-taker; an item that fails to discriminate is an item to fix.

## A Checklist for Better Evaluation Design

Before finalizing an evaluation plan, ask:

- What decision will this evidence support?
- Which level of evidence is actually needed?
- What baseline exists for comparison?
- How will transfer or retention be checked later?
- What alternate explanations could distort the conclusion?

The stronger the consequence of the decision, the stronger the evidence should be.
