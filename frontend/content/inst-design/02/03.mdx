## Evaluating Impact and Psychometrics

Evaluation is traditionally viewed as the "final" phase of the ADDIE model. However, strong instructional designers understand that evaluation must be continuous. If you only measure at the very end, you have no data to correct course during development.

Evaluation is the empirical backbone of instructional design. It is the only way to show stakeholders that a training intervention actually addressed the performance gap identified in analysis and justified the cost of design, development, delivery, and maintenance.

### A Designer's First Reframe

Evaluation is not only about asking whether learners liked the course. It is about deciding:

- what counts as evidence,
- when evidence should be collected,
- how performance should be interpreted,
- and whether observed change can reasonably be attributed to the instruction.

That is why evaluation belongs in design conversations early, not just in end-of-course reporting.

### Formative vs. Summative Evaluation

- **Formative evaluation:** happens during design and development. It helps teams improve the instruction before full rollout.
- **Summative evaluation:** happens after implementation. It helps teams judge whether the instruction produced meaningful results.

Typical formative methods include:

- usability tests,
- pilot runs,
- SME reviews,
- cognitive walkthroughs,
- and think-aloud sessions with representative learners.

Typical summative methods include:

- pre and post assessments,
- transfer checks on the job,
- learner surveys,
- manager observations,
- and business-performance indicators.

## The Kirkpatrick Model and What Each Level Really Means

Kirkpatrick's Four Levels remain one of the most influential frameworks in instructional design because they help separate weak evidence from stronger evidence.

### Level 1: Reaction

This level asks whether learners found the experience engaging, relevant, and usable.

- useful signals: satisfaction, relevance, confidence, usability feedback
- common tools: surveys, ratings, comments, short reflections
- limitation: high enjoyment does not prove learning

### Level 2: Learning

This level asks whether learners actually acquired targeted knowledge, skills, or attitudes.

- useful signals: pre/post gains, scored demonstrations, rubric performance
- common tools: tests, scenarios, oral defenses, simulations
- limitation: strong performance in training does not guarantee transfer

### Level 3: Behavior

This level asks whether learners apply what they learned in real performance settings.

- useful signals: observed transfer, workflow changes, performance logs
- common tools: manager observation, peer review, delayed tasks, field audits
- limitation: workplace conditions often interfere with transfer

### Level 4: Results

This level asks whether behavior change affected meaningful organizational outcomes.

- useful signals: quality gains, fewer incidents, faster completion, reduced cost
- common tools: KPI dashboards, operational metrics, longitudinal comparison
- limitation: many outside variables may influence the result

This progression helps designers avoid overclaiming. A strong Level 1 result does not substitute for Level 3 evidence. A strong Level 2 result does not automatically imply Level 4 impact.

## A More Practical Selection Guide

When choosing evaluation methods, start with the decision the organization needs to make.

- If the team needs to improve the course before launch, prioritize formative evidence.
- If the team needs to prove learning happened, prioritize Level 2 measures.
- If the team needs to justify rollout or renewal, include Level 3 and Level 4 measures.
- If the team needs to compare versions, define a baseline and comparison window early.

This framing helps the designer choose evidence that is decision-relevant rather than merely easy to collect.

## Phillips ROI and the Isolation Problem

Jack Phillips added a fifth layer focused on return on investment. The logic is appealing: translate impact into money, compare it to program cost, and calculate ROI.

$$ ROI (\%) = \frac{\text{Net Program Benefits}}{\text{Program Costs}} \times 100 $$

But this raises the central methodological problem in evaluation: **isolation**.

If sales increase after sales training, what caused the increase?

- the training,
- a new marketing campaign,
- a seasonal trend,
- a change in incentives,
- or a weaker competitor?

This is why high-quality evaluation often needs:

- control or comparison groups,
- baseline measures,
- delayed follow-up,
- triangulation across multiple evidence sources,
- and explicit acknowledgment of confounding variables.

Without that rigor, ROI claims sound persuasive but remain analytically weak.

## Advanced Evaluation: Item Response Theory

In digital assessment systems, percent-correct scoring is often not enough. **Item Response Theory (IRT)** improves measurement by evaluating both learners and items.

In a 2-parameter logistic model, each question is described by:

- **Difficulty (b):** how much latent ability a learner needs to have a 50 percent chance of success
- **Discrimination (a):** how sharply the item separates lower-ability and higher-ability learners

$$ P(X_i = 1 | \theta, a_i, b_i) = \frac{e^{a_i(\theta - b_i)}}{1 + e^{a_i(\theta - b_i)}} $$

Where:

- $\theta$ represents learner ability,
- $a_i$ represents item discrimination,
- $b_i$ represents item difficulty,
- and $P(X_i = 1)$ is the probability of a correct response.

IRT helps designers do more than score learners. It helps them inspect the **quality of the assessment itself**.

For example, a poorly written item may:

- confuse experts,
- reward guessing,
- or depend on language complexity unrelated to the intended construct.

If designers never examine item quality, they may misdiagnose learner performance when the actual problem lies in the assessment instrument.

## A Checklist for Better Evaluation Design

Before finalizing an evaluation plan, ask:

- What decision will this evidence support?
- Which level of evidence is actually needed?
- What baseline exists for comparison?
- How will transfer or retention be checked later?
- What alternate explanations could distort the conclusion?

The stronger the consequence of the decision, the stronger the evidence should be.
