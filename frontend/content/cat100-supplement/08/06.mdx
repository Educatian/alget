## Core Concept

The core idea in this section is that **Revision Sprint** should be treated as a relationship among purpose, constraint, evidence, and revision. Purpose names what the learner or audience needs to accomplish. Constraint names what limits the decision. Evidence names the source that makes the decision defensible. Revision shows what changed after the evidence was considered.

Three course anchors shape the reasoning here:

- **NACE career readiness** helps you decide what counts as a meaningful learning or performance demand.
- **ISTE digital citizenship** helps you notice when a design or technology choice creates risk, burden, or unsupported assumptions.
- **data-ink and visual evidence** helps you check whether the artifact works for varied learners, audiences, or use contexts.

Read the anchors as competing checks on quality. One may highlight the learning demand, another may expose a risk, and another may help you test transfer.

## Worked Example: A 30-Minute Revision Sprint with a Changelog

A student has 30 minutes to revise a budget spreadsheet and its GitHub Pages page before submission. They first list every issue, then triage by impact, then log each fix with a timestamp.

**Triage list (highest impact first).**

| Priority | Issue | Type |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | `#REF!` in the Total cell B14 | Correctness |
| 2 | Dead link to the data source in `index.html` | Correctness |
| 3 | Default font, no heading styles | Cosmetic |
| 4 | "Rebuild layout with CSS grid" (AI suggestion) | Out of scope |

**Sprint changelog.**

```
14:05  Replaced #REF! in B14 with =SUM(B2:B13) = 8,420; checked against receipts tab. (Priority 1)
14:13  Fixed dead <a href> in index.html line 31 to the real CSV path; link now opens. (Priority 2)
14:22  Added <h2> styles and a readable font in styles.css. (Priority 3)
14:28  Logged "CSS grid rebuild" as a FUTURE item; declined for this sprint to avoid shipping unfinished. (Priority 4)
```

The sprint succeeds because correctness was fixed before cosmetics, and the changelog lets a reviewer trace every edit by time, location, and reason. The grid rebuild is recorded as future work rather than started with eight minutes left, which would have left the artifact broken.

## Decision Rule

Use the audience first, the evidence second, and the tool third. A digital product is justified only when another person can see what changed, why it changed, and what evidence supports the change.

A strong submission should survive three quick tests:

1. **Purpose check:** What should the audience be able to do after reading or using the artifact?
2. **Evidence check:** Which exact source, observation, annotation, rubric line, data pattern, or policy boundary justifies the revision?
3. **Revision check:** What changed in the artifact, and what suggestion did you reject or modify?

If you cannot answer all three questions, the artifact may be complete, but it is not yet ready as evidence of learning.

## Common Misreadings

Another misreading is to write about the tool instead of showing what the tool helped you revise.

In this course, quality means another reader can verify what changed and why.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Read this section as a small professional decision cycle, not as a list of terms. First, locate the work product: **privacy checklist for accounts, data sharing, and AI tool boundaries**. Then ask what problem the artifact is supposed to solve inside capstone transfer. A strong reader can explain the artifact in plain language before naming any theory: who will use it, what decision it supports, and what would count as evidence that it improved.

Next, connect the artifact to the course anchors: **NACE career readiness**, **ISTE digital citizenship**, **data-ink and visual evidence**. The point is not to cite every anchor. The point is to let one anchor change the artifact. If the anchor does not change wording, layout, evidence, timing, or feedback, it has not yet become design reasoning.

Finally, inspect the revision trail. A weak submission says the artifact was improved. A strong submission shows the earlier version, the issue noticed, the support requested, the decision accepted or rejected, and the reason the final version is better for the intended learner or audience.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: **claim** on the left, **evidence** on the right. The claim is the decision you think the section is teaching. The evidence is the exact phrase, example, annotation, support move, or artifact revision that makes the claim inspectable. By the end of CAT 100 08.06, your notes should make it possible to reconstruct your reasoning without asking you to explain it again.

A strong reader of Revision Sprint can answer four questions:

1. What is the real decision hidden inside this topic?
2. Which constraint makes the decision difficult?
3. What evidence would persuade a skeptical reviewer?
4. What would you revise if the audience, learner, classroom, or tool changed?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

For this section, the artifact is strong when a future employer, instructor, or peer can see the claim, the evidence, and the revision decision without guessing. Treat visual polish as the last layer. The earlier layers are purpose, constraint, evidence, and revision. ## Transfer Case

Use that change to test whether you understand Revision Sprint deeply enough to adapt it rather than repeat it.
