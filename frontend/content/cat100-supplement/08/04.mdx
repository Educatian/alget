## Why This Section Matters

AI tools will happily rewrite your resume bullet to sound impressive, and that is exactly the danger. A chatbot does not know what you actually did; it optimizes for words that sound strong. Left unchecked, it turns "tracked a small club budget" into "spearheaded financial operations," a phrase you cannot defend the moment an interviewer asks a follow-up question. The skill this section teaches is not avoiding AI, it is using it with a visible boundary: accept what is true, reject what inflates, and disclose what you did.

An AI use disclosure portfolio pairs each AI-assisted artifact with an honest note about what the tool proposed, what you kept, and what you refused and why. This is becoming a professional norm: employers and admissions offices increasingly ask how you used AI, and a clear disclosure signals integrity rather than concealment. The portfolio proves you can collaborate with a tool without letting it write claims you cannot stand behind.

> **What disclosure looks like in 2026.** The norm is concrete, not abstract. Many course syllabi now carry an explicit AI-use statement (UNC-Chapel Hill's College of Arts & Sciences mandates one on every undergraduate syllabus, and UCLA and the University of Minnesota published sample statements in 2025). And the emerging convention, matching APA practice of citing the tool name and access date, is to name the assistant by tool and version with a date, such as "Used ChatGPT (GPT-4o), May 2026," or to name Microsoft Copilot, Claude, or Gemini specifically, rather than writing the generic "an AI tool."

## Opening Case

Priya tracked her campus club's \$600 snack budget in a spreadsheet and caught a 12% overspend. Her first resume bullet reads, "Helped with the club budget." She asks an AI tool to make it stronger, and it returns, "Spearheaded financial operations and led analytics for a campus organization."

The new sentence is more impressive and less true. Priya did not lead operations or run analytics; she tracked a small budget with a `SUM` formula and flagged a problem. If she keeps the AI phrasing, the first sharp interview question collapses it. The disclosure portfolio is where she records that she took the verb upgrade, rejected the inflation, and kept the bullet defensible.

## Core Concept

AI use disclosure rests on a clear line between two kinds of help. Acceptable AI help improves how you express something you genuinely did: a stronger verb, tighter phrasing, a fixed grammar slip. Unacceptable AI help invents or inflates the underlying claim: a title you did not hold, a scope you did not own, a number you cannot verify. The disclosure note is the record that you policed that line.

A defensible resume bullet has three properties the AI cannot supply on its own:

- **Truthful.** Every claim matches what happened. "Tracked a \$600 budget" is true; "led financial operations" is not.
- **Role-fit.** The verb matches your actual scope. "Tracked" and "flagged" fit a member tracking a small budget; "spearheaded" implies authority you did not have.
- **Verifiable.** The number traces to a real artifact. The 12% overspend lives in the spreadsheet and can be reproduced with `=SUM(B2:B13)`.

The disclosure note then makes the collaboration transparent: it states which suggestion you accepted, which you rejected, and the reason. The reason is the important part. "Rejected because it overstates a \$600 task I cannot defend" shows judgment; "rejected because I did not like it" shows nothing. Disclosure is not an apology for using AI; it is evidence that you supervised it.

## Worked Example: A Truthful Before/After Resume Bullet with an AI-Use Note

A student tracked a campus club's \$600 snack budget in a spreadsheet and flagged a 12% overspend. They want one strong resume bullet and an honest AI-use note.

**Before (vague, written alone).**

> Helped with the club budget.

**AI proposal (one bounded critique).** The student prompts: "Make this resume bullet more specific and action-oriented." The AI returns:

> Spearheaded financial operations and led analytics for a campus organization.

**Decision (judged against the truth).** The role was tracking a small budget, not leading operations. The student rejects "spearheaded financial operations" as inflated and keeps the specific, true result.

**After (truthful, role-fit, defensible in an interview).**

> Tracked a \$600 club budget in a spreadsheet using `=SUM(B2:B13)`, flagged a 12% overspend, and proposed a cut that kept the term in budget.

**AI-use note (the disclosure).**

> Used ChatGPT (GPT-4o), May 2026, for one wording pass on this bullet. Accepted the suggestion to start with a strong verb. Rejected "spearheaded financial operations" and "led analytics" because they overstate a \$600 tracking task I cannot defend. Final numbers are mine and verifiable in the spreadsheet.

The portfolio does not hide the AI use; it shows the boundary. A reviewer can see exactly what was proposed, what was kept, and why one suggestion was refused.

## Decision Rule

Accept AI help with expression; reject AI help with facts. Before keeping any AI-suggested wording, run the interview test:

1. **Truth check.** Is every claim in the suggestion something that actually happened? If not, cut it regardless of how good it sounds.
2. **Defense check.** If an interviewer said "tell me more about that," could you, without backpedaling? "Spearheaded operations" fails because the next sentence is "well, it was a \$600 budget."
3. **Disclosure check.** Have you written down what you accepted, what you rejected, and why? If the boundary is not on paper, you have not really set one.

If a suggestion survives truth and defense, keep it and disclose it. If it fails either, reject it and record the reason.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is thinking disclosure means confessing wrongdoing. Using AI to sharpen a verb is normal and fine; the disclosure simply documents a healthy boundary. Hiding the use is what creates suspicion, not the use itself.

The second misreading is judging a bullet by how impressive it sounds instead of whether you can defend it. "Spearheaded financial operations" reads better than "tracked a \$600 budget" right up to the interview, where the inflated version becomes a liability and the truthful one becomes a story you can tell confidently.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Start with the truthful, unpolished version of what you did, in plain words: "tracked a small club budget and caught an overspend." Anchoring on the truth first means every later edit gets measured against reality instead of against ambition.

Now invite one bounded AI pass and read the result against the three properties. The verb upgrade ("tracked" stated cleanly, the result foregrounded) passes truth, role-fit, and verifiability, so keep it. The title inflation ("spearheaded financial operations") fails role-fit because you held no such role, so reject it. Pin the verifiable number to its source by naming the formula, `=SUM(B2:B13)`, so a reader knows the 12% is real.

Finally, write the disclosure note as a short paragraph: tool used, suggestion accepted, suggestion rejected, and the reason for the rejection, ending with "final numbers are mine and verifiable." The walkthrough output is a before bullet, an after bullet, and a disclosure note a reviewer can read as a single honest record.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, take one of your own real activities and run it through the same accept/reject pass, writing the disclosure note. A strong reader does not just nod at Priya's example; they can identify, in their own resume, where an AI suggestion would cross from expression into invention.

A strong reader of the AI use disclosure portfolio can answer four questions:

1. Which exact phrase did the AI propose, and was it true?
2. What did you accept, and does it pass the interview defense test?
3. What did you reject, and is the reason about truth and role-fit rather than taste?
4. Where does the verifiable number live, and can a reader reproduce it?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The portfolio is strong when each AI-assisted bullet is truthful, role-fit, and verifiable, and the disclosure note names a specific accepted and a specific rejected suggestion with reasons. The standard is not "I used AI responsibly" as a slogan; it is a paired before/after plus a note that shows the boundary in action.

A useful closing note would read: "Used ChatGPT (GPT-4o), May 2026, for one wording pass. I accepted its suggestion to lead with a strong verb because it states what I did more clearly. I rejected 'spearheaded financial operations' and 'led analytics' because they claim authority and scope I did not have on a \$600 tracking task and could not defend in an interview. The 12% overspend is reproducible in my spreadsheet with `=SUM(B2:B13)`."

## Transfer Case

Move the disclosure from a resume bullet to a different artifact: an AI-assisted cover letter, or AI-generated starter code in your portfolio site. The accept/reject logic holds, but the inflation looks different. In a cover letter, AI may assert enthusiasm or fit you have not earned; in code, it may add a feature you did not test or a comment claiming functionality that does not exist. Writing an accurate disclosure for code you only partly verified, including which lines you tested and which you cannot vouch for, is the test of whether you understand disclosure as ongoing supervision rather than a one-line resume footnote.
