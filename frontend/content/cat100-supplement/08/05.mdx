# Peer Showcase Feedback

![Peer Showcase Feedback textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat100-supplement/08-05-peer-showcase-feedback.png)

## Why This Section Matters

At a showcase you will collect a flood of feedback: peers, an AI reviewer, maybe an instructor, all pointing at your spreadsheet and your website. Most students do one of two unhelpful things with it. They accept every suggestion, including the wrong ones, or they nod politely and change nothing. Neither shows judgment. The skill this section builds is triaging feedback: deciding, item by item, whether to accept, modify, or reject a suggestion, and recording the reason.

An AI critique log is the artifact that captures that judgment. It is a table, one row per piece of feedback, that names the source, the suggestion, your decision, and the resulting edit or the reason for refusal. The log matters because feedback quality varies wildly: a peer might catch a real accessibility bug, while an AI reviewer might confidently suggest renaming a column in a way that makes the data wrong. A good log proves you can tell the difference and act accordingly.

## Learning Targets

- Analyze how the AI critique log showing accepted, modified, and rejected suggestions shows a defensible decision in CAT 100.

## Opening Case

At the peer showcase, Sam presents a budget spreadsheet and a small portfolio site and gathers four comments. A peer notices the contact form's email field has no label. An AI reviewer suggests renaming the "Net Revenue" column to "Profit." Another AI suggestion is to add a total row. A second peer just says, "Nice site overall."

Four comments, four very different qualities. One is a genuine accessibility fix, one would corrupt the meaning of the data, one is good but needs adjustment, and one is praise with no actionable target. If Sam treats them as a single undifferentiated to-do list, the artifact gets worse. The critique log is how Sam sorts them and shows the reasoning.

## Core Concept

A critique log records three possible decisions for every piece of feedback, and the decision you choose reveals your understanding of the artifact:

- **Accept** when the suggestion is correct as stated. You make the edit and record proof, such as the exact tag added and the re-test you ran.
- **Modify** when the suggestion identifies a real need but proposes the wrong fix. You implement the underlying intent differently, and you record why your version is better.
- **Reject** when the suggestion is wrong or harmful. You do not edit, and you record the reason, which is the most valuable entry in the log because refusing bad feedback for a stated reason is harder than accepting good feedback.

A fourth outcome is "not loggable": vague praise like "nice site" has no target, so it cannot become a revision. The honest move is to ask the reviewer to name a specific element.

The key idea is that feedback is input, not instruction. An AI reviewer suggesting "rename Net Revenue to Profit" sounds plausible, but if the column is revenue minus returns rather than revenue minus all costs, renaming it to Profit makes the spreadsheet lie. Accepting it would be polish that damages truth. The log forces you to judge each suggestion against what the artifact actually means, which is the difference between editing and just complying.

## Worked Example: A Critique Log with Accept, Modify, and Reject

At the peer showcase, a student presents a budget spreadsheet and a small portfolio site. They collect four suggestions, two from peers and two from an AI reviewer, and log a decision for each.

| # | Source | Suggestion | Decision | Reason and resulting edit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Peer | "The contact form email field has no label." | Accepted | Added `<label for="email">Email</label>` in `contact.html` line 24; re-tested with a screen reader |
| 2 | AI | "Rename the column 'Net Revenue' to 'Profit'." | Rejected | The column is revenue minus returns, not minus costs; renaming it would mislabel the data. Kept 'Net Revenue' |
| 3 | AI | "Add a total row to the budget table." | Modified | Added the total with `=SUM(B2:B13)` but placed it at the top so it stays visible while scrolling |
| 4 | Peer | "Nice site overall." | Not loggable | Praise with no target; asked the peer to name a specific element instead |

The log is strong because three of four rows show judgment: one accepted with proof, one rejected with a reason, one modified to fit a constraint. Row 4 shows that vague praise cannot become a revision. A reviewer can see not just that feedback arrived, but exactly what the student did with it and why.

## Decision Rule

Judge each suggestion against what the artifact means, then accept, modify, or reject with a recorded reason. For every item:

1. **Correctness first.** Does the suggestion make the artifact more true, more usable, or more accessible? The missing email label fails accessibility, so the fix is an accept.
2. **Meaning guard.** Would the change misrepresent the data or claim? Renaming Net Revenue to Profit would, so it is a reject even though it sounds tidier.
3. **Intent rescue.** If the suggestion points at a real need but the fix is off, modify it: add the total row, but place it where it stays visible.

If a comment has no target, it is not loggable; ask for specifics rather than inventing an edit.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is treating an AI reviewer as more authoritative than a peer. The AI suggestion to rename a column was the wrong one in the example, while the peer caught a real bug. Source does not determine quality; the content of the suggestion does, judged against the artifact.

The second misreading is logging only the suggestions you accepted. A log that shows three accepts and hides the rejects looks compliant but proves nothing about judgment. The rejected and modified rows, with their reasons, are what demonstrate you evaluated feedback rather than absorbed it.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Collect every suggestion verbatim before deciding anything, so you are reacting to what was actually said rather than your memory of it. Write each into its own row with its source named, because you will later want to see whether you over-trusted one source.

Now decide each row against meaning, not tone. For the email-label comment, confirm the bug is real (the field has no `<label>`), make the fix, and record the exact tag and the screen-reader re-test as proof. For the column-rename suggestion, check what the column actually computes; because Net Revenue is revenue minus returns, renaming it to Profit would mislabel it, so you reject and say why. For the total-row idea, keep the intent but improve the placement, and note the change.

Finally, handle the praise honestly: "nice site" has no target, so mark it not loggable and ask the peer to name a specific element. The walkthrough output is a four-decision table where each row shows source, suggestion, decision, and a reason a reviewer can audit.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, invent one new suggestion for each category, an accept, a modify, and a reject, for an artifact of your own. A strong reader does not just recognize Sam's four rows; they can generate a plausible bad suggestion and explain why they would refuse it.

A strong reader of peer showcase feedback can answer four questions:

1. For each suggestion, is the decision accept, modify, or reject, and why?
2. Where a suggestion was rejected, what about the artifact's meaning made it wrong?
3. Where a suggestion was modified, how does your version serve the intent better?
4. Which comment was not loggable, and what did you ask for instead?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The log is strong when it contains at least one rejected and one modified row with reasons, not only accepts, and when each accept records a concrete edit and re-test. The standard is visible judgment: a reviewer should see you weighing suggestions against the artifact, not just collecting checkmarks.

A useful closing note would read: "I accepted the missing-label fix and verified it with a screen reader. I rejected the Net Revenue to Profit rename because the column excludes returns, not all costs, so the new name would mislabel the data. I modified the total-row suggestion by anchoring the total at the top so it stays visible. I asked the peer who said 'nice site' to point at one specific element so the comment could become a real edit."

## Transfer Case

Carry the log into a workplace code review, where a senior colleague and an AI assistant both comment on a script you wrote. The categories transfer directly, but the stakes rise: rejecting a senior's suggestion now requires a clear, respectful reason, and accepting an AI fix without testing it could ship a bug. Writing a reject row that disagrees with a person whose authority exceeds yours, grounded in what the code actually does, is the test of whether you can run a critique log when it is socially uncomfortable rather than only in a friendly classroom.
