# Professional Story Across Artifacts

![Professional Story Across Artifacts textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat100-supplement/08-02-professional-story-across-artifacts.png)

## Why This Section Matters

By the end of CAT 100 you have a pile of files: a spreadsheet, a website, a resume, maybe an AI critique log. A pile is not a portfolio. A hiring manager who clicks through three disconnected files learns that you can use three tools, but not that you can solve a problem they care about. The difference between "I made things" and "I delivered value" is a story, and the storyboard is where you build it.

A presentation storyboard is the plan, slide by slide, before you ever open PowerPoint. It forces you to decide the order in which a specific audience meets your evidence, and which artifact answers which question. The constraint that drives every choice is the audience: a marketing recruiter and a data-analytics recruiter will want the same three artifacts arranged in completely different orders. Learning to storyboard around an audience is the skill that turns scattered coursework into a single, persuasive claim.

## Opening Case

Devon has three strong artifacts and a showcase deck that simply names them: slide one is "My Excel project," slide two is "My website," slide three is "My resume." During the dry run, a mock hiring manager asks, "So what did you actually do for me?" Devon has no answer, because the deck is a table of contents, not an argument.

The artifacts did not get worse; the framing did. Each slide describes a file instead of advancing a claim the manager cares about. The storyboard is the fix: it starts from the manager's opening question and arranges the same three artifacts so each one supplies a piece of the answer.

## Core Concept

A storyboard is an ordered sequence of slides, planned before design, where each slide carries one claim and the slide-level evidence that proves it. Two ideas make it work.

The first is the audience constraint. You cannot order slides until you know who is watching and what they are trying to decide. A marketing recruiter is asking "can this person turn data into a decision and communicate it?" A data-analytics recruiter is asking "can this person handle real data correctly?" The same Excel model leads the marketing deck as a business outcome ("I cut paid-channel spend 18%") and leads the analytics deck as a method ("I used `SUMIFS` to segment 512 records"). Same artifact, different headline, because the audience changed.

The second is slide-level evidence: every claim slide must sit next to something inspectable, not an adjective. "I am detail-oriented" is an adjective. "Pivot table reducing 512 rows to a Region by Month grid" is evidence. The storyboard rule is one claim, one piece of evidence, one slide. This connects to the data-ink principle (Tufte's data-ink ratio: maximize the ink that shows the data, erase the rest; The Visual Display of Quantitative Information): a slide that is mostly decoration buries the one number that matters, so the storyboard plans the evidence first and the styling last.

A storyboard also makes ordering deliberate. Summary artifacts like a resume belong last, because they recap proof the audience has already seen. Leading with the resume asks the audience to trust a list; leading with the artifacts lets them verify, then accept the summary.

## Worked Example: Turning a File List into a Storyboard

A student has three capstone artifacts: an Excel budget model, a GitHub Pages portfolio site, and a one-page resume. The audience for the showcase is a marketing-internship hiring manager.

**Before (a file dump).** Three slides, each just naming a file:

| Slide | Title | Body |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | My Excel project | "Spreadsheet with formulas" |
| 2 | My website | "HTML site on GitHub Pages" |
| 3 | My resume | "Updated resume" |

The hiring manager asks, "So what did you actually do for me?" The deck cannot answer, because it lists files instead of telling a story.

**After (an audience-led storyboard).** Same three artifacts, reordered around one question:

| Slide | Title | Slide-level evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | "Can this candidate turn data into a decision?" | The opening question the manager cares about |
| 2 | "I cut paid-channel spend 18%" | Pivot table with `=SUMIFS(Spend,Channel,"Paid")` = 12,400 highlighted |
| 3 | "And I can ship it publicly" | Live GitHub Pages link plus the `index.html` heading outline |
| 4 | "Here is the one-page summary" | Resume line: "Reduced paid-channel spend 18% with a pivot-table model" |

The resume slide moves last because it summarizes proof the manager already saw. Each claim now sits next to a visible artifact, not an adjective. That ordering is the storytelling decision a reviewer can inspect.

## Decision Rule

Storyboard from the audience's opening question backward to your artifacts, not from your file list forward. For each slide, apply three tests:

1. **Claim test.** Does the slide title state something the audience cares about, or does it just name a file? "I cut spend 18%" passes; "My Excel project" fails.
2. **Evidence test.** Is there one inspectable artifact on the slide that proves the claim, with the specific formula, number, or link visible?
3. **Order test.** Does each slide build on the last toward the audience's decision, with summaries (the resume) placed after the proof they summarize?

If a slide fails any test, it is decoration, not argument, and it should be cut or rewritten.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is thinking a storyboard is about visual themes and fonts. Those come last. A storyboard is about the order of claims and the evidence behind them; you can build a strong one on index cards before opening any software.

The second misreading is believing one deck fits every audience. Reusing your marketing storyboard verbatim for an analytics interview tells the analytics recruiter you did not think about them. The artifacts stay; the headlines and order must be re-derived from the new audience's question.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Start by writing the audience's opening question at the top of a blank page. For Devon's marketing recruiter it is "Can this person turn data into a decision?" Everything downstream answers that one question.

Next, list your artifacts and assign each one a claim that advances the answer. The Excel model becomes "I cut paid-channel spend 18%," not "I know pivot tables." The website becomes "I can ship work publicly," shown by a live link. Pin the exact evidence to each claim: the `SUMIFS` result, the rendered page, the resume bullet that recaps the number.

Then order the slides so proof precedes summary. Place the resume last because it restates results the audience has already verified. Only after the sequence is locked do you add styling, and you add it sparingly so the one number per slide stays the focal point. The walkthrough output is a slide table like the worked example, where a reviewer can read the argument from titles alone.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, sketch the same three artifacts arranged for a different audience than the one in the example. A strong reader does not memorize Devon's marketing deck; they can re-order the same files into an analytics deck where slide two leads with method instead of outcome.

A strong reader of professional story across artifacts can answer four questions:

1. What is the audience's opening question, and does slide one name it?
2. What single claim does each artifact carry, and what evidence proves it on the slide?
3. Why are the slides in this order, and which slide summarizes the others?
4. How would the headlines change if the audience changed from one role to another?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The storyboard is strong when a reviewer can read only the slide titles and reconstruct the argument, and when each title is backed by visible evidence rather than an adjective. The standard is a slide table with a stated audience, one claim per slide, the exact evidence pinned to each claim, and a deliberate order that ends on the summary.

A useful closing note would read: "Audience is a marketing-internship recruiter asking whether I turn data into decisions. I led with the 18% spend cut (proved by the `SUMIFS` pivot), followed with the public site as proof I can ship, and placed the resume last because it recaps numbers already shown. I rejected the resume-first slide order suggested by Copilot in PowerPoint because it would ask the recruiter to trust a list before seeing proof."

Ground the AI move in whatever current tool you actually used: as of January 2026, Copilot in PowerPoint generates an editable outline you can refine before it builds the slides, and Gamma drafts a roughly ten-slide deck with its own narrative arc from a single prompt; either will happily propose a default ordering you must judge against the audience question. Tool names and features change fast (Copilot added Agent Mode and Claude-powered agents in January 2026); the disclosure habit does not.

## Transfer Case

Keep the three artifacts but switch the audience to a transfer-admissions committee deciding whether you are ready for upper-division coursework. The committee's question is not "can you do the job" but "can you learn and document rigorously." The Excel slide now leads with the method and the correctness check, the website becomes evidence of self-directed publishing, and the resume becomes secondary. Re-deriving the order from the new question, rather than reshipping the marketing deck, is the test of whether you understand storyboarding as audience reasoning instead of a fixed template.
