# AI-Assisted Email Drafting

![AI-Assisted Email Drafting textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat100-supplement/07-04-ai-assisted-email-drafting.png)

## Why This Section Matters

An AI writing tool will happily produce a fluent, confident email that names the wrong professor, invents a deadline, or quietly drops the one request you actually needed to make. The fluency is the trap: a polished draft feels finished even when it is factually wrong. This section teaches you to use AI as a drafting accelerator while keeping yourself as the verifier, because in professional and academic email the cost of a confident error (a wrong name in the greeting, a fabricated date) is borne by you, not the tool.

The core competency is bounded delegation: giving the AI a specific enough prompt to produce something usable, then running a verification pass on every fact before the message leaves your outbox, and never feeding the tool private data it does not need.

## Learning Targets

- Write a specific email prompt that names the recipient, course, exact request, deadline, and reason.
- Run a verification pass on every fact (names, dates, the actual ask) before sending an AI-drafted message.
- Keep private data (student ID, grades, personal details) out of public AI tools.
- Keep a short log of which AI suggestions you accepted, modified, or rejected, and why.

## Opening Case

A student types "write an email to my professor" into an AI tool and receives a grammatically perfect message addressed to "Professor [Name]" requesting "the assignment extension we discussed." There was no discussion, the professor's name is missing, and there is no course or deadline. The draft is unusable not because the writing is bad but because the prompt gave the tool nothing real to work with, so it filled the gaps with plausible-sounding placeholders.

The fix runs in both directions: a specific prompt produces a usable draft, and a verification pass catches the inevitable remaining errors before they reach the recipient.

## Core Concept

AI-assisted email rests on three moves: specify, verify, and bound.

**Specify.** An AI tool produces output proportional to the constraints you give it. A prompt with the recipient, course, exact request, deadline, and reason yields a sendable draft; a vague prompt yields generic filler with placeholders. The prompt is where you do the thinking; the tool handles phrasing.

**Verify.** AI tools generate text that is statistically likely, not necessarily true. They will confidently produce a name or date that sounds right but is wrong. So every factual element (the greeting name, the deadline, the course number, the specific ask) gets checked against a real source before sending. Fluency is not evidence of accuracy.

**Bound.** Public AI tools may store or train on what you type. Identifiers and sensitive details (your student ID, grades, health specifics) should never go into the prompt. You add such items by hand, privately, only if the recipient actually needs them. The boundary is decided by the data's sensitivity, not by convenience.

The distinction students miss is between **polish** (smooth wording, which AI is good at) and **correctness plus appropriateness** (right facts, right boundary, right ask), which remain your responsibility.

## Worked Example

Here is a real email request taken from a vague AI prompt to a sendable draft.

**Step 1, a weak prompt:**

```
Write an email to my professor
```

The AI returns a generic email with no course, no request, and a placeholder name. You cannot send it.

**Step 2, a specific prompt:**

```
Write a short, polite email to Prof. Lee for CAT 100 requesting a 2-day
extension on the Module 7 lab, due Friday, because I was ill. Keep my
student ID out of the message; I will add it later.
```

**Step 3, the draft, then your verification pass before sending:**

```
Subject: CAT 100 Module 7 Lab -- Extension Request

Dear Prof. Lee,                          [check: real name is Lee, correct]
I am a student in your CAT 100 section. I was ill this week and
would like to request a two-day extension on the Module 7 lab,
currently due Friday.                    [check: deadline Friday, correct]
Thank you for considering this.
Best,
[Your name]                              [fill in; ID added only if she asks, privately]
```

Three decisions did the work:

1. **Specifics in the prompt** (recipient, course, exact ask, deadline, reason) so the draft is usable.
2. **A verification pass** confirming the name and date are real before the email leaves your outbox.
3. **Privacy boundary**: the student ID never went into the public AI tool; it is added by hand only if needed.

## Decision Rule

Treat the AI as a drafter and yourself as the editor of record. Before prompting, decide what facts the tool needs and what sensitive data it must not see. After drafting, verify every fact before you click send.

A sendable AI-drafted email passes three checks:

1. **Specificity check:** Did the prompt include the recipient, course, exact request, deadline, and reason?
2. **Verification check:** Has every name, date, and claim in the draft been confirmed against a real source?
3. **Boundary check:** Is all private or identifying data kept out of the AI tool and added by hand only where needed?

If any fact is unverified, the email is a draft, not a message ready to send.

## Common Misreadings

The most dangerous misreading is trusting fluency. A smooth, confident draft can still name the wrong instructor or invent a date; smoothness is not accuracy. Always verify the facts.

A second misreading is over-sharing in the prompt. Pasting your full record "so the AI has context" hands sensitive data to a tool that may retain it. Give the tool only what the email itself requires.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply this to a recommendation-letter request, which is higher-stakes than an extension. You prompt: "Write a polite email to Dr. Ramirez asking if she would write a recommendation letter for the summer research program, due June 15; remind her I took her CAT 100 in spring and earned an A; offer to send my resume." The tool drafts a courteous message.

Now verify: is the professor's name spelled Ramirez? Is the deadline really June 15? Did you actually take her course in spring? Each is a fact the tool will assert confidently whether or not it is true, so each is checked against your records and the program page. The boundary move: you do not paste your transcript into the tool; you simply reference the grade in the prompt and attach the resume yourself. The walkthrough shows that the higher the stakes, the more the verification pass matters, because a wrong name in a recommendation request is a self-inflicted wound.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

A strong reader reads an AI draft suspiciously, looking specifically for the confident facts that have not been checked. The smoother the draft, the more deliberately they verify, because polish hides errors well.

A strong reader of an AI-drafted email can answer four questions:

1. Which facts in this draft did the tool generate, and which have I confirmed against a real source?
2. What did the prompt include or omit that shaped the draft's quality?
3. What private data did I keep out of the tool, and how does the recipient still get what they need?
4. If a fact were wrong, who pays the cost, and have I checked that fact?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The artifact is the email plus a short critique log marking each AI suggestion as accepted, modified, or rejected, with the reason. It is strong when a reader can see not just the final message but the judgment behind it: which facts were verified and which suggestions were turned down.

A useful log entry sounds like this: "Accepted the AI's subject line; modified the greeting after confirming the professor goes by Dr. not Prof.; rejected the AI's offer to 'attach my full transcript' because the request does not need it and the tool should not see grade data."

## Transfer Case

Move AI drafting from email to a cover letter for a job application. Specify, verify, and bound all transfer, but the boundary tightens: a cover letter may tempt you to paste the full job posting and your resume into the tool. The adaptation is to check the employer's and the AI tool's policies, since some application systems forbid AI-generated text and some AI tools retain inputs. The three moves stay; what adapts is the stricter privacy and policy environment around hiring.
