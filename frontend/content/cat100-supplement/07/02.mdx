## Core Concept

The core idea in this section is that **Calendar and Task Systems** should be treated as a relationship among purpose, constraint, evidence, and revision. Purpose names what the learner or audience needs to accomplish. Constraint names what limits the decision. Evidence names the source that makes the decision defensible. Revision shows what changed after the evidence was considered.

Three course anchors shape the reasoning here:

- **NACE career readiness** helps you decide what counts as a meaningful learning or performance demand.
- **ISTE digital citizenship** helps you notice when a design or technology choice creates risk, burden, or unsupported assumptions.
- **data-ink and visual evidence** helps you check whether the artifact works for varied learners, audiences, or use contexts.

Use these anchors as lenses, not as names to drop into a reflection. A useful lens changes what you notice, what you question, and what you revise.

## Worked Example

Here is one week's CAT 100 load turned from a vague list into a working calendar plus task split.

**Raw list (no dates, no times):**

```
- read chapter 7
- write lab report
- email advisor
- group project due
```

**After splitting into calendar events and dated tasks:**

```
CALENDAR (fixed times)
  Tue 10:00-10:50 AM   CAT100 lecture   (reminder 9:40 AM, covers 15 min walk + 5 min setup)
  Wed 6:00-8:00 PM     Work block: lab report draft
  Thu 4:00-5:30 PM     Work block: group project section

TASK LIST (due dates + done state)
  [ ] read chapter 7        due Tue
  [ ] submit lab report     due Wed 11:59 PM
  [ ] email advisor         due Thu
  [ ] group project upload  due Fri 11:59 PM
```

Three decisions did the work:

1. **Events versus tasks.** Lecture is a fixed time, so it is a calendar event. "Submit lab report" is work with a due date, so it is a task.
2. **Work blocks before deadlines.** The 6 to 8 PM Wednesday block is when the lab report actually gets written; the 11:59 PM due time is only when it is handed in.
3. **Reminder lead time.** The 9:40 AM alert leaves 20 minutes for the 15 minute walk plus 5 minute setup, instead of a useless "class now" ping.

## Decision Rule

Use the audience first, the evidence second, and the tool third. A digital product is justified only when another person can see what changed, why it changed, and what evidence supports the change.

Before submitting the privacy checklist for accounts, data sharing, and AI tool boundaries, slow down and check whether the trace can answer three questions:

1. **Purpose check:** What should the audience be able to do after reading or using the artifact?
2. **Evidence check:** Which exact source, observation, annotation, rubric line, data pattern, or policy boundary justifies the revision?
3. **Revision check:** What changed in the artifact, and what suggestion did you reject or modify?

If you cannot answer all three questions, the artifact may be complete, but it is not yet ready as evidence of learning.

## Common Misreadings

A common mistake is to treat digital fluency as finishing the file. A finished file can still hide weak evidence or unclear audience judgment.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Read this section as a small professional decision cycle, not as a list of terms. First, locate the work product: **privacy checklist for accounts, data sharing, and AI tool boundaries**. Then ask what problem the artifact is supposed to solve inside workflow and collaboration. A strong reader can explain the artifact in plain language before naming any theory: who will use it, what decision it supports, and what would count as evidence that it improved.

Next, connect the artifact to the course anchors: **NACE career readiness**, **ISTE digital citizenship**, **data-ink and visual evidence**. The point is not to cite every anchor. The point is to let one anchor change the artifact. If the anchor does not change wording, layout, evidence, timing, or feedback, it has not yet become design reasoning.

Finally, inspect the revision trail. A weak submission says the artifact was improved. A strong submission shows the earlier version, the issue noticed, the support requested, the decision accepted or rejected, and the reason the final version is better for the intended learner or audience.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: **claim** on the left, **evidence** on the right. The claim is the decision you think the section is teaching. The evidence is the exact phrase, example, annotation, support move, or artifact revision that makes the claim inspectable. By the end of CAT 100 07.02, your notes should make it possible to reconstruct your reasoning without asking you to explain it again.

A strong reader of Calendar and Task Systems can answer four questions:

1. What is the real decision hidden inside this topic?
2. Which constraint makes the decision difficult?
3. What evidence would persuade a skeptical reviewer?
4. What would you revise if the audience, learner, classroom, or tool changed?
