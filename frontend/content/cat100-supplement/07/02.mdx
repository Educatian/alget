## Why This Section Matters

A common pattern behind missed deadlines is not forgetting a date but confusing a due time with a work time. A syllabus says "lab report due Friday 11:59 PM," and the student writes "Friday" on a list, then opens the assignment Friday at 9 PM and discovers it needs four hours. The due date was never the problem; the missing reservation of time to do the work was. This section teaches the single most important distinction in personal scheduling: the difference between a calendar (fixed-time commitments) and a task list (things with a deadline that still need a work block carved out for them).

Getting this right turns a vague pile of obligations into a week you can actually execute. It is also the backbone for everything else in the chapter: templates, automation, and audits all assume you know when work happens, not just when it is due.

## Learning Targets

- Separate fixed-time events (which belong on a calendar) from deadline-bound work (which belongs on a task list).
- Schedule a work block for each task before its deadline, so the work has a reserved time and not just a due time.
- Set reminder lead times that account for travel and setup, not just the moment the event starts.
- Track task state (not started, in progress, done) so the list shows what is left, not just what exists.

## Opening Case

A student keeps everything in one notes app: "read chapter 7, write lab report, email advisor, group project due." Every item looks equal and none has a time. Tuesday's lecture (a fixed commitment) sits next to "write lab report" (hours of work) as if they were the same kind of thing. The result is a list that tells you what exists but never tells you what to do next, or when.

The fix is to sort each item by one question: is this a fixed time someone else set, or is this work I must find time for? That sort is the whole skill, and it determines which tool the item belongs in.

## Core Concept

A **calendar** holds events: things that happen at a fixed time you do not control, like a lecture, a shift, or a meeting. A **task list** holds work: things with a deadline that you must complete, where you choose when to actually do them. The classic mistake is putting a task ("submit lab report") on the calendar at its due time, which reserves the wrong moment, the instant it is due rather than the hours it takes to produce.

Three rules make the system work:

- **Events go on the calendar; tasks go on the list.** A lecture is an event. "Write the lab report" is a task; its deadline is a property of the task, not a calendar event.
- **Every task earns a work block.** Before a task's deadline, you place an event on the calendar that is the time you will do it. The deadline (Friday 11:59 PM) and the work block (Wednesday 6 to 8 PM) are different entries.
- **Reminders include lead time.** A reminder should fire early enough to act on, accounting for travel and setup, not at the start time when it is already too late.

The underlying logic is that time is a finite resource you allocate in advance. A due date is a constraint; a work block is the allocation that satisfies it.

Current tools implement this split directly: Google Calendar or the Outlook calendar holds events, while Microsoft To Do, Google Tasks, or Todoist holds the task list. AI features now propose work blocks for you; as of 2026, Copilot in Outlook can block focus time and resolve calendar conflicts, and Gemini in Google Calendar can find free slots and create events. The concept stays primary: you still verify and accept (or modify, or reject) the AI's block placement, and tool interfaces and AI features churn, so check what your tool currently does.

## Worked Example

Here is one week's CAT 100 load turned from a vague list into a working calendar plus task split.

**Raw list (no dates, no times):**

```
- read chapter 7
- write lab report
- email advisor
- group project due
```

**After splitting into calendar events and dated tasks:**

```
CALENDAR (fixed times)
  Tue 10:00-10:50 AM   CAT100 lecture   (reminder 9:40 AM, covers 15 min walk + 5 min setup)
  Wed 6:00-8:00 PM     Work block: lab report draft
  Thu 4:00-5:30 PM     Work block: group project section

TASK LIST (due dates + done state)
  [ ] read chapter 7        due Tue
  [ ] submit lab report     due Wed 11:59 PM
  [ ] email advisor         due Thu
  [ ] group project upload  due Fri 11:59 PM
```

Three decisions did the work:

1. **Events versus tasks.** Lecture is a fixed time, so it is a calendar event. "Submit lab report" is work with a due date, so it is a task.
2. **Work blocks before deadlines.** The 6 to 8 PM Wednesday block is when the lab report actually gets written; the 11:59 PM due time is only when it is handed in.
3. **Reminder lead time.** The 9:40 AM alert leaves 20 minutes for the 15 minute walk plus 5 minute setup, instead of a useless "class now" ping.

## Decision Rule

For every obligation, ask in order: is it a fixed time (calendar) or work with a deadline (task)? If it is work, where is the work block that will produce it? Does its reminder leave enough lead time to act?

A week is well-planned when it survives three checks:

1. **Sort check:** Is every item correctly placed as either a fixed-time event or a deadline-bound task?
2. **Allocation check:** Does every task have a work block reserved on the calendar before its deadline?
3. **Lead-time check:** Does each reminder fire early enough to cover travel and setup, not at the start?

If a task has a deadline but no work block, it is a wish, not a plan.

## Common Misreadings

The biggest misreading is "I put the deadline on my calendar, so I am organized." Putting the due time on the calendar reserves the moment work is due, not the time to do it; the work still has no home. Add a separate work block.

A second misreading is treating reminders as alarms that fire at the start time. A reminder at the moment class begins is useless if you are across campus. Lead time is the point of a reminder.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the system to a real conflict. You have a lecture Tuesday at 10 AM, a lab report due Wednesday at 11:59 PM, and a part-time shift Wednesday 1 to 5 PM. Sort first: lecture and shift are fixed-time events; the lab report is a task. Now allocate: the shift eats Wednesday afternoon, so the only viable work block is Wednesday 6 to 8 PM (after the shift, before the deadline). Place it on the calendar.

The walkthrough exposes a hidden risk: if you had only written "lab due Wed," you would not have noticed that the shift consumed your obvious work time until Wednesday evening. By allocating the work block in advance, you discovered the squeeze on Sunday, when you could still adjust (start a day earlier or trade the shift). The system's value is catching the collision before it becomes a missed deadline. The work-block move is also one of the best-evidenced habits in the goal literature: forming a specific if-then plan for when and where you will act (an "implementation intention") reliably raises goal attainment, with a medium-to-large effect across 94 studies (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006).

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

A strong reader looks at a week and can tell instantly whether it is executable, not just populated. A calendar full of deadlines with no work blocks is a warning sign, not a plan. The reader asks where the actual hours of work will come from.

A strong reader of a calendar and task system can answer four questions:

1. For each deadline this week, when is the work block that will satisfy it?
2. Which two commitments are competing for the same hours, and how is that resolved?
3. Does each reminder give enough lead time to actually arrive or start on time?
4. What does the task list say is left to do, as opposed to what merely exists?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The artifact is a one-week plan that separates calendar from tasks and shows a work block for each deadline. It is strong when a reader can trace every deadline to a reserved block of time and see that no two commitments silently overlap.

A useful note sounds like this: "I moved 'submit lab report' off my calendar and made it a task with a Wednesday 11:59 PM deadline, then added a separate Wednesday 6 to 8 PM work block. I rejected scheduling the work block Friday afternoon because that left zero margin if the report ran long; an earlier block gives me a recovery day."

## Transfer Case

Move the system into a job with on-call hours. The fixed-event versus task split still holds, but a new constraint appears: some events are unpredictable (an on-call page can land any time). The adaptation is to leave deliberate buffer blocks rather than filling every hour, so an interruption does not topple the whole week. The core distinction transfers unchanged; what adapts is how much of the calendar you leave unreserved to absorb shocks.
