## Why This Section Matters

Every page you will publish in this module begins with two questions that most beginners skip: who is going to look at this, and what do I want them to do next? Information architecture (often shortened to IA) is the answer to the second question made visible as structure. It is the decision about which pages exist, what each page is named, and how a visitor moves between them. A portfolio with five well-chosen pages and clear navigation does its job; a portfolio with eleven pages, three of them empty, sends the visitor into a maze and quietly undermines the work it was meant to showcase.

This section comes first because the file tree you build in 06.03 and the HTML you write in 06.02 are downstream of the IA decision. If you write markup before you decide what the site is for, you end up styling pages no one needs. Get the architecture right and the rest of the module is mostly execution.

## Learning Targets

- Write a one-sentence purpose statement ([audience] + [single job]) and use it to rank candidate pages.
- Apply the task, reach, and empty checks to keep, promote, or cut each candidate page within the click budget.
- Document a bounded AI/software support move with accepted and rejected suggestions.
- Use feedback, support requests, and revision notes to improve the artifact before sharing it.

## Opening Case

A first-year student opens her portfolio repo and starts adding pages the way she would add tabs to a notebook: Home, About, Projects, Resume, Contact, Blog, Hobbies, Photos, Favorite Links, Guestbook, Now Playing. Eleven files. When a peer reviewer visits, he clicks Blog (empty), then Photos (one selfie), then gives up before finding the one dashboard project that actually demonstrates her skill. The site is not broken; it is undifferentiated. Nothing tells the visitor where the evidence is.

The problem is not effort, it is the absence of a purpose statement that could have ranked the pages. This section gives you the tool to make that ranking before you write a single line of HTML, so that every page you keep earns its place against a stated goal.

## Core Concept

Information architecture rests on one principle: structure should follow the visitor's task, not the author's inventory of things they could mention. Three ideas make that principle usable.

**Purpose statement.** Write one sentence in the form "This site helps [specific audience] [do one thing]." For a job-seeking portfolio: "This site helps a hiring manager confirm in under a minute that I can build working data tools." That sentence is a filter. Any page that does not serve it is a candidate for cutting.

**Navigation depth and the click budget.** Visitors abandon sites whose navigation gives weak cues about where the content lives. The practical default for a small portfolio is that any important page is reachable in one click from Home (depth 1), not because clicks are costly in themselves, but because every extra hop is another chance for a vague label to lose the visitor. If your most impressive project takes three clicks to reach (Home, then Projects, then a category, then the project), you have spent your visitor's patience on hierarchy they did not ask for. Flat beats deep for a five-to-seven-page site because each link can carry a strong, specific label.

> **Recent evidence.** The popular "three-click rule" is a heuristic, not a hard limit. Nielsen Norman Group's review of the evidence (Laubheimer, 2019) found that user drop-off and satisfaction do not hinge on a raw click count. What governs abandonment is information scent: the cues in link labels and surrounding context that tell a visitor a path leads to their goal (Budiu, 2020; Pirolli, 2005). Users tolerate more clicks when each click has strong scent and leave when scent is weak, regardless of depth, which is why the Labeling principle below does as much work as the depth target.

**Labeling.** Page names are an interface. "Projects" tells a visitor what they will find; "Stuff" or "My Work Maybe" does not. Use the words your audience already uses. A hiring manager searches for "Resume," not "Curriculum Vitae of My Journey."

## Worked Example

A student is planning the information architecture for a portfolio aimed at one audience: hiring managers for entry-level data roles. Watch the structure move from a dumped list to a navigable map.

**Step 1. Name the audience and the one job.** Audience: hiring manager scanning for evidence I can do the work. Job: reach a relevant project and my resume in under 30 seconds.

**Step 2. List candidate pages, then cut to fit the job.**

| Candidate page | Keep? | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Home | Keep | Orients and routes to the rest |
| About | Keep | One paragraph plus photo, builds trust |
| Projects | Keep | The evidence the audience came for |
| Resume | Keep | A reviewer expects to download it |
| Contact | Keep | The conversion step |
| Blog | Cut | No posts yet; an empty page reads as neglect |
| Guestbook | Cut | Serves no hiring-manager goal |

**Step 3. Write the file tree that this map implies.**

```text
my-portfolio/
  index.html        (Home: one-line intro plus links to Projects, Resume)
  about.html        (About: short bio)
  projects/
    index.html      (project cards, one link each)
    dashboard.html  (one project detail page)
  resume.html       (or links to resume.pdf)
  assets/
    style.css
    resume.pdf
```

**Step 4. Justify one cut in writing.** "I removed Blog because it would have been empty. An empty section signals an unfinished site, which works against the trust the About page is trying to build."

Notice the shift: the architecture is now a path, not a pile. Every kept page maps to the audience's job, and the one removal is documented as a decision rather than an accident.

Reconstruct the order of the four IA planning moves the worked example used. Each step depends on the one before it, so getting the sequence right is the skill.

## Decision Rule

Keep a page only if you can name the visitor task it serves and that task ranks against your purpose statement. Apply this test to every candidate page before it enters the file tree:

1. **Task check:** What does a visitor come to this page to do? If you cannot name the task in one verb phrase ("download my resume," "see a working project"), the page is decoration.
2. **Reach check:** How many clicks from Home? If an important page is deeper than one click, ask whether the hierarchy is for the visitor or for you.
3. **Empty check:** Does the page have real content today? An empty page is worse than a missing one because it signals abandonment.

A page that fails the task check should be cut. A page that fails the reach check should be promoted toward Home. A page that fails the empty check should be removed until it has content.

> **Accessibility of your IA.** The labeling principle is also an accessibility requirement, which is what makes your artifact an accessibility-checked file tree rather than just a tidy one. Descriptive page names double as accessible link text: [WCAG 2.4.4 Link Purpose (In Context), Level A](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/link-purpose-in-context.html) asks that a link's purpose be clear from its text alone, which "Projects" satisfies and "Stuff" does not. A shallow, consistent nav with a skip-to-content link and proper landmark and heading structure is what lets screen-reader users jump straight to a section (WCAG 2.4.1 Bypass Blocks, Level A), and WCAG 2.4.6 Headings and Labels (Level AA) requires that those headings and labels describe their topic or purpose. Run this one IA-level check before you write any HTML in 06.02: read each page name aloud and ask whether it would still orient a visitor if it were the only thing a screen reader announced.

## Common Misreadings

The most common mistake is treating "more pages" as "more impressive." A visitor judges a portfolio by the strongest thing they can find quickly, not by the total count of links. Adding a thin Hobbies page does not raise the ceiling; it lowers the floor by diluting the path to your best work.

A second misreading is confusing visual polish with architecture. You can apply a beautiful stylesheet to a site whose navigation still hides the resume three clicks down. Polish is the last layer; structure is the first.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the rule to a concrete case. A student is building a site to apply for a summer data internship and a campus tutoring job. He drafts the purpose statement first: "This site helps an internship recruiter and a tutoring coordinator quickly confirm I can analyze data and explain it clearly." Notice the statement names two audiences, which means the architecture must serve both without forcing either to dig.

He lists candidate pages and runs the task check. Projects (serves the recruiter: shows analysis), Tutoring Experience (serves the coordinator: shows teaching), Resume (serves both), About (builds trust for both), Contact (the conversion step for both). A Blog page fails the empty check (no posts) and is cut. A Photos page fails the task check (neither audience needs it) and is cut.

He then runs the reach check. His best dashboard project was going to live under Projects, then under a "Data" subcategory, two clicks deep. Because the recruiter is the higher-stakes audience, he promotes that single project to a featured card directly on Home, keeping it one click away. The hierarchy now bends toward the visitor with the least patience and the most influence.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

A strong reader does not just accept the example site; they can regenerate the reasoning. As you study the worked example below, keep a two-column note: in the left column write each kept or cut page, and in the right column write the exact task or failed check that justified the decision. If you can reconstruct the full IA from your notes without rereading the prose, you understand the section.

A strong reader of website IA can answer four questions about any site:

1. What single sentence states this site's purpose, and which audience does it name?
2. Which page is the visitor's first goal, and how many clicks away is it?
3. Which existing page would a skeptical reviewer call filler, and why?
4. If the audience changed (say, from a recruiter to a graduate admissions committee), which pages would you reorder or rename?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

Your file tree is strong when a stranger can infer your purpose statement just by reading the page names and following the navigation. If they have to guess who the site is for, the labeling or the structure is doing too little work. Write your purpose statement at the top of your planning notes, then check that every page name and every link visibly serves it.

A strong submission includes the one cut you found hardest to make and the reason you made it. For example: "I removed the Blog page even though I liked the idea, because an empty Blog signals neglect to a recruiter, and that risk outweighed the chance I might post later."

## Transfer Case

Move the same portfolio to a different audience: a graduate program in statistics rather than an industry internship. The purpose statement changes ("help an admissions reviewer judge my research potential"), and that single change cascades through the architecture. Projects stays but is reframed around method and rigor rather than business impact. A Coursework or Research page, useless to a recruiter, now earns a top-level spot. Contact becomes less central; a clear academic email is enough. The HTML and the file mechanics are identical; the architecture is different because the visitor's task is different. If you can perform that re-ranking deliberately, you understand IA as a decision rather than a template.
