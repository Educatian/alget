## Why This Section Matters

AI coding assistants are fast and confident, and that combination is dangerous for a beginner who cannot yet tell a correct suggestion from a plausible-sounding wrong one. An assistant will happily propose a server configuration for a problem that has no server, or rewrite your whole site to fix a single typo. The skill that matters is not getting answers from AI; it is judging them against the actual symptom in your code and recording which you accepted, modified, or rejected and why.

This section teaches you to debug with AI as a junior partner you supervise, not an oracle you obey. You will learn to write a prompt that includes the real symptom and code, to verify a suggested fix instead of assuming it, and to keep a decision log that makes your judgment auditable. That log is the artifact, because it shows you understood the bug, not just that the bug went away.

## Learning Targets

- Write a verifiable debugging prompt that states the symptom and includes the relevant code, and contrast it with a prompt the AI can only guess at.
- Classify each AI debugging suggestion as accept, modify, or reject using a match check, a scope check, and a browser verify check, recording the reason.
- Document a bounded AI/software support move with accepted and rejected suggestions.
- Use feedback, support requests, and revision notes to improve the artifact before sharing it.

## Opening Case

A student's nav looks fine, but every paragraph below it renders blue and underlined, as if the whole page had become one link. He pastes "fix my website" into an AI assistant. It returns three suggestions: close an unclosed anchor tag, add a CORS server configuration for his headshot image, and rewrite the whole site in a JavaScript framework. All three sound technical and authoritative. He has no way to tell that only the first one is right, that the second is impossible for a local image, and that the third is wildly out of scope, so he tries them in order and makes the site worse before he makes it better.

The failure was not the AI; it was the absence of a method for evaluating what the AI said. This section gives you that method.

## Core Concept

Effective AI-assisted debugging has three moving parts.

**Read the symptom in the code first.** Before asking anyone (human or AI), locate the evidence. Blue runaway styling that swallows everything below a link is the classic signature of an unclosed `<a>` tag: the browser keeps treating later content as part of the link. Identifying the symptom yourself is what lets you judge whether a suggested fix actually matches it.

**Write a verifiable prompt.** "Fix my site" gives the assistant nothing to verify against, so it guesses. A verifiable prompt states the symptom, includes the relevant code, and asks a specific question: "Every paragraph below my nav renders blue and underlined as if it were a link. Here is my nav block and the first paragraph. What in my markup causes this?" A prompt grounded in the symptom and the code produces suggestions you can check against reality.

**Classify every suggestion: accept, modify, or reject, with a reason.** An assistant produces a mix of right, half-right, and irrelevant ideas. The discipline is to judge each one against the evidence. Accept a fix that matches the symptom. Modify a fix that is close but wrong in detail. Reject a fix that cannot apply (a same-folder image loaded by a relative path is same-origin, so [CORS](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/HTTP/CORS), which governs cross-origin requests, does not apply) or that is out of scope (rewriting everything to fix one tag). The reason is what proves the judgment was yours.

Whether the assistant is GitHub Copilot Chat in VS Code, ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini, the method is the same. Model versions and behavior change frequently, so what transfers is the supervision method (read the symptom, write a verifiable prompt, classify with a reason, verify in the browser), not the specific tool.

This is where [ISTE digital citizenship](https://iste.org/standards/students) becomes concrete: using AI responsibly means verifying its output, not laundering it as your own work. [NACE career readiness](https://www.naceweb.org/career-readiness/competencies/career-readiness-defined) applies because professionals are expected to supervise tools, owning the result whether the suggestion came from a colleague, a search, or a model.

## Worked Example

Maria's nav looks fine, but everything below it renders blue and underlined. She debugs with AI as a partner, not an oracle, and logs each suggestion.

**Step 1. Read the symptom in the code.**

```html
<nav>
  <a href="projects.html">Projects</a>
  <a href="about.html">About
</nav>
<p>Welcome to my portfolio.</p>
```

The About `<a>` is never closed. The open link swallows the content that follows, so the welcome paragraph below the nav renders as part of the link.

**Step 2. Write a verifiable prompt.** Not "fix my site." Instead: "Every paragraph below my nav renders blue and underlined as if it were a link. Here is my nav block and the first paragraph. What in my markup causes this?"

**Step 3. Log the AI critique with a decision.**

| AI suggestion | Decision | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Close the open anchor: add `</a>` after About | Accept | Matches the missing-close-tag symptom exactly |
| Also add a CORS server config for the headshot | Reject | The image is a same-folder local file; CORS cannot apply here |
| Rewrite the whole nav in a framework | Reject | Out of scope; the bug is one tag |

**Step 4. Verify, do not assume.** Add `</a>`, reload, and check the browser console: the runaway styling is gone and no errors remain.

The fix was a single closing tag. The accepted-modified-rejected log shows judgment: Maria kept the suggestion that fit the evidence and rejected the off-target CORS advice because it could not apply to a local file.

Order the supervised-debugging steps so you read the evidence before you trust any suggestion. Identifying the symptom yourself comes first; verifying in the browser comes last.

## Decision Rule

For every AI suggestion, run it through three questions before touching your code:

1. **Match check:** Does this fix address the symptom I actually observed? An unclosed-tag fix matches runaway styling; a server config does not match a local-file problem.
2. **Scope check:** Is this proportionate to the bug? A one-tag bug does not justify a framework rewrite. Reject fixes that solve more than you asked.
3. **Verify check:** After applying, did I confirm the result in the browser (and console), or did I just assume it worked?

A suggestion that passes match and scope gets applied; then the verify check decides whether it stays. Anything failing match or scope is rejected with a reason, and the reason goes in your log.

## Common Misreadings

The most common misreading is treating AI output as correct because it is fluent. Confidence is not accuracy. A suggestion can be grammatically perfect, technically worded, and completely irrelevant to your bug, like recommending CORS for an image that lives in the same folder.

A second misreading is logging only what you accepted. The rejections carry the most evidence of judgment, because choosing not to follow a confident suggestion, and saying why, is exactly the skill the section is testing.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the rule to the nav bug. The student first reads the symptom and finds the `<a href="about.html">About` link is never closed; the open link swallows the content after the nav, which explains why every paragraph below it renders as part of a link. Now armed with the actual cause, he evaluates the three AI suggestions. "Add `</a>` after About" passes the match check (it fixes the exact symptom) and the scope check (it is one tag), so he accepts it. "Add a CORS server config for the headshot" fails the match check (the image is a same-folder local file; CORS governs cross-origin requests, which do not apply), so he rejects it. "Rewrite the nav in a framework" fails the scope check (a framework is absurd overhead for one missing tag), so he rejects it.

He applies the closing tag, reloads, and runs the verify check: the runaway styling is gone and the console shows no errors. His log records all three decisions with reasons, so a reviewer can see he understood why the wrong suggestions were wrong, not just that the page now works.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

A strong reader evaluates suggestions against evidence rather than against how authoritative they sound. As you study the worked example, cover the Decision column in the log and predict, for each suggestion, whether you would accept or reject it and on which check it turns. If your reasoning matches the log, you can supervise an assistant rather than be steered by one.

A strong reader of AI-assisted debugging can answer four questions:

1. What is the difference between a prompt the AI can verify against and one it must guess at?
2. Why is a fluent, confident suggestion not necessarily a correct one?
3. For a given bug, which suggestions fail the match check and which fail the scope check?
4. Why does the log of rejected suggestions show more skill than the list of accepted ones?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

Your debugging log is strong when a reader can reconstruct your reasoning for each AI suggestion: what the symptom was, what the assistant proposed, what you decided, and the specific reason. The log should make clear that you, not the model, made the call, and that you verified the outcome rather than trusting it.

A strong submission highlights the suggestion you were most tempted to follow and chose not to: "The CORS fix sounded technical and convincing, but I rejected it because my image is a local same-folder file, so cross-origin rules cannot apply."

## Transfer Case

Take the same supervision method to a different tool and a different bug, say an AI assistant suggesting an Excel formula that returns the wrong total. The medium changes from HTML to a spreadsheet, but the method is identical: read the symptom (the total is off by one row), write a prompt that includes the formula and the wrong result, then judge the suggestion against the evidence (does the proposed range actually cover all rows?) and verify by checking the recomputed total. The accept-modify-reject log works the same way. Carrying the method across tools is the proof that you learned to supervise AI, not to debug one specific page.
