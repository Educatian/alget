# Privacy and Account Safety

![Privacy and Account Safety textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat100-supplement/01-05-privacy-and-account-safety.png)

## Why This Section Matters

Your school email is the master key to your digital life. It can reset your bank login, your social media, your financial aid portal, and your course accounts. If someone takes it over, they do not just read your mail; they walk through every door it can unlock. This section is about the small set of habits that make that takeover hard: unique passwords, a second factor, and the ability to recognize a phishing message before you click.

The work product is a privacy and account-safety checklist where each item is a concrete action a reviewer could confirm, not a vague claim that you "made things safer." Security is not a feeling; it is a set of settings that are either on or off and a set of behaviors you either practice or do not.

## Learning Targets

- Create a unique, strong passphrase and stop reusing one password across sites.
- Turn on two-factor authentication and explain why a stolen password alone then fails.
- Spot the standard signals of a phishing message and respond without clicking.
- Strip personal and others' data out of any text before pasting it into an AI tool.

## Opening Case

Sam reuses one password across his shopping account, his streaming service, and his school email. A shopping site he used two years ago suffers a data breach, and his email and password end up on a list that attackers buy. Within days, someone logs into his school email from another country, resets his financial aid password, and changes his contact email so he cannot get back in.

Sam never clicked anything suspicious. The breach happened somewhere else entirely. Password reuse is what turned one company's mistake into his crisis, and a second factor on his email would have stopped the login cold.

## Core Concept

Account safety rests on a simple idea: do not let one failure become total failure. Three layers carry most of the protection a student needs.

| Layer | What it does | The failure it blocks |
|-------|--------------|------------------------|
| Unique passwords | A breach at one site cannot unlock others | Credential stuffing from leaked lists |
| Two-factor authentication | A second proof is required to log in | A stolen password used alone |
| Phishing awareness | You verify before you act | Handing your credentials to an attacker |

Why unique passwords matter is concrete: attackers take email-and-password pairs leaked from one breach and try them on hundreds of other sites automatically. Reuse means one leak unlocks everything. Why two-factor authentication matters is that it breaks the value of a stolen password: even with your password, an attacker also needs the code on your phone, which they do not have. Why phishing awareness matters is that the strongest password and second factor do not help if you type them into a fake login page yourself. The three layers cover the three main ways accounts fall: a leak elsewhere, a guessed or stolen password, and a tricked user.

## Worked Example

The weak version below sounds responsible, but a reviewer cannot confirm it:

> I made my account safer.

That sentence is too thin because "safer" names no setting and no action. Nobody can check it. A strong checklist names each item and the specific action you took, so a reviewer can verify it the same way you would verify a resume claim.

### Worked Example: hardening one account, step by step

Walk through securing your school email, checking each item against the privacy checklist.

1. **Unique password.** Old setup: same password as your shopping site. Action: create a unique 16-character passphrase, for example `purple-train-coffee-42`, used nowhere else.
2. **Turn on two-factor authentication.** Enable app-based codes so a leaked password alone cannot grant access.
3. **Test a phishing signal.** A message says: "Verify in 1 hour or lose access: bit.ly/uni-verify." Decision: do not click; open the portal at its known address and confirm there. Mark the message as phishing.
4. **Check AI data boundary.** Before asking AI to reword an email, remove your student ID and a classmate's phone number, then paste only the text to revise.

Checklist result: 4 of 4 items addressed, each with a concrete action a reviewer can confirm, not just "I made my account safer."

## Decision Rule

Before you act on any message that asks for credentials, money, or urgent clicks, slow down and verify through a channel you already trust. Urgency, a threat of losing access, a slightly-off sender address, and a shortened link are the classic phishing tells; any one of them means you go to the site yourself instead of clicking the link. For your accounts, the rule is: every important account gets a unique password and, wherever offered, a second factor.

1. **Reuse check:** Is this password used on any other site? If yes, change it.
2. **Second-factor check:** Is two-factor authentication on for this account?
3. **Verify-before-click check:** Did you confirm the request through a known-good channel rather than the link in the message?

## Common Misreadings

A common mistake is thinking a "strong" password (lots of symbols) on every site is enough. Strength does not help against reuse: if you use the same strong password everywhere and one site leaks it, every account falls. Uniqueness matters more than complexity, which is why a long passphrase you can remember, different per site, beats one clever password shared across all of them.

A second misreading is assuming phishing always looks obviously fake. Modern phishing copies real logos, uses urgent but plausible wording, and may even spoof a real name. The reliable tell is not bad spelling; it is the request itself. A legitimate institution does not threaten to delete your account in one hour over an unsolicited link.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Picture a password manager, which is the practical way to follow the unique-password rule without memorizing dozens of strings. You install it, let it generate a different long random password for each site, and protect the whole vault with one strong master passphrase plus two-factor authentication. Now reuse is solved structurally: the manager remembers a unique password per site so you do not have to. The one password you must memorize is the master one, and it is the one you most protect.

The walkthrough's point is that good security is mostly about removing the human bottleneck. People reuse passwords because remembering unique ones is hard; the manager removes the hardship, so the safe behavior becomes the easy behavior. Pair that with two-factor authentication on your email and a habit of verifying suspicious messages, and you have covered the three layers without relying on willpower at every login.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

When you read a security claim (your own or a friend's), check whether it names a verifiable setting or just a feeling. "I'm careful online" is a feeling; "2FA is on for my email and my password manager generates unique passwords" is a set of checkable facts. The difference is the same one you practiced with resume bullets: a result a third party could confirm versus a vague impression.

A strong reader of an account-safety claim can answer four questions:

1. Is each important account protected by a unique password?
2. Is two-factor authentication on where it is offered?
3. Does the person verify before clicking on urgent requests?
4. Is private data kept out of tools that might store it?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

Your checklist is strong when each row names a setting that is demonstrably on or an action you actually took, so a reviewer could confirm it. The earlier layers are the unique password, the second factor, and the phishing response; the last layer is presentation. If you are unsure whether a setting counts, mark it in your trace and ask BigAL to help you find the exact toggle rather than asking it for general security tips.

A useful note would read: "I changed my school email password from one reused across three sites to a unique passphrase, turned on app-based 2FA, and treated a 'verify in one hour' message as phishing by going to the portal directly instead of clicking. I also stripped a classmate's phone number from an email before pasting it into an AI tool."

## Transfer Case

Move the safety habit from your own accounts to a shared device or a group account, such as a club's social media login. The reuse and second-factor rules still hold, but a new constraint appears: multiple people need access without sharing one personal password. The transfer move is to use the platform's role-based access or a shared password manager vault, and to turn on two-factor authentication tied to a method the whole leadership team can reach, not one person's phone. The underlying principle, do not let one failure become total failure, does not change; what changes is that you now design for several trusted users rather than one. Understanding the principle, not just the personal recipe, is what lets you keep a shared account safe.
