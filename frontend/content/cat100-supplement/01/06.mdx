## Why This Section Matters

When you make a slide, a document, or a web page, some of your audience will use a screen reader, some cannot distinguish red from green, and some are reading on a dim phone in bright sun. Accessibility is the practice of making sure your content works for all of them, not just for a sighted reader on a good monitor. It is not charity or an afterthought; it is part of doing the job correctly, and in many settings it is a legal requirement (the U.S. DOJ's 2024 ADA Title II rule adopts WCAG 2.1 AA as the standard for state and local government web content, which includes public universities; see [ADA.gov](https://www.ada.gov/resources/2024-03-08-web-rule/)).

The work product is a before-after accessibility pass on a real artifact, where each fix maps to a specific, checkable standard. The [WCAG](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/) guidelines turn "make it accessible" into concrete, testable items: does the image have alt text, does the page use real headings, does meaning survive without color, does the text have enough contrast.

## Learning Targets

- Write alt text that conveys an image's meaning, not just its file name.
- Use real heading styles so assistive technology can navigate the structure.
- Ensure no information is carried by color alone.
- Check text contrast against the [WCAG](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/) threshold and fix failures.

## Opening Case

Jordan finishes a slide titled "Club Growth" with a bar chart and a list of steps. It looks clean. But the chart's alt text is `Chart1.png`, the title is just large bold text rather than a heading, the required steps are marked only in red, and the body text is light gray on white. To a sighted reader on a laptop, none of this is visible. To a student using a screen reader, the chart is meaningless and the title is not announced; to a colorblind classmate, "required" is invisible; to anyone in bright light, the gray text disappears.

The slide is not broken for Jordan, which is exactly why the problem is easy to miss. Accessibility errors are usually invisible to the person who made them.

## Core Concept

Accessibility is the principle that content should not depend on a single way of perceiving it. A screen reader needs text where you put an image; a colorblind reader needs a second cue besides color; a low-vision reader needs contrast. Each need maps to a concrete [WCAG](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/) check.

| Check | What it fixes | How to verify it |
|-------|---------------|------------------|
| Alt text | Images for screen-reader users | Read the alt aloud: does it convey the meaning? |
| Heading structure | Navigation for assistive tech | Use real heading styles, not big bold text |
| Color independence | Meaning for colorblind users | Remove color: is the info still there? |
| Contrast ratio | Readability for low vision | Check against the [WCAG](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/) 4.5:1 ratio for body text; large text (18pt+, or 14pt bold) and graphical objects need 3:1 |

Two ideas make these checks make sense. First, alt text should describe the image's function in context, not its appearance: a chart's alt text is the conclusion the chart shows ("membership rose from 20 to 55"), not "a bar chart." Second, a real heading is structural, not visual: applying the `Heading 1` style lets a screen reader announce and jump to it, while text that is merely large and bold looks like a heading but is invisible to navigation. Building access in from the start (the Universal Design for Learning idea) is far easier than retrofitting it at the end.

## Worked Example

A first draft of an accessibility claim often hides the real work:

> I made the slide more accessible.

That sentence is too thin because it names no check and no change. A reviewer cannot confirm it. A strong record shows each [WCAG](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/) check, the before state, and the specific fix, so accessibility becomes a list of confirmable items rather than a claim.

### Worked Example: fixing one slide for accessibility, step by step

Take a single slide titled "Club Growth" and run an accessibility pass.

1. **Alt text.** Before: the chart's alt text is `Chart1.png`. After: `Bar chart showing club membership rising from 20 members in fall to 55 in spring.`
2. **Heading structure.** Before: the title is just 28pt bold text. After: apply the real `Heading 1` style so screen readers announce it as a heading.
3. **Color is not the only cue.** Before: required steps are shown only in red. After: keep the red but add the word `Required` next to each, so meaning does not depend on color.
4. **Contrast check.** Before: light gray text on white. After: switch to dark gray on white to clear the contrast threshold.

Result: the slide looks similar but now passes four concrete checks, each one a change a reviewer can confirm rather than a claim that it is "more accessible."

## Decision Rule

For every visual element you create, ask whether a person who cannot see it, cannot tell colors apart, or cannot read low-contrast text would still get the full meaning. If the answer is no, you have an accessibility gap to fix. The rule of thumb: never let a single channel (sight, color, contrast) be the only way to receive important information.

1. **Image check:** Does every meaningful image have alt text that conveys its point?
2. **Structure check:** Are titles and sections marked with real heading styles?
3. **Independence check:** Is any information carried by color alone, and does the text meet the contrast ratio?

## Common Misreadings

A common mistake is writing alt text that describes the picture literally instead of its purpose. "Image of a graph with blue bars" tells a screen-reader user nothing useful; "membership grew from 20 to 55 over the year" tells them what the graph is for. Alt text answers "why is this image here?" not "what does it look like?"

A second misreading is treating accessibility as a final cleanup step. Retrofitting access after the design is locked is slow and incomplete; you end up bolting alt text onto images and discovering that your color-coded system has no backup cue. Building access in from the start, the way Universal Design for Learning recommends, means the design works for everyone the first time instead of being patched for some people at the end.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Imagine running an accessibility pass on a one-page event flyer you built in a word processor. Start with images: the venue map and the sponsor logos all need alt text, and the decorative divider line should be marked decorative so the screen reader skips it. Move to structure: the event name becomes `Heading 1`, the schedule and the RSVP sections become `Heading 2`, so a screen-reader user can jump between sections. Check color: the "SOLD OUT" sessions were shown only in gray, so add the word "(full)" next to each. Finally check contrast: the pale-blue tagline on white fails, so darken it.

The walkthrough shows accessibility as a repeatable checklist, not a judgment call. Each pass (images, structure, color, contrast) has a clear test, and each fix is something a reviewer or an automated checker can confirm. The flyer still looks like the same flyer; it is now usable by people whose needs you cannot see when you look at your own screen.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

When you review a document or slide for accessibility, do not ask "does it look fine?" Ask the four checks directly: turn on the screen reader or read the alt text aloud, view the page in grayscale to find color-only meaning, and run a contrast check on the body text. Each test exposes problems that are invisible to a normal glance.

A strong reviewer of an artifact can answer four questions:

1. Would a screen-reader user get every meaningful image and the page structure?
2. Does any information vanish when color is removed?
3. Does the body text meet the contrast threshold?
4. Were these built in, or bolted on at the end?

**Tools that run these checks (as of 2026).** In Microsoft PowerPoint, Word, and Excel, the built-in Accessibility Checker (Review > Check Accessibility) flags missing alt text, structure problems, and contrast failures, and has a "Keep accessibility checker running while I work" option for real-time checking. For contrast, the free WebAIM Contrast Checker reports pass or fail against the 4.5:1 and 3:1 thresholds from two entered colors. Google Slides and Docs have alt-text fields but no built-in standards checker, so checking a Google deck takes an add-on such as Grackle or an external contrast checker. Menu labels drift between versions, so search the app's help if a path has moved. BigAL can draft functional alt text or explain a failed check, but the standards check itself comes from these tools and the [WCAG](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/) criteria, not from the LLM.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

Your accessibility pass is strong when each fix maps to a named [WCAG](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/) check and could be confirmed by a reviewer or a checker tool. The earlier layers are alt text, heading structure, color independence, and contrast; the last layer is visual polish, which should never come at the cost of access. If a fix is unclear, mark it in your trace and ask BigAL to help you write functional alt text or find the contrast ratio rather than asking it to make the slide "look better."

A useful note would read: "On the Club Growth slide I rewrote the chart alt text to state the trend, applied a real Heading 1 to the title, added the word 'Required' so meaning does not rely on red, and darkened the body text to meet the contrast ratio. Each change maps to a [WCAG](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/) check a reviewer can confirm."

## Transfer Case

Carry the four checks to a different medium: a short video you post for a group project. The principles re-map cleanly. Alt text becomes captions and a transcript, so deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers get the audio content. Heading structure becomes clear on-screen titles and a described agenda, so the video is navigable. Color independence becomes "do not say 'click the green button' without also naming it." Contrast applies to any on-screen text over video. The medium changed from a slide to a video, but the underlying question, can someone with a different way of perceiving still get the full meaning, did not. Owning that question rather than a fixed slide-checklist is what lets you make any artifact accessible.
