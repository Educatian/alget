# Prompting With Verification

![Prompting With Verification textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat100-supplement/01-04-prompting-with-verification.png)

## Why This Section Matters

A language model predicts likely words; it does not look facts up unless told to. That is why it can state a wrong room number, a fake citation, or an out-of-date hour with total confidence. The text reads smoothly whether it is true or invented. This section teaches the one habit that makes AI safe to use for factual work: every factual claim it produces is a hypothesis until you check it against an authoritative source.

The work product is an AI critique log that records, claim by claim, what you kept, corrected, and removed after verification. A good prompt gets you a useful draft; verification turns that draft into something you can stand behind. Without the verification step, you are not using AI, you are repeating it.

## Learning Targets

- Write a prompt specific enough to produce checkable claims, and ask the model to flag uncertainty.
- Separate the model's output into individual factual claims you can verify one at a time.
- Confirm, correct, or remove each claim against an official source, and log the result.
- Recognize a hallucination: a confident, specific claim with no source behind it.

## Opening Case

Priya needs three campus facts for a "new student guide." She asks a chatbot and gets a clean list: the Writing Center is on the second floor of the library, the Health Center is open 24/7, and there are free laptop loans at the Student Union in room 210. The list looks authoritative. But when she checks, the Health Center's posted hours are 8 AM to 6 PM, and there is no room 210 laptop program anywhere on the university site.

Two of the three facts were wrong, and nothing in the AI's tone warned her. If she had pasted the list straight into her guide, she would have published confident misinformation under her own name.

## Core Concept

Verification works because of a key property of language models: fluency and accuracy are independent. The model is optimized to produce text that sounds right, not text that is right. A made-up room number and a real room number are written with identical confidence. Therefore you cannot judge a claim by how it reads; you must judge it by whether a trustworthy source confirms it.

The reliable procedure is a **prompt-then-verify loop** with three moves.

| Move | What you do | Why it matters |
|------|-------------|----------------|
| Constrain the prompt | Ask for specifics and request uncertainty flags | Specific claims are checkable; vague claims hide errors |
| Atomize the output | Split the answer into separate claims | You verify one fact at a time, not a paragraph |
| Verify each claim | Confirm, correct, or remove against a source | Only checked facts survive into your work |

The strongest single tactic is to ask the model to mark anything it is unsure about. Models will often flag their own shaky claims if invited, which tells you where to aim your checking first. But the flag is a hint, not a guarantee; an unflagged claim still needs verification.

## Worked Example

The weak version below sounds plausible, but it does not yet give a reader enough evidence:

> I asked AI and it gave me three campus facts.

That sentence is too thin because it does not show that any fact was checked. A reader has no way to know whether the claims are true. A strong record shows the original output, the source you checked each claim against, and what you did with it.

### Worked Example: prompt, then verify, step by step

You need three campus facts for a "new student guide." Run a prompt-then-verify loop.

1. **Write a specific prompt:** `Act as a campus guide. List three services for first-year students (name, location, one-line purpose). Mark any item you are not certain about.`
2. **Read the output and flag claims.** AI returns: (a) Writing Center, Library 2nd floor; (b) Health Center, open 24/7; (c) "Free laptop loans at the Student Union, room 210."
3. **Verify each against an official source.** (a) Confirmed on the library site: kept. (b) Official hours say 8 AM to 6 PM, not 24/7: corrected to the real hours. (c) No room 210 laptop loan program found anywhere: removed as likely fabricated.
4. **Record the verification:** `Claim a: confirmed (library site). Claim b: corrected hours (health center page). Claim c: removed, unverifiable.`

Result: one claim kept, one corrected, one cut. The guide now contains only facts you checked, with a trail a reader can follow.

## Decision Rule

Treat any specific, factual claim from AI as unverified until a primary or official source confirms it. Numbers, names, dates, locations, hours, prices, and citations are the highest-risk items because they are exactly what models fabricate most convincingly. If you cannot find a source for a claim, you do not get to keep it because it "sounds right"; you correct it or remove it.

1. **Source check:** Is there an official page, document, or record that confirms this claim?
2. **Specificity check:** Did you verify the exact number, date, or location, not just the general idea?
3. **Removal check:** For any claim you could not confirm, did you correct or delete it?

## Common Misreadings

The most damaging misreading is "the AI was confident, so it was probably right." Confidence is a property of the writing style, not of the truth. A fabricated citation is formatted as carefully as a real one. The smoother the lie, the more important the check.

A second misreading is verifying the easy claims and trusting the hard ones. Students often confirm a claim they could already guess and skip the unfamiliar fact, which is the one most likely to be wrong. Aim your verification at the claims you cannot already vouch for, especially anything flagged or anything oddly specific.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Imagine using AI to help with a citation list for a short paper. You ask for "three peer-reviewed sources on study habits." The model returns three entries with authors, journals, years, and page numbers, all perfectly formatted. This is the most dangerous case, because fabricated citations are the model's specialty: the format is real even when the paper is not. Verify by searching each title in the library database or Google Scholar. Two resolve to real articles; one returns nothing, and the "journal" does not exist. You remove the third and replace it with a real source you found and read.

The walkthrough shows verification is not optional even when the output looks scholarly. The fix is procedural, not clever: split the list, search each entry, keep only what resolves to a real, accessible source, and log what you removed. A reader who opens your citation list should be able to find every source you kept, because you already did.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

When you read AI-generated text, train yourself to underline every specific, checkable claim: a number, a name, a date, a location, a quotation. Those underlines are your verification to-do list. Vague sentences ("study habits are important") need no source; specific ones ("students who sleep 8 hours score 12% higher") demand one before you repeat them.

A strong reader of AI output can answer four questions:

1. Which claims here are specific enough to be true or false?
2. Which of those have I confirmed against a source?
3. Which claim is the model itself flagging as uncertain?
4. What did I correct or remove, and why?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

Your verification log is strong when a skeptic could re-check every kept claim using the sources you listed and reach the same conclusions. The earlier layers are the constrained prompt, the atomized claims, and the per-claim verification; the last layer is wording. If a claim is hard to verify, mark it in your trace and ask BigAL to help you locate an authoritative source rather than asking it to reassure you that the claim is fine.

A useful note would read: "AI gave three campus facts. I confirmed the Writing Center location on the library site, corrected the Health Center hours from a fabricated 24/7 to the posted 8 to 6, and removed the room 210 laptop claim because no official source mentions it."

## Transfer Case

Carry the loop into a spreadsheet task where AI suggests a formula or a data value. The same independence between fluency and accuracy applies: AI will confidently propose `=VLOOKUP(...)` syntax that is subtly wrong, or assert that a column's total is a certain number. You verify a formula by running it on a row you can check by hand, and you verify a claimed total by recomputing it with `=SUM()`. The medium changed from facts to formulas, but the discipline did not: a confident output is a hypothesis, and you keep only what you have independently confirmed. Once verification is a reflex, it protects you in writing, research, data work, and anywhere else a tool hands you a fluent answer.
