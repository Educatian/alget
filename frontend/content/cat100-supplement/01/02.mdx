## Why This Section Matters

Generative AI tools can brainstorm, summarize, draft, and rewrite in seconds. That power creates a new question for every assignment: not "Can I use AI?" but "How much of this work is allowed to be the tool's, and how do I prove the rest is mine?" Getting that line wrong is how a capable student ends up in an academic-integrity meeting. Getting it right is a transferable professional skill, because workplaces are writing the same kind of AI-use rules.

The work product here is a privacy and AI-use disclosure built around a specific assignment policy. A disclosure is not a confession; it is a record that lets a grader trust your work. When the disclosure is precise about what the tool did and what you did, it protects both your grade and your integrity.

## Learning Targets

- Read an assignment's AI policy and sort each planned action into allowed, required, or prohibited.
- Write an AI-use disclosure that names the tool, the step, and the suggestions you rejected.
- Recognize the line between AI assisting your thinking and AI replacing your thinking.
- Keep private data (yours and others') out of tools that may store or train on inputs.

## Opening Case

A first-year writing assignment carries this policy: "AI may be used to brainstorm and to check grammar. The argument and final wording must be yours. Disclose all AI use." Two students use AI on the same essay. The first asks AI for thesis ideas, picks one, writes the essay herself, and runs a grammar check. The second pastes the prompt into a chatbot, submits the output with light edits, and writes "I used AI a little" at the bottom.

Both used AI. Only the first stayed inside the policy, and only the first can prove it. The vague disclosure in the second case is not protection; it is a flag that invites scrutiny without answering any question.

## Core Concept

Every AI policy draws a boundary between **assistance** and **substitution**. Assistance helps you think (brainstorming angles, explaining a concept, catching a comma splice). Substitution does the graded thinking for you (writing the argument, generating the analysis, producing the final wording). The graded learning objective tells you which side of the line a given use falls on: if the assignment is testing your argument, AI cannot write the argument; if the assignment is testing your code logic, AI cannot write the logic.

A defensible disclosure has four parts.

| Part | Question it answers | Example |
|------|---------------------|---------|
| Tool | Which system did you use? | "Used a chatbot and a grammar checker" |
| Action | What specific step did it do? | "Generated 5 thesis angles; fixed 2 comma splices" |
| Ownership | What did you do yourself? | "Selected angle 3 and wrote the draft myself" |
| Rejection | What did you decline and why? | "Rejected its thesis rewrite to keep the argument mine" |

The rejection line is what turns a disclosure from a formality into evidence of judgment. Anyone can say "I used AI"; only someone who actually controlled the tool can say which suggestion they turned down and why.

(In 2026, "a chatbot" usually means a general assistant such as ChatGPT, Google Gemini, Claude, or Microsoft Copilot, and "a grammar checker" a tool such as Grammarly. Tool names and model versions change fast, so the Tool line of your disclosure should record the specific tool and version you actually used and when, for example "Used ChatGPT on 6/3," not just the category.)

## Worked Example

A thin disclosure that many students write looks like this:

> I used AI to help with my essay.

That sentence is too vague to verify. It does not say which tool, which step, or whether the argument is yours. A grader reading it cannot tell whether you stayed inside the policy, so it creates suspicion instead of trust.

### Worked Example: writing an AI-use disclosure that matches the policy

Build the disclosure step by step against the policy quoted above.

1. **List what you actually did with AI.** (a) Asked for five possible thesis angles. (b) Picked angle 3 and wrote the draft yourself. (c) Ran the draft through a grammar checker that fixed two comma splices.
2. **Sort each step against the policy.** Brainstorm angles: allowed. Write the argument yourself: required. Grammar fix: allowed.
3. **Check for anything out of bounds.** The grammar tool also tried to rewrite your thesis into its own words. That is generating the argument, which is not allowed, so you reject it.
4. **Write the disclosure note:** `AI use: brainstormed 5 thesis angles (selected angle 3, drafted essay myself); used a grammar checker for two comma fixes. Rejected its thesis rewrite to keep the argument my own.`

The note is specific (what tool, what step, what you rejected), so a grader can verify your use stayed inside the policy.

## Decision Rule

Before you use a tool on graded work, ask what the assignment is actually testing, then keep the tool off that exact thing. If the assignment grades your argument, brainstorming with AI is fine but drafting the argument is not. When you are unsure, the safe move is to disclose more, not less: a detailed disclosure of an allowed use never hurts you, while a hidden use that turns out to be prohibited always does.

1. **Objective check:** What skill is this assignment grading, and did the tool perform that skill?
2. **Disclosure check:** Does your note name the tool, the step, and at least one rejected suggestion?
3. **Data check:** Did you paste any private or identifying information into the tool?

## Common Misreadings

A frequent error is assuming that lightly editing AI output makes the work yours. Changing a few words in a generated paragraph does not move the thinking from the tool to you; the argument is still the tool's. If the assignment grades the argument, that is substitution no matter how many synonyms you swap in.

A second misreading is treating disclosure as risky, as if admitting AI use invites trouble. The opposite is true under most policies: an honest, specific disclosure of an allowed use is exactly what the policy asks for, and it is the vague or missing disclosure that triggers an integrity review.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Picture a coding assignment with the policy, "You may ask AI to explain errors, but you must write and debug your own functions." You hit a `KeyError` and do not understand it. Asking AI "what does this error mean and what usually causes it?" is assistance: it explains the concept, and you still write the fix. Asking AI "rewrite my function so the error goes away" is substitution: it produces the logic the assignment is testing. The same tool, two prompts, opposite sides of the line.

Now write the disclosure for the allowed path: "Asked AI to explain a KeyError in the abstract; it described missing-key lookups. I located the bad key and rewrote the lookup myself." That note names the tool's role (concept explanation), preserves your ownership (you wrote the fix), and would survive any instructor's question. Notice that the disclosure is short. Precision, not length, is what makes it defensible.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

When you read an AI policy, do not skim for the word "allowed." Read for the verb that names the graded skill: argue, analyze, design, calculate, code. That verb is the thing AI must not do for you. Everything around it (formatting, grammar, brainstorming, explaining) is usually negotiable, but the graded verb is the boundary.

A strong reader of an AI policy can answer four questions:

1. What graded skill does this assignment protect from AI?
2. Which of my planned uses are clearly allowed?
3. Which use is ambiguous and needs me to ask or disclose more?
4. What private data must never enter the tool?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

Your disclosure is strong when an instructor can read it and immediately see that the graded thinking is yours. The earlier layers are the policy reading, the allowed/prohibited sort, and the rejection note; the last layer is wording. If you are unsure whether a use is allowed, mark it in your trace and ask BigAL to help you classify it against the policy rather than asking it to do the assignment.

A useful note would read: "The policy allowed brainstorming but required my own argument. I used AI for thesis angles and grammar only, wrote the argument myself, and rejected the tool's attempt to rewrite my thesis. I also stripped a classmate's name from the text before pasting it for a grammar check."

## Transfer Case

Carry the skill into a setting with no written policy: a group project where teammates have different comfort levels with AI. Here the boundary is not handed to you; you negotiate it. The same four-part habit still works: agree as a team on which parts of the project AI may touch (formatting, drafting outlines) and which it may not (the analysis you will each defend), then keep a shared disclosure log. When the boundary is implicit, the disclosure becomes even more important, because it is the only record showing that the graded thinking belonged to the people, not the tool. Understanding responsible use as a boundary you can locate and document, rather than a rule you wait to be given, is what lets you act well when no policy is printed at the top of the page.

**This habit is already a professional requirement.** Academic journals now forbid listing an AI as an author and require contributors to disclose generative-AI assistance by tool name, version, and purpose (ICMJE and COPE policies), while routine grammar polishing is typically exempt. The assistance-versus-substitution line you practice here is the same line working professionals must document.
