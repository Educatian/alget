## Why This Section Matters

ETHOBOT is the AI tutor you will use throughout CAT 100. The point of meeting it now is to set the relationship up correctly: ETHOBOT is a critic, not an author. It can react to a draft you wrote, point out weak spots, and propose changes, but the deciding vote is always yours. A student who treats the tutor as an oracle copies whatever it says; a student who treats it as a reviewer keeps a record of which suggestions they took, changed, or refused.

The work product for this orientation is an AI critique log that shows accepted, modified, and rejected suggestions. The log is the proof that you ran the tutor, not the other way around. Every row records a suggestion and your call on it, so an instructor can see your judgment rather than just the final text.

## Learning Targets

- Bring a draft of your own to the tutor instead of asking it to generate from scratch.
- Record each tutor suggestion as accepted, modified, or rejected with a one-line reason.
- Keep rejected suggestions in the log so the trail shows what you turned down.
- Use the tutor to sharpen claims without letting it invent facts or inflate them.

## Opening Case

Devon asks ETHOBOT to review one resume bullet: "Helped with the club's fall event and boosted attendance." The tutor returns three suggestions: add a number, replace "helped" with "led," and add "expert event planner." A passive user would accept all three and submit "Led the fall event as an expert event planner, boosting attendance." That sentence is now partly false: Devon co-led, ran one event, and is not an expert.

The orientation skill is to take each suggestion separately. One is a clear improvement, one needs a truthful tweak, and one must be refused. The log is where those three different calls become visible.

## Core Concept

A critique log treats every AI suggestion as a proposal that must clear a bar before it enters your work. There are exactly three outcomes, and a strong log uses all three.

| Decision | Meaning | When to use it |
|----------|---------|----------------|
| Accepted | You take the suggestion as-is | It improves the work and stays truthful |
| Modified | You take part of the idea, change the rest | The direction is right but the wording overreaches |
| Rejected | You decline it | It is false, off-target, or weakens the claim |

The hardest and most important column is **rejected**. A log with only "accepted" rows is indistinguishable from copy-paste; it shows no judgment. The rejected and modified rows are the evidence that a person sat between the tool and the page. This is why the orientation insists you record the rejections: the value you add as a learner lives in the suggestions you did not take. Research on AI-assisted decision-making backs this discipline: people frequently accept an AI's suggestion even when it is wrong, and interventions that force deliberate engagement, exactly what a reject-or-modify row demands, measurably reduce that overreliance (Bucinca, Malaya, & Gajos, 2021).

**AI-use disclosure in 2026.** ETHOBOT is the course's bounded tutor, but the same accept/modify/reject discipline applies to the general-purpose assistants you will also encounter, such as ChatGPT, Claude, or Microsoft Copilot. As of 2026, many programs require an AI-use disclosure that names which tool was used, for what purpose, and to what extent, and your critique log doubles as exactly that disclosure: it already records the tool, the suggestions, and your calls on them. Keeping the log current here means the disclosure you assemble later in the course is already written.

## Worked Example

A first draft of a log often hides the real decision behind a vague summary:

> ETHOBOT helped me improve my bullet.

That sentence is too thin because it does not name the suggestions or your calls on them. A reviewer cannot tell whether you exercised judgment or simply accepted everything. A strong log shows each suggestion and your decision, including the one you refused.

### Worked Example: turning a tutor conversation into a critique log

You ask ETHOBOT to review one resume bullet and it returns three suggestions. Build the log row by row.

| # | Tutor suggestion | Your decision | Reason |
|---|------------------|---------------|--------|
| 1 | Add a number to "boosted attendance" | Accepted | Changed to "raised event attendance from 15 to 40" |
| 2 | Replace "helped" with "led" | Modified | I co-led, so I used "co-led a 4-person team" to stay truthful |
| 3 | Add "expert event planner" | Rejected | One event does not make me an expert; the claim is not defensible |

Final bullet: `Co-led a 4-person team that raised event attendance from 15 to 40 in one month.`

The log records all three suggestions, not just the ones you used. A reader can see the accepted, modified, and rejected calls and the reason for each.

Work Devon's three tutor suggestions as a decision tree. For each one, pick accept, modify, or reject before you read the feedback.

## Decision Rule

For every suggestion the tutor makes, ask whether it makes the claim both stronger and still true. If yes, accept it. If the idea is good but the wording overreaches, modify it down to what you can defend. If it is false, irrelevant, or inflates your role, reject it and log why. The rule that keeps you safe is simple: the tutor may improve how you say something, but you decide what is true.

1. **Improvement check:** Does the suggestion make the claim clearer or stronger?
2. **Truth check:** Is the suggested wording still something you could defend?
3. **Logging check:** Did you record the decision and reason, including for rejections?

## Common Misreadings

The most common error is accepting a suggestion because it sounds more impressive. "Expert," "spearheaded," and "led" feel like upgrades, but if they describe work you did not do, they are downgrades to your credibility. The tutor optimizes for polish; you have to optimize for truth.

A second misreading is deleting the rejected suggestions from the log to make it look clean. That removes exactly the evidence that proves you were in control. A log with visible rejections is more trustworthy than a tidy log that shows only accepted changes.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Suppose you bring the tutor a short "About me" paragraph for a class profile. ETHOBOT suggests five changes. Rather than reading them as a to-do list, open a three-column table and route each one: a tighter opening sentence (accept), a claim that you "mentored dozens of students" when you mentored three (modify to "mentored 3 first-year students"), a suggestion to add a hobby that is not true (reject), a grammar fix (accept), and a recommendation to add a metric you cannot verify (reject). In ten minutes you have a profile that reads well and a log that shows five decisions.

The walkthrough's lesson is that the tutor's output is raw material, not a verdict. The orientation is teaching you a stance you will reuse in every later module: read AI output as a set of proposals, sort them, and keep the sort. When you reach the verification and accessibility sections, you will be running the same loop on facts and on design, but the habit of logging accepted, modified, and rejected starts here.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

When you read someone else's critique log, look for whether the three decision types are all present and whether the reasons are specific. A log that says "accepted: good idea" for every row is weak; a log that says "rejected: I never used Python, so I cannot claim it" shows a person guarding the truth. The reasons are where the learning shows.

A strong reader of a critique log can answer four questions:

1. Did the learner bring their own draft, or did the tool generate it?
2. Are there modified and rejected rows, not just accepted ones?
3. Does each reason name a real reason (truth, fit, scope), not just "better"?
4. Is the final artifact consistent with the decisions the log records?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

Your critique log is strong when a reviewer can reconstruct your reasoning from the log alone, without re-running the tutor. The earlier layers are bringing your own draft, capturing every suggestion, and recording an honest decision for each; the last layer is tidiness. If a suggestion is tempting but you are unsure, mark it in the log and ask BigAL to compare the truthful version against the inflated version rather than asking it to decide for you.

A useful note would read: "ETHOBOT gave three suggestions on my event bullet. I accepted the number, modified 'led' to 'co-led' because that is accurate, and rejected 'expert event planner' because one event does not support that claim. The final bullet matches what I actually did."

## Transfer Case

Move the critique-log habit from a resume bullet to a piece of code the tutor reviews. The structure is identical: ETHOBOT might suggest renaming a variable (accept), wrapping a block in a try/except (modify, because you only want to catch one specific error), and "rewriting the whole function to be cleaner" (reject, because the assignment grades your logic). The decision types do not change when the medium changes; what changes is the truth and ownership test you apply. A code rewrite that hides your logic is the same kind of overreach as an "expert" label you did not earn. If you have internalized the accept/modify/reject loop here, you can run it on writing, on data, on design, and on code without learning a new skill each time.
