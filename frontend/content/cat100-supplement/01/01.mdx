## Why This Section Matters

Your digital identity is the version of you that a stranger meets before you walk into the room. When a hiring manager searches your name, when a scholarship committee opens your LinkedIn, or when a recruiter scans the top third of your resume for six seconds, they build a fast model of who you are from whatever evidence is visible. This section is about controlling that evidence on purpose instead of leaving it to chance.

The work product for this section is a resume before-after revision that carries truthful evidence, shows clear role fit, and includes an AI-use note. A resume is not a list of duties; it is a short argument that says, "Hire me, and here is the proof." Each line you keep should earn its space by showing a result a reader could, in principle, verify with a manager, a transcript, or a record.

## Learning Targets

- Convert a duty statement into an accomplishment statement built from a strong action verb plus a quantified, truthful result.
- Tailor resume claims to a specific role so a reader sees fit in the top third of the page.
- Disclose any AI editing help with a note that records what you accepted and what you rejected.
- Keep every claim defensible: write nothing you could not back up if a reference checker asked.

## Opening Case

Maya is applying for a part-time front-desk job at the campus recreation center. Her draft resume says, "Responsible for the front desk at the rec center." It is true, but it tells the reader nothing about whether she was good at it. A second applicant wrote, "Processed about 60 member check-ins per shift with zero equipment losses across 14 shifts." Same job, very different signal. The second line gives the reader numbers to picture and a result to trust.

The difference is not confidence or fancy wording. It is evidence. Maya did the same work; she just had not yet translated it into a claim a reader could weigh.

## Core Concept

A professional resume line is an accomplishment statement, and accomplishment statements follow a reliable shape: **action verb + task + quantified result**. The verb signals what you did (processed, designed, co-led), the task names the work, and the number turns a vague impression into a measurable outcome. This is why [NACE career readiness](https://www.naceweb.org/career-readiness/competencies/career-readiness-defined) lists communication and professionalism as core competencies: employers are not just buying your time, they are buying your ability to make your contribution legible.

Three rules separate strong resume identity from weak resume identity.

| Principle | Weak version | Strong version |
|-----------|--------------|----------------|
| Result over duty | "Responsible for social media" | "Grew club Instagram from 80 to 240 followers in one semester" |
| Truthful and verifiable | "Expert event planner" | "Co-led a 4-person team that ran one 60-person event" |
| Role fit in the top third | Generic objective line | A summary aimed at the exact posting's keywords |

Truthfulness is not optional polish; it is the foundation. A resume claim is a promise that survives a reference check. "Managed all operations" when you staffed one desk is not an exaggeration, it is a claim that collapses the moment someone calls your supervisor. Role fit is the second discipline: the same true accomplishment can be ordered and worded to match a posting, because a reader spends most of their attention on the top third of the page.

## Worked Example

The weak version below sounds plausible, but it does not yet give a reader enough evidence:

> I made the resume bullet clearer.

That claim is too thin because "clearer" does not name the verb, the result, or the source you could verify. A stronger revision shows the actual line that changed and why the new wording is both more useful and still truthful.

### Worked Example: revising one resume bullet, step by step

Take a real before/after on a single bullet for the campus rec-center job.

1. **Before (duty only):** `Responsible for the front desk at the rec center.`
2. **Name the result you can verify.** You checked in members and logged equipment. A printout shows roughly 60 check-ins per shift and zero lost-equipment reports during your 14 shifts.
3. **Pick an action verb + number.** Verb: `Processed`. Number: `60 member check-ins per shift`. Outcome: `zero equipment losses across 14 shifts`.
4. **After (result, truthful, verifiable):** `Processed about 60 member check-ins per shift and tracked equipment with zero recorded losses across 14 shifts.`
5. **AI-use note.** You asked AI to tighten the wording. It suggested `Managed all rec center operations` (false: you did not manage operations). You **reject** that line, log it as rejected, and keep the truthful version.

Result: the bullet went from a duty with no evidence to one verb, two verifiable numbers, and a logged AI decision a reader can audit.

## Decision Rule

For any resume line, run this test before you keep it: Can a reference checker confirm it, and does it help the specific role? If a manager you actually worked for would nod when read the line, it is defensible. If it helps a reader picture you succeeding in the posted job, it earns the top third of the page. A line that is true but irrelevant gets moved down or cut; a line that is impressive but unverifiable gets rewritten until it is honest.

1. **Verb-and-number check:** Does the line start with an action verb and contain at least one concrete result?
2. **Reference check:** Would your former supervisor confirm this exact wording without flinching?
3. **Fit check:** Does this line match a real requirement in the posting you are targeting?

## Common Misreadings

The most common mistake is inflating a claim to sound senior. "Spearheaded a company-wide initiative" reads as a red flag on a freshman resume because the reader knows the scope does not match the stage. A smaller, true claim ("organized a 12-person study group that met weekly for a semester") is stronger precisely because it is believable.

A second misreading is treating the AI editor as a co-author whose suggestions are facts. AI can tighten phrasing, but it does not know what you actually did. When it invents a number or a title, accepting it silently turns your resume into fiction. The disclosure note exists so that your judgment, not the tool's guess, stays the source of truth.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Imagine you are converting an entire "Experience" section, not just one line. Start by listing every duty you can remember from a job or activity. Next to each duty, write the result: what changed because you were there? If you cannot find a result, ask whether the duty belongs on the resume at all. Then attach a number wherever one honestly exists: how many people, how often, how much, how long. Numbers do not have to be large; "tutored 3 students twice a week for 10 weeks" is more persuasive than "tutored students" because it is concrete.

Once the lines carry results, reorder them for the target role. If you are applying to a data-entry job, the check-in throughput line goes first; if you are applying to a leadership role, the co-led-a-team line leads. The same true facts produce two different resumes because role fit decides the order. Finally, add the AI-use note as a short line in your submission record, not on the resume itself, so an instructor or employer can see that the wording is yours even where a tool helped you polish it.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read other people's resumes, practice spotting which lines would survive a reference check and which would not. Keep a two-column note: on the left, the claim as written; on the right, the evidence that would have to exist for it to be true. A line like "increased revenue by 30%" demands a baseline, a final figure, and a time window. If those are missing, the claim is decoration, not evidence.

A strong reader of professional presence can answer four questions about any line:

1. What did this person actually do, in plain language?
2. What result proves it mattered?
3. Could that result be confirmed by a third party?
4. Does the line fit the job being applied for?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

Your before-after revision is strong when a future employer can read the "after" line and predict how you would perform in the role without needing you to explain. Treat formatting (fonts, spacing, bullet symbols) as the last layer. The earlier layers are the verb, the result, the truthfulness, and the role fit. If a line lacks a result, mark it and ask BigAL to help you find the measurable outcome hidden inside the duty rather than asking it to make the line "sound better."

A useful note for your submission would read: "I rewrote the rec-center bullet from a duty into a result with two verifiable numbers. The AI editor suggested an inflated 'managed all operations' line; I rejected it because I could not back it up in a reference check, and a false claim is worse than a modest one."

## Transfer Case

Now move the same skill to a different surface: your LinkedIn headline and your email signature. A resume bullet, a LinkedIn "About" sentence, and a signature line are all the same identity problem under different space limits. On LinkedIn you have room for a sentence of context, so the rec-center accomplishment might read, "Reliable front-desk operator who kept equipment loss at zero across a busy semester." In an email signature you have only a phrase, so it shrinks to "CAT 100 student | campus rec-center staff." The truthful core never changes; the constraint (length, audience, formality) decides how much of the evidence you show. If you understand identity as evidence rather than as decoration, you can re-cut it for any surface without lying to fit the space.
