# Evidence of Digital Citizenship

![Evidence of Digital Citizenship textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat100-supplement/01-07-evidence-of-digital-citizenship.png)

## Why This Section Matters

Claiming a skill and proving a skill are different acts. "I'm good with technology" is a claim anyone can make; a captioned screenshot that shows two-factor authentication enabled on your email, mapped to the words "account safety," is proof. This section is about building that proof: a small portfolio where each item is an artifact tied to a specific competency, so a reviewer can verify the skill instead of taking your word for it.

The work product is a portfolio entry that links one artifact to one learning target with a caption and a short before-after note. A portfolio is not a folder of files; it is an argument that you can do specific things, with the evidence attached to each claim. Twenty unlabeled screenshots prove nothing; one well-captioned, well-mapped artifact proves a skill.

## Learning Targets

- Map a chosen artifact to a specific digital-citizenship competency.
- Write a caption that states which skill the artifact demonstrates and how.
- Add a before-after note that shows growth, not just a finished state.
- Curate: choose the few strongest pieces of evidence rather than collecting everything.

## Opening Case

Lin needs to show she learned account safety this module. She has two options. Option one: write "I got better at digital safety" in a reflection. Option two: include a screenshot of her two-factor authentication switched on, captioned "Evidence for account safety: enabled app-based 2FA on my school email so a leaked password alone cannot grant access," plus a note that she previously reused one password across three sites.

Option one is a claim; option two is evidence. A reviewer can open the screenshot, read the caption, and confirm the skill without asking Lin to explain. The artifact does the proving.

## Core Concept

A portfolio works by the same logic as a resume bullet or a security checklist: a claim is only as strong as the evidence a third party can inspect. A strong portfolio entry has four parts that turn a file into proof.

| Part | Question it answers | Example |
|------|---------------------|---------|
| Target | Which competency does this show? | "Account safety" |
| Artifact | What is the evidence? | Screenshot of 2FA enabled |
| Caption | How does the artifact show the skill? | "Enabled app-based 2FA on my email" |
| Before-after | What changed (growth)? | "Was reusing one password; now unique + 2FA" |

The before-after note is what separates a portfolio from a trophy case. A finished artifact shows a state; a before-after shows learning. Reviewers, employers, and instructors are evaluating growth and capability, so the most valuable entries make the change visible. The second key habit is curation: a portfolio of three sharp, mapped artifacts beats a dump of fifty files, because each strong entry carries an explicit claim while a pile carries none.

## Worked Example

The weak version below sounds plausible, but it does not yet give a reader enough evidence:

> I improved my digital citizenship skills.

That sentence is too thin because it names no competency, no artifact, and no change. A reviewer has nothing to inspect. A strong entry shows the target, the artifact, the caption that links them, and the before-after that proves growth.

### Worked Example: building one portfolio entry, step by step

Turn a finished task into a portfolio entry that proves a digital citizenship skill.

1. **Pick the learning target.** Target: account safety.
2. **Choose one artifact.** A screenshot of your two-factor authentication turned on for your school email.
3. **Write the caption that maps it to the target.** `Evidence for "account safety": enabled app-based 2FA on my school email so a leaked password alone cannot grant access.`
4. **Add a short process note.** `Before: same password reused across three sites. After: unique passphrase plus 2FA. Decided after the Privacy section showed how breaches spread.`
5. **Confirm the claim is backed.** The reflection says "I improved my account safety," and the linked screenshot plus note proves it.

Result: one item, captioned, mapped to a target, with a before/after a reviewer can open. That is stronger than twenty unlabeled screenshots.

## Decision Rule

For every item you consider adding, ask whether it proves a named skill to someone who has never met you. If the artifact, caption, and note together let a stranger verify the competency, it earns a place. If the item is just a screenshot with no claim attached, either give it a target and caption or leave it out. Quantity is not the goal; verifiable claims are.

1. **Mapping check:** Does this artifact tie to a specific competency, not a vague "skills"?
2. **Caption check:** Does the caption explain how the artifact demonstrates the skill?
3. **Growth check:** Does a before-after note show what changed?

## Common Misreadings

A common mistake is treating volume as strength: adding every file from the course in the belief that more evidence is more convincing. The opposite is true. An unlabeled pile forces the reviewer to guess what each item proves, and a guess is not proof. A curated set of a few mapped artifacts is read in full and believed; a dump is skimmed and discounted.

A second misreading is showing only finished products with no before. A polished final artifact can be impressive, but it does not show that you learned anything, since it could have been easy for you all along. The before-after note is what demonstrates growth, which is what a portfolio is meant to evidence.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Imagine assembling a four-entry portfolio for Module 1. You map one entry to each major skill: a resume before-after for professional presence, an AI-use disclosure for responsible AI use, a verification log for prompting with verification, and a 2FA screenshot for account safety. For each, you attach the artifact, write a caption naming the competency, and add a before-after note. The disclosure entry, for instance, is captioned "Evidence for responsible AI use: disclosed brainstorming help and rejected a thesis rewrite," with a note that an earlier draft had only said "I used AI a little."

The walkthrough's lesson is that a portfolio is built claim-first, not file-first. You start from the competencies you want to prove, then select the single best artifact for each. This keeps the portfolio small and every entry load-bearing. A reviewer reading it can confirm four distinct skills in a few minutes, which is exactly the outcome a portfolio exists to produce.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

When you review a portfolio (yours or a peer's), read each entry and ask, "What skill does this prove, and could I confirm it without the author present?" If an entry has no named target or no caption, it is a file, not evidence. If it shows only a finished state, it proves a state but not learning. Strong entries pass both tests.

A strong reader of a portfolio can answer four questions:

1. Is each artifact mapped to a specific competency?
2. Does each caption explain how the artifact shows the skill?
3. Does each entry show growth, not just a finished product?
4. Is the set curated, or is it an unfiltered dump?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

Your portfolio entry is strong when a stranger could verify the named competency from the artifact, caption, and note alone. The earlier layers are the target mapping, the artifact choice, and the before-after note; the last layer is presentation. If you are unsure which artifact best proves a skill, mark it in your trace and ask BigAL to help you compare two candidate artifacts against the competency rather than asking it to write the reflection for you.

A useful note would read: "For account safety I chose the 2FA screenshot over a generic 'I'm careful online' statement, because the screenshot is verifiable. The caption maps it to the competency and the before-after note shows I moved from reused passwords to unique passwords plus 2FA."

## Transfer Case

Move the portfolio skill into a job-application setting, where the "portfolio" becomes a set of work samples or a LinkedIn featured section. The four-part structure transfers exactly: a hiring manager wants to see a specific competency, an artifact that proves it, a caption explaining your role, and ideally evidence of impact or growth. The constraint shifts because the audience is busier and more skeptical than an instructor, so curation matters even more; you might show three samples, not ten. The principle, evidence over assertion, mapped and captioned, does not change between a class portfolio and a professional one. Learning to build claim-first, verifiable evidence here is what lets you assemble a convincing work portfolio later without starting over.
