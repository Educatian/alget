# Slide Structure Before Decoration

![Slide Structure Before Decoration textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat100-supplement/05-02-slide-structure-before-decoration.png)

## Why This Section Matters

When a slide is hard to follow, the usual instinct is to add: a brighter theme, an icon, an animation. That instinct treats a structure problem as a decoration problem, and it almost always makes the slide worse. A slide whose title is the word "Results" cannot be rescued by a good color palette, because the reader still does not know what the result was.

This section teaches you to build the message first and the look last. The core technique is the assertion headline (a complete claim in the title bar) supported by a small number of evidence points, with theme and styling applied only after the message survives on its own. The artifact, your presentation storyboard, is where you can show a graders or a teammate that the structure carries the point before any styling exists.

## Opening Case

A team rehearses a deck and keeps explaining each slide out loud: "So this one is about response times, and basically the point is it got faster." If you have to narrate the point of a slide, the slide is not carrying the point; you are. The fix is not a transition or a stock photo. The fix is to put the spoken sentence ("Response time dropped from 4.2s to 1.1s after caching") into the title bar, where it belongs, and let the bullets become the proof.

## Core Concept

A slide has two jobs: state one idea and prove it. The structure that does both is the **assertion-evidence** pattern. The title is a full sentence that asserts the slide's single claim. The body holds the minimum evidence that supports that claim, typically two or three items, not six. Everything else (theme, font, icon, animation) is decoration, which is legitimate only after the assertion and evidence already work in plain text.

Two failure modes are worth naming because they look productive but are not:

- **Label titles.** A title like "Results," "Overview," or "Data" names a topic instead of making a claim. It forces the reader to hunt the bullets for the point, and it lets vague filler bullets survive because nothing has to support a real assertion.
- **Decoration-first revision.** Choosing a theme, then trying to fit a message into it, inverts the dependency. The theme should serve the message; when it leads, you end up protecting a pretty layout instead of a clear point.

This is the data-ink idea applied to slides: every visual element should carry meaning. An assertion headline carries the claim; the supporting points carry the proof; a decorative element that carries neither is ink the reader has to wade through. The structural test is brutal and useful: strip all styling, leave black text on white, and ask whether the slide still makes its point. If it does not, no theme will save it.

## Worked Example

Goal: fix one slide whose message is buried, before any theme is applied.

Weak slide (decoration-first):

```
Title: Results
- We did a survey
- There were many responses
- The tool was used by the team
- It saved some time
- People liked it
- More details in the report
```

Structured rewrite (message-first). Replace the label title with an assertion headline and keep at most three supporting points:

```
Title: Response time dropped from 4.2s to 1.1s after caching
- 4,000 requests measured before and after
- Same hardware, same test script
- Recommendation: ship caching to production
```

Steps:

1. Read the old title "Results"; it states no point, so rewrite it as a claim with a number.
2. Cut the six vague bullets to three that support that one claim.
3. Only now consider a theme.

Notice what the structure forced out. "People liked it" had no number and supported no claim, so it could not survive once the title became a real assertion. The headline now carries the message, and a reader gets the point even if the projector renders the slide in plain black and white.

## Decision Rule

Build the message before the look: write the title as a complete claim, attach only the evidence that supports it, and apply styling last. If stripping the theme breaks the slide, the slide was never structured; it was decorated.

Use this check when a slide looks finished but may still be hiding its point:

1. **Purpose check:** Read only the title. Does it state a claim a reader could agree or disagree with?
2. **Evidence check:** Does each remaining point support that exact claim, and could any be cut without losing the proof?
3. **Revision check:** What label title did you turn into an assertion, and which bullets did you drop and why?

If the title is a topic word rather than a claim, fix the structure before you touch the design.

## Common Misreadings

One misreading is thinking the assertion-evidence rule means "fewer words always wins." The goal is not minimal text; it is a title that makes a claim and evidence that proves it. A three-word title that says nothing is worse than a longer title that states the point.

A second misreading is accepting an AI or template suggestion because the result looks more polished, without checking whether it preserved the claim. A redesign that centers a beautiful image but demotes the headline to a caption has traded the message for the look.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the pattern across a sequence, not just one slide. Take a five-slide deck and read only the titles in order, ignoring everything else. If the titles by themselves tell a coherent story (claim, claim, claim), the deck is structured. If the titles read "Intro, Background, Data, Results, Conclusion," you have a labeled outline, not an argument, and a busy reader skimming the title bar learns nothing.

Now rewrite the title row alone: "Our reports took two days, so analysts fell behind" leading to "Caching cut response time to 1.1s" leading to "We recommend shipping caching this sprint." You have not touched a single theme or image, yet the deck now makes its case from the titles down. That is the order the work belongs in: structure that stands on its own first, decoration on top of it second.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: on the left, each slide's title; on the right, whether it is a claim or a label and what evidence supports it. Flag every label title and every bullet that supports nothing. By the end of 05.02, your note should let a reader see the deck's argument from the titles alone.

A strong reader of this section can answer four questions:

1. Which titles are claims and which are just topic labels?
2. For each claim, what is the minimum evidence that proves it?
3. Which element is decoration that carries no meaning?
4. Would the slide still make its point with all styling removed?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The storyboard is strong when its titles, read alone, carry the argument, and every supporting point earns its place. Treat the theme as the final coat, applied only after the plain-text version already works. If a slide depends on its styling to make sense, mark the gap and rebuild the structure first.

A useful note for the artifact would sound like this: "I changed slide 3's title from 'Results' to 'Response time dropped from 4.2s to 1.1s after caching' and cut three filler bullets that supported no claim. I accepted the AI's tighter wording of the recommendation, but rejected its suggestion to add an animated build, because the animation carried no meaning the static slide lacked."

## Transfer Case

Move the same content to a one-page printed handout with no slide software and no animation at all. The assertion-evidence structure transfers directly: the headline becomes the section heading, the supporting points become the body, and there is no theme to hide behind. If your slide collapses into a clear handout without losing anything, the structure was real. If it falls apart without its visual styling, the decoration was doing work the structure should have done.
