## Why This Section Matters

A slide that only some of your audience can read is a slide that failed for the rest. Low-contrast text disappears for anyone with reduced vision or on a sun-washed projector. A chart with no alternative text is silent to a screen-reader user. Status dots that mean "red equals risk" are invisible to the roughly one in twelve men with color vision deficiency. None of these problems show up when the designer, who can see the slide perfectly, looks at it; they show up only when you test against specific, measurable criteria.

This section teaches a concrete, repeatable accessibility pass built on the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines: check contrast against the 4.5:1 ratio, give every meaningful image alternative text, and never let color carry meaning alone. The artifact is a storyboard that records each check and the fix it triggered, so a reviewer can confirm the slide works for varied viewers.

## Opening Case

A team is proud of a sleek slide: light gray headings on white, a clean chart, and green and red dots showing project status. In the room, a reviewer with color blindness asks which projects are at risk, and cannot tell, because to him the dots look identical. A second reviewer squints at the gray heading and gives up. The slide looked elegant to the designers and excluded two people in the first row. Accessibility is not a finishing touch; it is whether the slide reaches its audience at all.

## Core Concept

Accessibility on slides is not subjective taste; it is a set of testable criteria, and three of them catch the large majority of real problems:

- **Contrast.** Text must stand out enough from its background to be legible. [WCAG](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/) sets a minimum contrast ratio of 4.5:1 for normal text (3:1 for large text). The ratio is computed from the relative luminance of the two colors; you do not eyeball it, you check it. Light gray on white, a common "elegant" choice, often lands near 1.9:1, far below the line.
- **Text alternatives.** Every image that carries meaning needs alt text that states the point, not the file type. "Chart" is useless; "Bar chart; signups rose from 1,200 to 2,400 after launch" conveys what a sighted viewer gets.
- **Do not rely on color alone.** If color is the only thing distinguishing two states (green vs. red, used vs. free), viewers with color vision deficiency cannot read it. Add a label, a shape, or a pattern so the meaning survives in grayscale.

These map directly to the data-ink idea but extend it: an element must not only carry meaning, it must carry that meaning to *every* viewer in the room. A useful gut-check is the grayscale test: if you converted the slide to grayscale, would it still be readable and would every distinction still be clear? If contrast collapses or the status dots merge, the slide was relying on something not everyone can perceive.

Current tooling (as of 2026) makes these checks cheap to run. PowerPoint ships a built-in Accessibility Checker (Review > Check Accessibility) that flags missing alt text and color-only issues, and on Copilot+ PCs (Microsoft 365 Version 2512 and later) Word and PowerPoint auto-generate alt-text suggestions on-device via the NPU, with a toggle at File > Options > Accessibility. Google Slides has no built-in checker and relies on add-ons such as Grackle Slides or Accessibility Checker for Slides. The free WebAIM Contrast Checker works standalone for any tool. Auto-generated alt text is a bounded AI move: verify it against what the chart actually shows before accepting it, and expect menu paths and build numbers to change.

## Worked Example

Goal: run a three-point accessibility check on one slide and fix what fails.

Starting slide:

```
Title color:  #BBBBBB on #FFFFFF background
Chart image:  no alt text
Status dots:  green = on track, red = at risk (no labels)
```

Steps:

1. Contrast: #BBBBBB on white is about 1.9:1, below the 4.5:1 minimum for normal text. To get the number, open a contrast checker (for example, the WebAIM Contrast Checker), paste #BBBBBB into Foreground Color and #FFFFFF into Background Color, and read the ratio; the tool instantly reports the number and a Pass/Fail against Normal Text (AA, 4.5:1) and Large Text (AA, 3:1). In PowerPoint, the built-in Accessibility Checker (Review > Check Accessibility) flags low contrast and missing alt text as you author. Darken the title to #1A2A4F, which exceeds 4.5:1.
2. Alt text: add a description that states the point, not just the type:

   `Alt: "Bar chart; signups rose from 1,200 to 2,400 after launch."`

3. Color-only meaning: a colorblind viewer cannot tell the dots apart, so add a label to each:

   `On track (green) / At risk (red)` with text labels next to the dots.

4. Re-check all three. Now the slide passes contrast, has alt text, and does not rely on color alone.

Each fix targets a different viewer. The contrast fix helps anyone with low vision or a bad projector; the alt text reaches screen-reader users; the labels reach colorblind viewers. The same "elegant" slide failed three different people in three different ways, which is why a single visual judgment ("looks clean") is not an accessibility check.

## Decision Rule

Test contrast against 4.5:1, give every meaningful image alt text that states the point, and never let color be the only carrier of meaning. If a fix would help only some viewers and exclude others, it is not finished.

Check a slide against three questions:

1. **Purpose check:** Can every viewer, including those with low vision or color blindness or using a screen reader, get the slide's point?
2. **Evidence check:** What is the measured contrast ratio, what does the alt text say, and what label backs each color-coded element?
3. **Revision check:** Which specific failure did you fix, and to which [WCAG](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/) criterion does it map?

If the slide depends on color, fine print, or an unlabeled image, it has not passed.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is treating accessibility as a stylistic preference ("I like high contrast") rather than a measurable threshold. 4.5:1 is a number you check, not a feeling; a slide either clears it or it does not.

The second is writing alt text that names the file instead of the meaning. "image1.png" or "chart" tells a screen-reader user nothing; the alt text must deliver the same information a sighted viewer takes from the visual.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the three checks to a slide with a photo and a caption overlaid on it, a common design that quietly fails. Contrast: white caption text over a busy photo can swing from readable to invisible depending on the pixels behind each letter, so you add a solid or darkened band behind the text to guarantee the ratio. Alt text: the photo either carries meaning (then describe it) or is decorative (then mark it as such so the screen reader skips it rather than announcing a filename). Color-only: if the caption uses a colored word to flag something, add a non-color cue.

Now stress-test with the grayscale conversion. Drop the slide to grayscale and look again: the caption band keeps the text legible, the decorative photo is correctly skipped by assistive tech, and no meaning was hiding in a color that just turned gray. If the slide survives grayscale and a screen-reader pass, it will survive the actual room, including the viewers the designer cannot see the slide as.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: on the left, each visual element; on the right, its contrast ratio, alt text, and whether any meaning rests on color alone. Flag every element that fails one of the three checks. By the end of 05.07, your note should show that the slide was tested, not just admired.

A strong reader of this section can answer four questions:

1. What is the measured contrast ratio of the text against its background?
2. Does every meaningful image have alt text that states the point?
3. Does any meaning rest on color alone, and how is it backed up?
4. Would the slide survive a grayscale and screen-reader pass?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The storyboard is strong when a reviewer can see, for each slide, the contrast ratio, the alt text, and the non-color cue for any coded element, plus the fix each check triggered. Treat "it looks clean to me" as the start of the check, not the result.

A useful note would sound like this: "I changed the title from #BBBBBB (about 1.9:1) to #1A2A4F (above 4.5:1) to meet [WCAG](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/) contrast, added alt text stating the signup increase, and labeled the status dots so the meaning does not rely on green versus red. I rejected a suggestion to use a lighter, trendier title color, because it would have dropped contrast back below the minimum."

## Transfer Case

Move the same content to a printed handout that may be photocopied in black and white. Two of the three checks transfer immediately and one gets harder: contrast still matters (and copies degrade it further), color-only meaning becomes fatal because the copy is grayscale by definition, and alt text has no role since there is no screen reader for paper. If your slide already passed the grayscale test, the handout works; if it relied on color, the photocopy will strip the meaning out entirely.
