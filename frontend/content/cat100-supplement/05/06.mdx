# Citation and Attribution

![Citation and Attribution textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat100-supplement/05-06-citation-and-attribution.png)

## Why This Section Matters

A presentation makes claims, borrows charts, and increasingly leans on AI to draft text. Each of those is a debt to a source, and citation is how you record the debt so a skeptical viewer can check it. "Found online" is not a citation; it is an admission that you did not trace the source. A figure with no verifiable origin is a figure the audience has no reason to trust, and an AI-drafted paragraph passed off as your own is a different problem entirely, an integrity failure rather than a sloppiness one.

This section teaches the practical mechanics of attribution on a slide: tracing a borrowed asset to its real source, checking its license, verifying that the number you quote actually appears where you point, and disclosing AI assistance where it is required. The artifact is a privacy-and-attribution checklist that makes each of those checks visible.

## Learning Targets

- Apply the four attribution obligations (trace the source, check the license, verify the exact figure or quote location, disclose AI authorship) to a borrowed chart, a quotation, and an AI-generated asset.
- Distinguish citation (making a borrowed fact or asset checkable) from AI-use disclosure (making your own slide's authorship honest), and distinguish public-domain government works from CC-licensed third-party assets.
- Document a bounded AI/software support move with accepted and rejected suggestions.
- Use feedback, support requests, and revision notes to improve the artifact before sharing it.

## Opening Case

A student drops a striking unemployment chart onto a slide; her note to herself says "found on Google." During the presentation a faculty member asks, "What year is that data, and who published it?" She cannot answer, and the strong claim built on the chart collapses in front of the room. A classmate who captioned the same chart "U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025 (public domain, U.S. government work)" answers the question in one breath, and his claim stands. The difference was not design; it was whether the source could be traced.

## Core Concept

Attribution answers three separate questions, and weak slides confuse them:

- **Where did this come from?** The source: who produced the asset and when. A chart, a photo, a statistic, and a quotation each need a traceable origin, not "online."
- **Am I allowed to use it this way?** The license. A CC BY image can be reused with credit; an "all rights reserved" image cannot be reposted just because you found it. Public-domain government data (like BLS releases) is broadly reusable but still must be cited so the audience can verify it.
- **Is the number actually there?** Verification. Citing a page is not enough if the figure you quote does not appear on it. Point to the exact table or line that states the value.

AI assistance adds a fourth obligation: **disclosure**. If AI drafted or substantially shaped your text, and the assignment or context expects you to say so, you disclose it. Disclosure is not a confession of weakness; it is the honest labeling of who wrote what, the same way a citation labels who produced a chart. The line that matters: citation makes a borrowed *fact or asset* checkable, while disclosure makes the *authorship* of your own slide honest. Both are required, and they are not the same move. Disclosure wording should follow your course's stated AI-use policy or syllabus statement; expectations are evolving and vary by instructor. Current style guides give concrete models: APA (updated September 2025) cites the specific AI chat with the company as author, the model named, and a date, and MLA recommends naming the prompt, tool, model, and date.

The course anchor doing the work here is [ISTE digital citizenship](https://iste.org/standards/students), which treats verifiable sourcing and honest disclosure as conditions for participating in a shared information space, not as bureaucratic box-checking.

## Worked Example

Goal: attribute a reused chart and an AI-drafted summary correctly on one slide.

Starting state (under-credited):

```
[chart image]          <- "found online"
Summary paragraph      <- drafted by AI, lightly reworded
```

Steps:

1. Trace the chart to its real source and check the license. It is from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025, in the public domain as a U.S. government work (cite BLS as the source).
2. Add a caption that names source, year, and license:

   `Chart: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025 (public domain, U.S. government work).`

3. Verify the figure you quote actually appears at the linked page. The 18% you cited was not on that page, so swap the link to the exact table that states it.
4. Disclose AI assistance where the assignment requires it:

   `Summary drafted with AI assistance (e.g., ChatGPT, Claude, or Copilot; [date]), edited and verified by me.`

Step 3 is the one students skip and reviewers catch. The chart had a real source, but the specific 18 percent figure lived on a different table than the page you linked, which means a checker following your citation would not find your number. Citing the right document but the wrong page is a near-miss that still fails verification.

## Decision Rule

Trace every borrowed asset to its real source, confirm the license permits your use, verify the exact figure where you point, and disclose AI authorship where it is expected. If a viewer cannot follow your citation to the precise claim, the citation is incomplete.

Check a slide's attribution against three questions:

1. **Purpose check:** Could a skeptical viewer reach the original source and confirm it is yours to use this way?
2. **Evidence check:** Does the exact number, quote, or asset appear at the precise location you cite, not just on the general site?
3. **Revision check:** What did you re-source, relicense-check, or disclose, and what suggestion did you reject?

If the citation lands on a homepage rather than the specific table or figure, tighten it before you present.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is that linking the general website counts as citing the figure. A viewer who follows the link must land where the number actually is; a homepage link makes the claim unverifiable in practice.

The second is that lightly rewording AI-drafted text removes the need to disclose. Paraphrasing changes the words, not the authorship; if AI shaped the content and disclosure is expected, rewording does not cancel the obligation.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the four questions to a quotation rather than a chart. You want to quote a researcher's line on your slide. Source: name the author and the work, not "an expert said." License and use: a short attributed quotation for commentary is generally fine, but reproducing a long passage may not be, so you keep it brief. Verification: paste the exact words and confirm they match the original, because misquoting an attributed source is worse than not quoting at all. Disclosure: if AI helped you find or summarize the quote, you still verified the original yourself, and you say so if required.

Now suppose the same slide also uses an AI-generated image. The attribution logic forks: a borrowed human-made asset needs source, license, and verification, while an AI-generated image needs a different note ("image generated with AI"), because there is no external creator to credit but the audience still deserves to know it is synthetic. One slide, two kinds of honesty: crediting what you borrowed and labeling what a machine produced.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: on the left, each borrowed or AI-touched element on the slide; on the right, its source, license, verification location, and any disclosure. Flag anything marked "found online" or "AI-drafted" without a check. By the end of 05.06, your note should let a reviewer follow every claim to its origin.

A strong reader of this section can answer four questions:

1. For each asset, who produced it and when?
2. Does the license allow your specific use?
3. Does the exact figure appear at the precise place you cite?
4. Where did AI shape the content, and is that disclosed?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The checklist is strong when a reviewer can trace every borrowed asset to its source and license, confirm each quoted figure at its exact location, and see where AI assistance is disclosed. Treat a polished caption as worthless if the link behind it does not reach the claim.

A useful entry would sound like this: "I replaced 'found online' with 'U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025 (public domain, U.S. government work)' and re-pointed the link from the homepage to the exact table, because my 18 percent figure was not on the page I first cited. I disclosed that the summary was AI-drafted and verified by me, and I rejected a suggestion to drop the disclosure to look more original, because that would misrepresent authorship."

## Transfer Case

Move the same content into a printed handout with no clickable links. Attribution must still be traceable, so the caption now needs enough text for a reader to find the source without a hyperlink: full publisher, year, table number, and access note. The verification burden does not shrink because the medium changed; it grows, because the reader cannot click through. If your citation works on paper, it was a real citation, not a link standing in for one.
