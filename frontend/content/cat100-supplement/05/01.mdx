# Presentation Purpose and Audience

![Presentation Purpose and Audience textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat100-supplement/05-01-presentation-purpose-and-audience.png)

## Why This Section Matters

Most weak presentations fail before a single slide is designed, because the author never decided who the audience is or what that audience must be able to do afterward. A deck built for "everyone" persuades no one. The same data that should make a hiring panel say "invite this candidate" will bore a classmate and confuse a parent, because each of those audiences arrives with different questions, different prior knowledge, and a different decision to make.

In this section you learn to write a one-sentence purpose statement and a one-sentence audience profile before you open slide software, then to size and shape the deck to fit them. The artifact you produce, a presentation storyboard with an explicit audience constraint and slide-level evidence, is the place where those two sentences become visible and checkable by someone else.

## Learning Targets

## Opening Case

Two students submit decks on the same project. Maya writes at the top of her storyboard: "Audience: the analyst-internship hiring panel (three working analysts, five minutes per candidate). Purpose: they should rank me in their top three to interview." Devon writes nothing; he opens PowerPoint and starts choosing a theme.

Maya's first slide leads with "Cleaned 4,000 rows and cut report time 40 percent," because that is what a hiring analyst weighs. Devon's first slide is a title slide with his name in a script font. Both decks are finished and both look fine. Only Maya can tell you what each slide is for, and only Maya's storyboard lets a reviewer check whether the slides actually serve the stated audience.

## Core Concept

Purpose and audience are two separate decisions that people routinely collapse into one. **Purpose** is the action you want the audience to take: interview me, fund the referral feature, approve the budget, change the deadline. **Audience** is the specific reader and the constraints they bring: how many of them, how much time, how much background, and what would make them say no. A purpose without a named audience drifts into generic information dumping; an audience without a stated purpose drifts into "here is everything I did."

Once both are fixed, they generate every later choice. Three properties of the audience set hard limits on the deck:

| Audience property | What it constrains | Example consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time available | Number of slides and depth | 3 minutes at roughly 1 slide/minute means about 3 slides, not 12 |
| Prior knowledge | What you can assume vs. must explain | Working analysts need no "what is a spreadsheet" slide |
| The decision they own | Which evidence belongs and which gets cut | A hiring panel weighs skill match, not your favorite class |

This is why "audience first, evidence second, tool third" is not a slogan. The audience determines which evidence is relevant, and only after the relevant evidence is chosen does the tool (theme, layout, AI assistant) have anything legitimate to do. The course anchor that does the heaviest work here is NACE career readiness, which asks whether the artifact would let a professional decision-maker act; the data-ink anchor matters later, once you know what the audience needs to see.

## Worked Example

Goal: build a 3-minute pitch deck for a campus hiring panel that should leave thinking "invite this candidate."

1. Name the audience and purpose in one line: *Audience = analyst-internship hiring panel; Purpose = earn an interview in 3 minutes.*
2. Size the deck to the time. At roughly one minute per slide, that is 3 slides, not 12.
3. Draft the storyboard so each slide answers a panel question:

   | Slide | Panel question | Takeaway headline |
   | --- | --- | --- |
   | 1 | Who are you and why care? | "I turned messy survey data into a decision the team used." |
   | 2 | Can you do the work? | "Cleaned 4,000 rows, cut report time 40%." |
   | 3 | Do you fit this role? | "These three skills match the internship posting." |

4. Audit against the purpose line. A "favorite class project" slide does not help the panel decide to interview, so it is cut.

The cut is the evidence of judgment. Anyone can add slides; the storyboard shows that you removed a slide because it did not serve the named audience. That is the difference between a deck that is finished and a deck that is aimed.

## Decision Rule

Set the audience and purpose before any design choice, then justify every slide by the question it answers for that audience. A slide that does not move the audience toward the purpose is decoration, no matter how good it looks.

Before submitting the presentation storyboard with audience constraint and slide-level evidence, check whether the storyboard can answer three questions:

1. **Purpose check:** What single action should this audience take after the deck, and does the last slide ask for it?
2. **Evidence check:** For each slide, which panel or audience question does it answer, and what number or example backs the headline?
3. **Revision check:** Which slide did you cut or rewrite because it did not serve this audience, and why?

If you cannot name the audience question each slide answers, the deck is built for no one in particular.

## Common Misreadings

The most common error is treating "audience" as a demographic label ("college students") rather than as a decision-maker with limited time and a specific call to make. A label tells you nothing about what to put on a slide; a decision and a time budget tell you almost everything.

A second misreading is believing a longer, more detailed deck is safer because it "covers more." Coverage is the opposite of purpose. Every extra slide spends the audience's limited attention and dilutes the one action you want.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply this to a real reassignment. Suppose your hiring-panel deck must now be sent as a self-running file to a busy recruiter who will skim it on a phone with no narration. The purpose can stay the same (earn an interview), but two audience properties just changed: there is no live time budget you control, and there is no voice to carry meaning. Your storyboard has to absorb that.

You would move the spoken context into the slide itself, so the headline "Cut report time 40 percent" now carries its own one-line proof beneath it, because no narrator will say it. You might add a single closing slide with explicit contact and availability, since the recruiter cannot ask you live. You are not redesigning for taste; you are re-deriving the deck from a changed audience constraint, which is exactly the skill the storyboard is meant to demonstrate.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: on the left, the audience question; on the right, the slide and evidence that answer it. If a slide has no audience question, flag it as a candidate to cut. By the end of 05.01, your note should let a reviewer reconstruct why each surviving slide exists.

A strong reader of this section can answer four questions:

1. Who is the specific decision-maker, and how much time do they have?
2. What single action defines success?
3. Which evidence does that audience actually weigh, and which did you cut?
4. How would the deck change if the audience's time, knowledge, or decision changed?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The storyboard is strong when a stranger can read your one-line audience and purpose statements, then verify that every slide earns its place against them. Treat theme and font as the last layer. The earlier, load-bearing layers are the audience definition, the per-slide question, and the cut you made.

A useful note for the artifact would sound like this: "I cut the 'favorite project' slide because the hiring panel weighs skill match, not enthusiasm, and at one minute per slide it would have crowded out the 40 percent result. I kept the AI's suggestion to add a metric to slide 2, but rejected its suggestion to add a fourth slide, because three minutes leaves no room for it."

## Transfer Case

Take the same content to a stricter setting: a 60-second elevator version for a career fair, spoken, with no slides at all. The purpose holds (earn a follow-up), but the constraint collapses from three slides to roughly 150 spoken words. You must now choose the single strongest piece of evidence and drop the rest. If you can explain which slide survives as your one sentence and why, you understand purpose and audience as a relationship you can re-derive, not a template you copied.
