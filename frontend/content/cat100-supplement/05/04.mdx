# AI Narration Pathways

![AI Narration Pathways textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat100-supplement/05-04-ai-narration-pathways.png)

## Why This Section Matters

A narrated slide gives the audience two channels at once: the words on screen and the voice in their ears. When those channels say the same thing, you waste half the bandwidth and you trigger the redundancy effect, where reading and listening to identical text actually makes comprehension worse, not better. The voice should add what the slide cannot show: the cause behind a number, the example behind a claim, the recommendation behind the chart.

This section also covers the practical pathways for getting narration onto a deck, from recording your own voice to generating it with a text-to-speech tool, and the obligations that come with each. The artifact is an AI critique log of the narration choices you accepted, modified, and rejected, including any AI-voice errors you caught and any disclosure the assignment requires.

## Opening Case

A student records narration by reading each slide aloud, word for word. The result feels redundant and slightly insulting, because the audience can read faster than he can speak and they finish each bullet before he does. A classmate records narration that never repeats a bullet; instead she says why the number happened and what she recommends. Same slides, same length, completely different value. The voice that adds reasoning is worth listening to; the voice that reads the screen is noise the audience would mute.

## Core Concept

Narration is a second information channel, and its job is to be **complementary**, not redundant. The slide shows the *what* (the chart, the headline, the bullet); the voice supplies the *why* and the *so what* that the slide leaves implicit. This is the multimedia principle in practice: words and visuals teach more together when each carries different, mutually supporting information, and they teach less when the audio merely recites the on-screen text.

A useful test is the **delete-the-overlap test**. Draft the script, then strike every sentence that only repeats visible text. What remains should be the cause, the example, the caveat, or the recommendation. If almost nothing remains, the narration was redundant and the slide did not need a voice at all.

The pathway you choose to produce the narration carries its own obligations:

| Pathway | Strength | Obligation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Record your own voice | Authentic, easy to add emphasis | Proofread audio; re-record stumbles |
| AI text-to-speech | Fast, consistent, easy to revise | Check pronunciation; disclose AI voice where required |
| AI-drafted script you read aloud | Fast first draft | Edit for accuracy and your own claims; you own every word |

AI voices mispronounce domain terms with total confidence ("cache" read as "kashay"), so an AI narration pathway adds a proofreading-by-ear step that a typed slide never needed. Disclosure is not optional decoration; where the assignment or audience expects to know a voice is synthetic, hiding it is a citizenship failure.

## Worked Example

Goal: write narration for a chart slide so the audio adds meaning instead of reading the screen.

On-screen text: a bar chart titled **"Signups doubled after launch."**

Weak narration (reads the slide):

> "Signups doubled after launch. As you can see, signups doubled after launch."

Stronger narration (adds the cause and the so-what):

> "The jump you see came from the referral feature we shipped in week two. That single change drove most of the doubling, which is why I recommend funding referrals next quarter."

Steps:

1. Draft the script with AI, then read it aloud against the slide.
2. Delete any line that repeats visible text.
3. Add the reason, an example, or the recommendation the chart cannot show.
4. Proofread the audio: the AI voice said "kashay" for "cache," so re-record that term and disclose AI narration where required.

The strong version never says "doubled" again, because the chart already said it. The voice spends its entire budget on the two things the chart cannot show: what caused the jump and what to do about it. That is complementary narration, and the mispronunciation catch in step 4 is exactly the kind of AI-pathway check your log should record.

## Decision Rule

Make the voice complementary to the slide, not redundant with it, and own every word the audience hears regardless of how it was produced. If the narration only reads the screen, cut it.

Check a narration pass against three questions:

1. **Purpose check:** After the audio, can the audience act, knowing the why and the so-what, not just the on-screen what?
2. **Evidence check:** Which sentences add cause, example, or recommendation, and which only repeat visible text?
3. **Revision check:** What redundant line did you cut, and what AI-voice error or disclosure did you handle?

If you removed nothing in the delete-the-overlap test, the narration was redundant.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is that reading the slide aloud is "safe" or "thorough." It is the redundancy effect in action: identical audio and text compete for the same working memory and lower comprehension. Thoroughness on screen plus reasoning in the voice is the goal, not the same words twice.

The second is treating an AI-generated voice as finished once it sounds smooth. Smooth and correct are different; a confident mispronunciation of a key term undermines credibility more than an obvious robotic tone would.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply complementary narration to a slide with no chart, just a single claim: "We should move the deadline to Friday." On screen, that is the whole message. The voice cannot repeat it, so it must supply the support the slide omits: "We moved it because the data load now finishes Thursday night, so a Friday deadline gives the team a full review day instead of zero." The audio carried the reasoning; the slide carried the decision.

Now suppose you generate that narration with text-to-speech. You run the audio once with your eyes closed and catch that the tool stressed the wrong word, flattening "Friday" into the middle of the sentence so the date got lost. You re-prompt for emphasis or re-record that clause, then add the required disclosure that the narration is AI-generated. The script was complementary; the pathway forced a listen-test and a disclosure that a silent slide never would.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: on the left, each narration sentence; on the right, whether it repeats the slide or adds cause, example, or recommendation. Flag every redundant line and every term the AI voice could mispronounce. By the end of 05.04, your note should show that the voice earns its place.

A strong reader of this section can answer four questions:

1. What does the voice add that the slide cannot show?
2. Which sentences are redundant with on-screen text?
3. Which pathway produced the audio, and what did that obligate you to check?
4. Where is AI narration disclosed, if the audience expects to know?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The critique log is strong when a reviewer can see which narration lines you cut for redundancy, which you added for reasoning, and which AI-voice errors you caught and fixed. Treat a smooth-sounding take as the starting point, not the finish line.

A useful entry would sound like this: "I cut the line that reread 'signups doubled' and replaced it with the cause (the week-two referral feature) and the recommendation (fund referrals). I accepted the AI voice for speed but rejected its first render because it pronounced 'cache' as 'kashay,' re-recorded that term, and added the required AI-narration disclosure on the closing slide."

## Transfer Case

Move the same content to a live, in-person talk with no recorded audio at all. The complementary-narration rule transfers directly: you still do not read your slides, you still speak the cause and the recommendation, and the slide still carries only the what. What drops away is the AI-pathway machinery, the pronunciation check and the disclosure, because there is no synthetic voice. If you can deliver the live version without reading a single bullet, your narration was complementary by design, not by accident.
