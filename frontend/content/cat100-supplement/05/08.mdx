# Module 5 Presentation Studio

![Module 5 Presentation Studio textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat100-supplement/05-08-module-5-presentation-studio.png)

## Why This Section Matters

The seven skills in this module are not a menu to pick from; they are a pipeline that runs in order, and skipping a stage breaks the ones downstream. If you choose a theme before you fix the slide structure, you protect a pretty layout instead of a clear message. If you check accessibility before you settle the content, you re-check it again after every edit. The capstone studio is where you stop practicing the moves in isolation and run the whole pipeline on a real deck, then attach a revision trail that proves you did.

This section gives you the assembly order and a single per-slide checklist that folds purpose, structure, media, narration, citation, and accessibility into one pass. The artifact is a complete storyboard with that revision trail, so a reviewer can inspect not just the finished deck but the decisions that produced it.

## Learning Targets

- Analyze how the presentation storyboard with audience constraint and slide-level evidence shows a defensible decision in CAT 100.
- Distinguish artifact polish from evidence-based revision.
- Document a bounded AI/software support move with accepted and rejected suggestions.
- Use feedback, support requests, and revision notes to improve the artifact before sharing it.

## Opening Case

A student has a deck that looks done: nice theme, smooth animations, confident narration. Run it through the pipeline and the cracks appear. Slide 3's title is the label "Results." The chart on slide 4 was "found online." The status colors on slide 5 fail in grayscale. None of these were visible to someone admiring the finish, because polish hides structural gaps. The studio's job is to make you walk every slide through the same six checks so the gaps surface before the audience finds them.

## Core Concept

Think of a deck as passing through six dependent layers, each building on the one before:

| Layer | Question it answers | Section it comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purpose | Who is the audience and what action do they take? | 05.01 |
| Structure | Does each title assert a claim the evidence proves? | 05.02 |
| Media | Does each image explain (and is it verified)? | 05.03 |
| Narration | Does the voice add reasoning, not reread the slide? | 05.04, 05.05 |
| Citation | Is every source and figure verifiable, AI disclosed? | 05.06 |
| Accessibility | Does it reach every viewer (contrast, alt text, not color-only)? | 05.07 |

The order is not arbitrary. Purpose decides which content belongs, so it comes first; there is no point structuring a slide the audience does not need. Structure decides what each slide claims, so media, narration, and citation can attach to a real assertion rather than a label. Accessibility comes last among the checks not because it matters least, but because it must be re-verified after content is final; checking contrast on a slide you are about to rewrite wastes the check.

What separates the capstone from the individual sections is the **revision trail**. Anyone can produce a deck; a defensible submission shows the earlier version, the gap each layer exposed, the support you used, and at least one suggestion you rejected with the reason. The trail is the evidence that you ran the pipeline rather than just arriving at a clean-looking result.

Goal: assemble the Module 5 capstone deck by walking the full pipeline, then attach a revision trail.

Pipeline checklist applied to one slide:

| Layer | Check | Result on slide 3 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purpose | Does it serve the hiring panel? | Yes, shows the analyst skill match |
| Structure | Assertion headline? | Fixed: "Results" -> "Cut report time 40%" |

Steps:

1. Run every slide through the six layers above; do not skip to polish.
2. Fix the two gaps found (the label headline and the failed contrast).
3. Attach a revision trail: the earlier slide 3, the contrast issue, the alt-text help used from BigAL, and one suggestion you rejected (an animated transition) with the reason it added no meaning.

Notice that two of the six layers caught problems on a single slide that already looked finished: structure (a label title) and accessibility (failed contrast). A deck that "looks done" routinely fails two or three layers per slide, which is exactly why the pass has to be systematic rather than a final glance.

## Decision Rule

Run every slide through the six layers in order, fix what each one exposes, and attach a revision trail that a reviewer can inspect. A finished-looking deck with no trail is a result without evidence of the work.

Check the assembled deck against three questions:

1. **Purpose check:** Does the whole deck drive the named audience toward one action, with no off-target slides?
2. **Evidence check:** For each slide, which of the six layers did you verify, and what did the failing layers expose?
3. **Revision check:** What gaps did the pipeline catch, and which suggestion did you reject and why?

If you cannot point to a gap the pipeline caught, you probably skimmed rather than ran it.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is treating the six layers as independent boxes to tick in any order. They are dependent; running them out of order forces rework, especially checking accessibility or applying a theme before the content is settled.

The second is believing a polished surface means the pipeline passed. Polish is the last and thinnest layer; it routinely hides a label title, an unverified chart, or a contrast failure underneath. The trail, not the finish, is the evidence.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the pipeline to a slide that passes the early layers but fails a late one, to see why order matters. Slide 5 has a strong purpose (it shows the cost saving the panel cares about) and a real assertion headline ("Caching saved 40 hours per month"). Media is fine, narration adds the cause, citation links to the exact log. Then accessibility catches it: the savings are encoded as a green-to-red heat strip with no labels, invisible to a colorblind panelist.

Because you ran the layers in order, this is a cheap fix at the end rather than a redo. The content is final, so you add value labels to the heat strip once and the check passes for good. Had you "done accessibility first," you would have labeled an earlier draft of the strip and then re-labeled it after the content changed. The walkthrough shows the payoff of the order: late checks land on stable content, and the revision trail records one clean fix instead of three.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: on the left, each of the six layers; on the right, the gap it caught on your deck and the fix. Flag any slide where you skipped a layer. By the end of 05.08, your note should be the backbone of the revision trail you submit.

A strong reader of this section can answer four questions:

1. Why must the six layers run in this order?
2. Which layer caught the gap that polish was hiding?
3. What does the revision trail show that the finished deck cannot?
4. Which suggestion did you reject, and why did it add no value?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The capstone storyboard is strong when a reviewer can walk the same six layers and see, for each, what you verified and what you fixed, plus a rejected suggestion with its reason. Treat the polished deck as the smallest part of the grade; the trail of decisions is the rest.

A useful note would sound like this: "Running the pipeline, structure caught a label title on slide 3 ('Results') which I rewrote to 'Cut report time 40 percent,' and accessibility caught a contrast failure on the same slide, which I fixed by darkening the title past 4.5:1. I used BigAL for alt-text wording and disclosed it. I rejected an animated transition suggestion, because it added motion but no meaning and could distract from the headline."

## Transfer Case

Suppose the final deliverable changes from a live pitch to a self-running file a recruiter opens alone. Re-run the same six layers and watch which results shift: purpose holds, structure holds, but narration must move into the slides (no live voice), and accessibility gets stricter (no presenter to clarify). The pipeline is the transferable asset; the deck it produces changes with the constraint. If you can re-derive the deck by re-running the layers rather than restarting, you have learned the studio as a process, not a one-time assignment.
