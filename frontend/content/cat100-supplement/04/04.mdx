# Classroom Communication Drafts

![Classroom Communication Drafts textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat100-supplement/04-04-classroom-communication-drafts.png)

## Why This Section Matters

A message to a parent has a job: it must let the reader know one concrete fact and one clear next step, without alarming them past what the situation warrants. AI tools default to vague, hedged drafts ("there have been some issues we should discuss at some point") because models are trained toward fluent, generic text and tend to hedge rather than commit to specifics they were never given; research finds LLMs are systematically reluctant to express calibrated, committed specifics (Zhou et al., 2024). As of 2026 the assistants most likely to hand you such a draft are Microsoft Copilot in Outlook ("Help me write" / "Help me reply"), Gemini in Gmail, and ChatGPT, but the four-part check this section teaches is tool-agnostic and outlives model and version churn. That average is useless to a parent, who cannot act on "some issues." This section teaches you to draft and check classroom messages against a fixed structure so every email carries a usable fact and a usable action.

The structure is what makes a draft checkable. Instead of asking "does this email feel okay," you score it part by part: subject line, specific fact, clear action, appropriate tone. A draft that fails three of four parts is not "almost done"; it is unsendable, and the structure tells you exactly which part to fix.

## Opening Case

A peer leader needs to tell a parent that three math worksheets are missing. The AI returns: "Subject: Concern. Hi, I am reaching out because there have been some issues lately that we should probably discuss at some point when you get a chance. Thanks." The parent who opens this learns nothing they can act on. They do not know what is wrong, how serious it is, or what to do, and the word "Concern" in the subject line raises alarm without giving direction.

A structured rewrite names the assignments and the dates ("the three math worksheets due May 18, 19, and 20"), asks for one specific action with a deadline ("could you check her folder and reply by Friday"), and keeps the tone calm ("she is doing well otherwise"). Same situation, but now the email does its job.

## Core Concept

A classroom message is built from four parts, and each part has a pass condition you can check.

| Part | Pass condition | Common failure |
|------|----------------|----------------|
| Subject line | names the topic in a few words | vague labels like "Concern" or "Update" |
| Specific fact | one dated, concrete detail | "some issues lately," "not doing well" |
| Clear action | what the reader should do, with a deadline | "at some point," "when you can" |
| Tone | respectful, proportional to the situation | alarming words, blame, or false urgency |

The two parts AI fails most often are the specific fact and the clear action, because both require committing to a concrete detail the model does not actually have. The model knows the genre of a concerned email but not the dates of the missing worksheets, so it hedges. Your job is to supply the concrete values the structure demands.

Tone is a constraint, not a goal. The aim is not to sound warm; it is to be proportional. A missed-homework email should not read like an emergency, and an emergency should not read like a routine note. Scoring tone against the actual situation prevents both the false-alarm and the buried-lede failures.

## Worked Example

A classroom communication drafts is a concrete document with a fixed structure: subject line, greeting, one specific fact, one clear action, and a closing. A freshman drafts a parent email, then checks it against that four-part structure. Follow the steps.

Step 1. The AI returns this first draft when asked for an email about a missed assignment:

```text
Subject: Concern

Hi, I am reaching out because there have been some issues lately
that we should probably discuss at some point when you get a chance.
Thanks.
```

Step 2. Score it against the required structure. Mark each part PASS or FAIL:

| Part | Requirement | Draft above |
|------|-------------|-------------|
| Subject line | names the topic | FAIL ("Concern" is vague) |
| Specific fact | one dated, concrete detail | FAIL ("some issues lately") |
| Clear action | what the parent should do | FAIL ("at some point") |
| Tone | respectful, not alarming | PASS |

Three of four parts fail, so the email would worry the parent without telling them anything to act on.

Step 3. Rewrite each failing part with a concrete value:

```text
Subject: Math homework missing for Maria (week of May 18)

Hi Ms. Lopez,
Maria has not turned in the three math worksheets due May 18, 19,
and 20. Could you check her folder this week and reply by Friday
so we can set a make-up plan? She is doing well otherwise.

Thank you,
J. Rivera
```

Step 4. Log the one rejected AI suggestion. The AI offered to add "this is urgent and serious." You rejected it because the tone field already passed and that phrase would alarm the parent without adding a fact. The before/after plus the structure score is the evidence: a reader can see which part failed and exactly how you fixed it.

## Decision Rule

Score every draft against the four parts before sending, and fix by part rather than by feel. A message is ready only when the subject names the topic, the fact is dated and concrete, the action has a deadline, and the tone matches the situation.

Apply these three checks:

1. **Fact check:** Could the reader repeat back exactly what happened, including a date or number, from your email alone?
2. **Action check:** Does the reader know what to do and by when, or are they left to guess?
3. **Proportion check:** Does the intensity of the wording match the actual stakes, or does it over- or under-alarm?

If the reader would have to reply "what do you mean?" to act, the fact or action part has failed, no matter how polished the sentences are.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is treating tone as the hardest part. AI almost always passes tone; it sounds pleasant. The parts it fails are the fact and the action, so spending your revision time softening tone while the email still says "some issues" fixes the wrong thing.

The second misreading is adding urgency to seem responsible. Phrases like "this is serious and urgent" feel diligent but usually fail the proportion check, alarming the reader without adding information. Concreteness, not intensity, is what makes a message land.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the four-part score to a positive message, since not every classroom email is bad news. You want to tell a parent their child led a group well. The AI draft says: "Subject: Good news! Your child has been doing great and is really shining lately. Keep it up!" Score it. Subject: PASS, it names the topic. Specific fact: FAIL, "doing great" names nothing concrete. Clear action: there may be no action needed, so this part can be marked N/A. Tone: PASS.

The rewrite supplies the missing fact: "Subject: Maria led her math group today. In today's class, Maria explained how to regroup in subtraction to her three group members, and two of them solved the next problem correctly afterward." Now the praise is credible because it is specific. The walkthrough shows the structure is not only for bad news; the specific-fact part is what makes a compliment believable instead of generic.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: **part** on the left, **pass or fail with the reason** on the right. A strong reader does not say "this email is weak"; they say "the action part fails because 'at some point' gives no deadline." Naming the part makes the fix obvious.

A strong reader of Classroom Communication Drafts can answer four questions:

1. Which of the four parts does this draft fail, and what is the exact failing phrase?
2. What concrete value would the fact part need to pass?
3. Does the action part give the reader a deadline, or only a vague invitation?
4. Is the tone proportional, or does it over- or under-state the stakes?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The artifact is strong when a reviewer can see the four-part score, the before draft, and the after draft side by side, so the fix is traceable to a failed part. Do not paraphrase the original; keep the failing draft verbatim so the improvement is visible.

A useful closing note for the artifact would read: "The first draft failed subject, fact, and action; I added the worksheet dates and a Friday deadline to fix the fact and action parts. I rejected the AI's 'this is urgent and serious' line because tone already passed and the phrase would alarm the parent without adding information." That ties each edit to a specific failed part and one defended rejection.

## Transfer Case

Move the message from a parent to a course instructor, the audience CAT 100 students email most. The four parts still apply, but the tone constraint shifts. With a parent you protect against alarm; with an instructor you protect against sounding entitled or vague. The fact part might become "I missed the May 18 quiz because of a documented medical appointment," and the action part becomes a specific request ("may I take the make-up before Friday?") rather than an open-ended one. The structure transfers intact; what changes is which tone failure you are guarding against in the new audience.
