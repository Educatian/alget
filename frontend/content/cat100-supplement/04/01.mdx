# Teacher Persona Setup

![Teacher Persona Setup textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat100-supplement/04-01-teacher-persona-setup.png)

## Why This Section Matters

A persona is the single most reusable thing you build when you work with an AI tool. Most students type a fresh request every time and get a different voice, a different reading level, and a different set of guesses with each reply. A saved persona fixes that: it is a short block of text you paste at the start of a chat that pins down who the AI is acting as, who the output is for, what facts it may rely on, and what it must refuse to do. In this section you learn to write that block as a real file you can version and reuse, not as a sentence you retype.

The payoff is consistency you can defend. When the persona is explicit, two different chats produce comparable output, a reviewer can read your persona and predict the AI's behavior, and you can point to the exact line that caused a good or bad result. That is the difference between prompting by luck and prompting by design.

## Opening Case

A peer leader asks an AI for help explaining long division to a third grader and types only "you are a teacher, help me." The first reply uses words like "dividend" and "quotient." The second reply, asked an hour later, switches to cartoon analogies. Same request, two voices, because nothing told the AI who the reader was or what vocabulary was allowed.

The fix is not a longer request. It is a saved persona that names the reader (a third grader's parent), caps the vocabulary, and states a refusal (do not invent the child's grade). Once that block exists in a file, every chat starts from the same fixed contract, and the inconsistency disappears.

## Core Concept

A persona is a system prompt: text that defines the assistant's standing instructions before any task is asked (this is exactly how providers document it; see OpenAI's custom-instructions help page, and Shanahan et al.'s (2023) Nature account of LLM role-play). A complete persona controls four fields, and each field closes off a specific way the output can drift.

| Field | Question it answers | What goes wrong if it is blank |
|-------|--------------------|-------------------------------|
| ROLE | grade level plus subject the AI is teaching | the AI picks a default reading level and tone |
| AUDIENCE | who reads the output | the AI guesses at vocabulary and formality |
| KNOWLEDGE BOUND | which facts the AI may treat as true | the AI fills gaps with plausible-sounding invention |
| HARD STOP | what the AI must never do | the AI offers advice outside its competence (medical, legal, grades) |

The KNOWLEDGE BOUND and HARD STOP fields are what separate a real persona from a friendly greeting. ROLE and AUDIENCE shape style; KNOWLEDGE BOUND and HARD STOP shape safety and accuracy. A persona that only sets ROLE ("you are a kind teacher") feels good but still hallucinates, because nothing limits what the AI may assert or refuse.

Save the persona as a plain text file (for example `persona.txt`) for two reasons. First, plain text pastes cleanly into any tool without hidden formatting. Second, a file can be revised and compared: you can keep `persona_v1.txt` and `persona_v2.txt` and show exactly which line you changed and why.

Where the persona actually lives in 2026 tools: most current assistants now give a saved persona a first-class home, so you do not have to re-paste the block at the start of every chat. ChatGPT has Custom Instructions and Projects, Claude has per-project custom instructions, Gemini has Gems, and Microsoft Copilot supports configurable agents; each applies your saved instructions automatically to every conversation in that space. Keep `persona.txt` as the version-controllable source of truth and treat those features as the place you paste or pin it, because exact feature names and interfaces churn while the file endures.

## Worked Example

A teacher persona is a concrete text artifact: a saved system prompt that tells the AI who it is acting as, what it knows, and what it must not do. A freshman writes it in a plain text file (for example `persona.txt`) so it can be reused and revised. Follow the steps and watch the persona move from vague to inspectable.

Step 1. Write the weak first draft (this is what most students save first):

```text
You are a helpful 3rd grade teacher. Help me with my lesson.
```

Step 2. Run the four-field persona checklist and mark what is missing:

| Field | Required value | In the draft above? |
|-------|----------------|---------------------|
| ROLE | grade + subject | partial (grade only) |
| AUDIENCE | who the output is for | missing |
| KNOWLEDGE BOUND | what facts it may assume | missing |
| HARD STOP | what it must never do | missing |

Three of four fields are blank, so the AI will guess and the output will drift.

Step 3. Rewrite the persona so every field has a value:

```text
ROLE: You are a 3rd grade math teacher in a U.S. public school.
AUDIENCE: You write notes a parent with no math background can read.
KNOWLEDGE BOUND: Use only the Grade 3 standards I paste below. If a topic is outside them, say so.
HARD STOP: Never invent a student's grade, never give medical or legal advice.
```

Step 4. Record the one rejected suggestion. The AI offered to add "act friendly and use emojis." You rejected it because the AUDIENCE field already fixes tone for parents, and emojis would clash with that. That accept/reject line is the evidence trace: a reader can now see the four filled fields and the one boundary you held.

## Decision Rule

Test a persona by what it forbids, not by how warm it sounds. A persona is ready when each of the four fields has a concrete value and at least one field actively prevents a known failure.

Run these three checks before you save:

1. **Bound check:** Does KNOWLEDGE BOUND name a real source (pasted standards, a specific document) rather than "be accurate"?
2. **Refusal check:** Does HARD STOP name something the AI could plausibly do but should not, so the line actually constrains behavior?
3. **Reuse check:** Could a classmate paste this persona into their own chat and get output in the same voice for the same reader?

If any field reads as a generic compliment ("be helpful," "be clear"), it is decoration, not control, and the AI will still drift.

## Common Misreadings

The most common mistake is treating a persona as a personality. Students write "you are an enthusiastic, caring teacher" and stop there. That sets a mood but leaves KNOWLEDGE BOUND and HARD STOP empty, so the AI still invents facts in a cheerful voice.

A second mistake is burying the persona inside the task ("write me a parent email, and by the way act like a teacher"). When the persona is mixed into the request, you cannot reuse it and you cannot tell whether a bad reply came from the persona or the task.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Walk through reusing one persona across two different tasks. You have saved the four-field math persona above. Today you need a note about a missed homework assignment; tomorrow you need a note about a strong quiz score. You paste the same persona block first, then the task underneath, in both chats.

Because the AUDIENCE field is fixed ("a parent with no math background"), both notes come back at the same reading level. Because the HARD STOP field forbids inventing a grade, neither note claims a score you did not provide. The persona did the work of holding voice and safety steady while only the task changed. That is the test of a good persona: the file stays put and the task slots in on top.

If the second note had drifted into jargon, you would not rewrite the request. You would open `persona.txt`, find the AUDIENCE line, and tighten it (for example, "use no math term above a 5th grade level"), then save a new version. The fix lives in the persona, which is why keeping it as a separate file matters.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: **claim** on the left, **evidence** on the right. For this section the claim is "this persona controls the AI's behavior," and the evidence is the specific line that does the controlling. A strong reader can point to the AUDIENCE line and say "that is why the output stays at parent reading level," not just assert that the persona is good.

A strong reader of Teacher Persona Setup can answer four questions:

1. Which of the four fields is doing the most work for this particular task?
2. Which field, if deleted, would let the AI invent or drift first?
3. What known failure does the HARD STOP line actually prevent?
4. What would you change in the persona, not the request, if the reader changed?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The artifact is strong when a reader can predict the AI's behavior from the persona alone, before seeing any output. Save two versions so the revision is visible: `persona_v1.txt` with the blank fields filled and `persona_v2.txt` after one tightening, plus a one-line note on what changed and why.

A useful final note for the artifact would read: "I revised the AUDIENCE line from 'parents' to 'a parent with no math background' because v1 still produced the word 'quotient.' I rejected the AI's offer to add emojis because the parent-note context makes them read as unserious." That note ties a specific line change to a specific observed failure, which is what makes the persona defensible rather than merely complete.

## Transfer Case

Move the same persona to a stricter setting: a school that bans pasting any student record into an AI tool. The ROLE and AUDIENCE fields can stay, but the KNOWLEDGE BOUND field can no longer say "use the standards I paste below" if those standards are tied to a named student. You rewrite the bound to rely only on the public, de-identified grade-level standards, and you add a HARD STOP line forbidding any student name or ID. The persona's shape survives; two fields change to honor the new privacy rule. If you understand the persona deeply, you adapt those two lines instead of starting over.
