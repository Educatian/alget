# Scenario-Based AI Dialogue

![Scenario-Based AI Dialogue textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat100-supplement/04-02-scenario-based-ai-dialogue.png)

## Why This Section Matters

A single long prompt that asks for everything at once usually returns a vague answer, because the AI has to balance too many goals in one shot. A scenario-based dialogue solves this by breaking the work into a planned sequence of turns, where each turn does exactly one job: set the situation, narrow the constraints, then stress-test the result. You stop hoping for a perfect first reply and instead steer the conversation toward a usable one.

This matters because the value lives in the steering, and the steering has to be visible. When each turn is labeled with its job, a reviewer can read your transcript and see which turn caused which improvement. An unlabeled wall of chat hides your reasoning; a labeled sequence makes it auditable, which is what turns a chat log into evidence of skill.

## Opening Case

A student needs a 90-second welcome to read aloud to a new study-group member. They type one giant prompt: "write a warm, short, professional, reassuring welcome for a nervous new student who missed orientation, no jargon, friendly but not childish." The reply is 300 words and uses the phrase "academic onboarding." It satisfies every adjective a little and none of them well.

The same student, working in turns, gets a tighter result. Turn one sets the situation. Turn two adds the single hard limit ("under 90 seconds aloud, no jargon"). Turn three pressure-tests it ("what if they already feel behind?"). Each turn fixes one thing, and the transcript shows exactly where the length dropped and where the reassurance line appeared.

## Core Concept

A scenario-based dialogue has three structural moves, run in order. Skipping or merging them is what produces vague output.

| Move | Turn job | What it controls |
|------|----------|------------------|
| SET CONTEXT | name the situation and the audience | who the answer is for and what counts as success |
| NARROW | add one hard constraint at a time | length, vocabulary, format, or tone |
| STRESS-TEST | introduce a complication | robustness when the easy case breaks |

The reason for one job per turn is diagnosis. If you change three things in a single message and the reply gets worse, you cannot tell which change caused it. Isolating one move per turn means every improvement and every regression has a single visible cause. This is the same logic a programmer uses when changing one variable at a time to find a bug.

Labeling is not decoration. Writing the turn's job ("Turn 2: NARROW") next to the message converts a private chat into a readable record. A reviewer can replay your reasoning turn by turn and check that each move actually did its job, which is the standard this section holds you to.

## Worked Example

A scenario-based dialogue is a concrete artifact: a saved chat transcript where each turn has a labeled job, so a reader can see how the conversation drove toward a usable answer. A freshman builds it turn by turn. Follow the steps.

Step 1. Write the scenario the AI must answer inside, as one paragraph:

```text
SCENARIO: A new student joined my study group mid-semester and missed
the syllabus walkthrough. I am the peer leader. Draft what I say in our
first 5-minute meeting.
```

Step 2. Plan three labeled turns before typing, so each turn does exactly one job:

| Turn | Job | What you type |
|------|-----|---------------|
| 1 | SET CONTEXT | paste the scenario; name the audience (a nervous new student) |
| 2 | NARROW | "Keep it under 90 seconds to read aloud; no jargon" |
| 3 | STRESS-TEST | "What if the student already feels behind? Add one reassurance line" |

Step 3. Run the turns and save the full transcript. Turn 2 cut the length; Turn 3 added a reassurance sentence. Because each turn is labeled, a reader can tell which turn caused which change. An unlabeled blob would hide that.

Step 4. Log the accept/reject. Turn 3 returned two reassurance lines; you kept "Everyone here joined at a different point" and cut the second because it just repeated the first. The labeled transcript plus that one cut is the evidence: a reader can replay your reasoning turn by turn.

## Decision Rule

Plan the turns before you type the first one. A dialogue is well-built when every turn has a named job and the transcript shows a visible cause for each change in the output.

Check the transcript against three questions:

1. **One-job check:** Does each turn change exactly one thing (context, a constraint, or a complication)?
2. **Cause check:** Can you point to the turn that produced each improvement, rather than a general "the conversation got better"?
3. **Stress check:** Did at least one turn introduce a complication, so the final answer is tested, not just polished for the easy case?

If two turns change several things at once, split them. You will not be able to defend which move mattered, and neither will a reviewer.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is thinking more turns automatically means better dialogue. Ten turns that each tweak wording are weaker than three turns that set context, narrow, and stress-test. Quality comes from distinct jobs, not from quantity.

The second misreading is saving only the final answer and discarding the path. The final answer alone proves nothing about your skill; anyone can copy a good paragraph. The labeled path is the artifact, because it shows you steered the result on purpose.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the three moves to a new scenario: convincing a hesitant classmate to join a group presentation. Turn one (SET CONTEXT) names the audience, a shy classmate who fears public speaking, and the goal, a two-line invitation. The first reply is generic encouragement. Turn two (NARROW) adds the constraint "offer one specific low-pressure role," and the reply now suggests "you could run the slides while we talk." Turn three (STRESS-TEST) asks "what if they say they will freeze on stage?" and the reply adds a fallback (they can read from notes).

Notice that the useful content, the specific role and the fallback, only appeared after the NARROW and STRESS-TEST turns. The SET CONTEXT turn alone gave you nothing actionable. That is the lesson: the early turn frames the problem, but the later turns extract the parts you can actually use. Reading the transcript, anyone can see that progression and trust that you built the answer rather than copied it.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: **claim** on the left, **evidence** on the right. The claim is "this turn improved the output"; the evidence is the labeled turn and the specific change it produced. A strong reader does not say "the dialogue worked"; they say "Turn 2 cut the welcome from 300 words to 70 because it added the length limit."

A strong reader of Scenario-Based AI Dialogue can answer four questions:

1. Which turn carried the most weight in reaching a usable answer?
2. Which turn, if removed, would leave the answer vague or untested?
3. What complication did the stress-test turn introduce, and did the AI handle it?
4. If you ran the same scenario again, which turn would you reorder or split?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The artifact is strong when the labeled transcript lets a reviewer reconstruct your steering without your narration. Keep the full sequence, mark each turn's job, and add a one-line accept/reject note for at least one AI suggestion you did not take whole.

A useful closing note for the artifact would read: "Turn 3 returned two reassurance lines; I kept the one about everyone joining at a different point and cut the duplicate. The cut matters because the reader has 90 seconds, and a repeated reassurance wastes time that the labeled transcript shows I was protecting." That ties a concrete edit to the constraint you set earlier in the dialogue.

## Transfer Case

Move the dialogue from a low-stakes welcome to a high-stakes message: a note to a struggling teammate about missed deadlines. The three-move structure transfers, but the STRESS-TEST turn changes character. For a welcome, you stress-tested for nerves; here you stress-test for defensiveness ("what if they feel blamed?"). The NARROW turn also tightens, since tone now carries real consequences. The skeleton (context, narrow, stress-test) holds across both, but the complication you choose to test depends on what could go wrong in the new setting. Understanding the structure means knowing which turn to re-aim, not retyping the old one.
