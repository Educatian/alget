## Core Concept

The core idea in this section is that **Scenario-Based AI Dialogue** should be treated as a relationship among purpose, constraint, evidence, and revision. Purpose names what the learner or audience needs to accomplish. Constraint names what limits the decision. Evidence names the source that makes the decision defensible. Revision shows what changed after the evidence was considered.

Three course anchors shape the reasoning here:

- **NACE career readiness** helps you decide what counts as a meaningful learning or performance demand.
- **ISTE digital citizenship** helps you notice when a design or technology choice creates risk, burden, or unsupported assumptions.
- **data-ink and visual evidence** helps you check whether the artifact works for varied learners, audiences, or use contexts.

Use these anchors as lenses, not as names to drop into a reflection. A useful lens changes what you notice, what you question, and what you revise.

## Worked Example

A scenario-based dialogue is a concrete artifact: a saved chat transcript where each turn has a labeled job, so a reader can see how the conversation drove toward a usable answer. A freshman builds it turn by turn. Follow the steps.

Step 1. Write the scenario the AI must answer inside, as one paragraph:

```text
SCENARIO: A new student joined my study group mid-semester and missed
the syllabus walkthrough. I am the peer leader. Draft what I say in our
first 5-minute meeting.
```

Step 2. Plan three labeled turns before typing, so each turn does exactly one job:

| Turn | Job | What you type |
|------|-----|---------------|
| 1 | SET CONTEXT | paste the scenario; name the audience (a nervous new student) |
| 2 | NARROW | "Keep it under 90 seconds to read aloud; no jargon" |
| 3 | STRESS-TEST | "What if the student already feels behind? Add one reassurance line" |

Step 3. Run the turns and save the full transcript. Turn 2 cut the length; Turn 3 added a reassurance sentence. Because each turn is labeled, a reader can tell which turn caused which change. An unlabeled blob would hide that.

Step 4. Log the accept/reject. Turn 3 returned two reassurance lines; you kept "Everyone here joined at a different point" and cut the second because it just repeated the first. The labeled transcript plus that one cut is the evidence: a reader can replay your reasoning turn by turn.

## Decision Rule

Use the audience first, the evidence second, and the tool third. A digital product is justified only when another person can see what changed, why it changed, and what evidence supports the change.

Before submitting the AI critique log showing accepted, modified, and rejected suggestions, slow down and check whether the trace can answer three questions:

1. **Purpose check:** What should the audience be able to do after reading or using the artifact?
2. **Evidence check:** Which exact source, observation, annotation, rubric line, data pattern, or policy boundary justifies the revision?
3. **Revision check:** What changed in the artifact, and what suggestion did you reject or modify?

If you cannot answer all three questions, the artifact may be complete, but it is not yet ready as evidence of learning.

## Common Misreadings

A common mistake is to treat digital fluency as finishing the file. A finished file can still hide weak evidence or unclear audience judgment.

The artifact should connect audience, evidence, tool use, and limitation in a way that can travel beyond the course.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Read this section as a small professional decision cycle, not as a list of terms. First, locate the work product: **AI critique log showing accepted, modified, and rejected suggestions**. Then ask what problem the artifact is supposed to solve inside teacher-AI dialogue and communication. A strong reader can explain the artifact in plain language before naming any theory: who will use it, what decision it supports, and what would count as evidence that it improved.

Next, connect the artifact to the course anchors: **NACE career readiness**, **ISTE digital citizenship**, **data-ink and visual evidence**. The point is not to cite every anchor. The point is to let one anchor change the artifact. If the anchor does not change wording, layout, evidence, timing, or feedback, it has not yet become design reasoning.

Finally, inspect the revision trail. A weak submission says the artifact was improved. A strong submission shows the earlier version, the issue noticed, the support requested, the decision accepted or rejected, and the reason the final version is better for the intended learner or audience.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: **claim** on the left, **evidence** on the right. The claim is the decision you think the section is teaching. The evidence is the exact phrase, example, annotation, support move, or artifact revision that makes the claim inspectable. By the end of CAT 100 04.02, your notes should make it possible to reconstruct your reasoning without asking you to explain it again.

A strong reader of Scenario-Based AI Dialogue can answer four questions:

1. What is the real decision hidden inside this topic?
2. Which constraint makes the decision difficult?
3. What evidence would persuade a skeptical reviewer?
4. What would you revise if the audience, learner, classroom, or tool changed?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

For this section, the artifact is strong when a future employer, instructor, or peer can see the claim, the evidence, and the revision decision without guessing. Treat visual polish as the last layer. The earlier layers are purpose, constraint, evidence, and revision. If one of those layers is missing, use the annotation tools to mark the gap and ask BigAL for a targeted comparison rather than a general explanation.
