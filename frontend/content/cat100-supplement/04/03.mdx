## Why This Section Matters

AI tools state false things in a confident, well-formatted voice. They invent statistics, attach real-sounding citations to claims the cited author never made, and present popular myths as settled research. The danger in a teaching context is that the advice sounds plausible and helpful, so you act on it without checking. This section teaches the discipline of breaking AI advice into separate factual claims and verifying each one against a real source before you use any of it.

The skill is not "distrust AI." It is "verify claim by claim." A single AI reply often mixes a usable idea with a fabricated number and a wrong attribution. The goal is to keep the part that survives verification and reject the parts that fail, with a record showing which source you checked. That record is what lets you defend acting on the advice you kept.

## Opening Case

A student asks an AI how to help a struggling reader and gets back: "Research shows students learn 90% of what they teach others, so have them tutor a classmate. This is the learning pyramid, proven by Edgar Dale (1969)." It reads like a citation-backed fact. It contains a fabricated statistic (the 90% figure traces to a corporate handout, not peer-reviewed research; Subramony et al., 2014), a real-but-unproven graphic (the learning pyramid; Letrud & Hernes, 2016), and a misattribution (Dale wrote on the Cone of Experience and assigned no percentages; Subramony et al., 2014).

If you act on the whole reply, you have built a tutoring plan on a made-up number and a wrong name. If you verify claim by claim, you can still keep the defensible idea (peer tutoring has independent support) while cutting the fabricated 90% and correcting the citation.

## Core Concept

AI advice fails in recognizable patterns. Learning the categories lets you predict where to look for trouble.

| Failure type | What it looks like | How to catch it |
|--------------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Fabricated statistic | a precise number (90%, 3x) with no real source | search for the number; if it only appears on blogs and slides, it is invented |
| Misattribution | a real author or study credited with a claim they never made | check what the cited source actually says |
| Myth-as-fact | a popular graphic or "brain fact" stated as proven | look for peer-reviewed support, not just popularity |
| Overgeneralization | a narrow finding stated as a universal rule | check the original study's population and conditions |

The core move is decomposition. A reply is not one claim; it is several claims glued together by confident prose. You separate them into rows, find a source for each, and assign a verdict. A spreadsheet (Excel or Google Sheets) makes this inspectable: one claim per row, a SOURCE column, and a VERDICT column. The verdict matters because the goal is not to throw out the whole reply but to filter it, keeping what survives.

> **Recent evidence:** The failures this section uses are documented, not hypothetical. Subramony, Molenda, Betrus, and Thalheimer (2014) traced the retention percentages to fabricated charts grafted onto Edgar Dale's Cone of Experience, which itself contained no percentages. Letrud and Hernes (2016) showed the learning-pyramid figures circulating widely in academic literature despite lacking empirical support. And Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer, and Bjork (2008) found no adequate experimental evidence that matching instruction to a student's learning style improves outcomes.

## Worked Example

Checking AI advice is concrete verification work: you put each claim the AI made into a row of a spreadsheet, find the source, and mark a verdict. A freshman builds this as a small table (in Excel or Google Sheets) so the check is inspectable. Follow the steps.

Step 1. The AI gave this advice when you asked how to help a struggling reader:

```text
"Research shows students learn 90% of what they teach others,
so have the struggling student tutor a classmate. This is the
'learning pyramid' and it is proven by Edgar Dale (1969)."
```

Step 2. Break the reply into separate checkable claims, one per row, and add a SOURCE and VERDICT column:

| Claim | Source you found | Verdict |
|-------|------------------|---------|
| "students learn 90% of what they teach" | no peer-reviewed study; traces to a corporate handout (Subramony et al., 2014) | FALSE (fabricated statistic) |
| "this is the learning pyramid" | the pyramid exists as a popular graphic with unsupported numbers (Letrud & Hernes, 2016) | TRUE-ish (real graphic, unproven numbers) |
| "proven by Edgar Dale (1969)" | Dale wrote on the Cone of Experience but assigned no percentages (Subramony et al., 2014) | MISATTRIBUTED |

Step 3. Compute the check result. 1 of 3 claims survives, and even that one is weakened. The advice is built on a fabricated number and a wrong citation, so you do not act on it as stated.

Step 4. Record the bounded outcome. You keep the usable idea ("peer tutoring can help") because that part is independently supported, and you reject the 90% statistic and the Dale citation, logging the source you checked. That filled table is the evidence: a reader can see exactly which claim failed and why.

## Decision Rule

Verify before you use, claim by claim, and keep only what passes. A piece of AI advice is safe to act on only when each factual claim inside it has a named source and a verdict.

Run these three checks on any advice that includes a number or a name:

1. **Number check:** Does every statistic trace to a real study, or does it only appear on blogs, slideshows, and corporate handouts?
2. **Attribution check:** Does the cited author actually make the claim, or were they named to lend false authority?
3. **Survival check:** After removing the failed claims, is there still a defensible idea left, and is it supported on its own?

If a precise-sounding number has no traceable source, treat it as fabricated until proven otherwise. Confident formatting is not evidence.

This is not a solved problem. As of 2026, current assistants (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot) still produce confident but fabricated or misattributed citations even in their newest reasoning and web-search modes, and a built-in "cite your sources" feature can itself point to a real source that does not actually support the claim, the exact misattribution pattern this section teaches you to catch. Claim-by-claim verification stays necessary regardless of which tool or model version you use.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is "if it has a citation, it is checked." A citation is a claim too. AIs routinely attach real names to invented findings, so a reference is a place to start verifying, not a stamp of approval.

The second misreading is throwing out the entire reply because one claim failed. The point of the row-by-row table is to filter, not to delete. A reply with a fabricated statistic can still contain a usable idea that has its own independent support; your job is to separate them.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the same row-by-row method to a different reply. You asked an AI for study tips and it returned: "Students retain information best when they match their learning style; visual learners should only use diagrams. Studies show this raises test scores by 40%." Decompose it. Row one: "matching learning styles improves retention," verdict MYTH-AS-FACT, because controlled studies have repeatedly failed to support learning-styles matching (Pashler et al., 2008). Row two: "raises test scores by 40%," verdict FABRICATED, because the number has no traceable study. Row three: "diagrams can help with spatial content," verdict TRUE, supported on its own.

After filtering, almost nothing of the original advice survives. The learning-styles framing fails, the 40% figure is invented, and only the modest, independently supported point about diagrams remains. The table makes that collapse visible: a reviewer sees three rows, two failures, and one survivor, and can trust that you did not act on the myth. Without the table, you might have repeated "match their learning style" because it sounded reasonable.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: **claim** on the left, **verdict and source** on the right. A strong reader does not write "the AI was wrong"; they write "the 90% figure is FALSE because it traces to a corporate handout, not a study." The source is what makes the verdict checkable by someone else.

A strong reader of Checking AI Pedagogical Advice can answer four questions:

1. How many separate factual claims are inside this one reply?
2. Which claim is a fabricated statistic, and what search exposed it?
3. Which claim is misattributed, and what does the cited source actually say?
4. After filtering, what defensible idea is left, and is it supported on its own?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The artifact is strong when a reviewer can open your verification table and confirm each verdict without redoing your searches, because the SOURCE column tells them where to look. Keep every row, including the claims that passed, so the filtering is visible rather than just the deletions.

A useful closing note for the artifact would read: "I rejected the 90% statistic because it traces only to a corporate training handout, and I corrected the Edgar Dale citation because his Cone of Experience assigns no percentages. I kept peer tutoring as a strategy because it has its own research support independent of the pyramid graphic." That note names the failed claim, the reason, and the surviving idea.

## Transfer Case

Move the method from pedagogy advice to a resume context, since CAT 100 also asks you to write resumes. An AI offers to "strengthen" your resume by adding "increased team productivity by 35%." The same verification applies: that is a fabricated statistic about your own work, and you have no source for it. The verdict is REJECT, not because AI is untrustworthy in general, but because the specific claim has no evidence you can defend in an interview. The skill transfers: any precise claim, about research or about yourself, needs a source before it goes into something a reader will trust.
