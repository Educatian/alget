# Tone, Bias, and Audience

![Tone, Bias, and Audience textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat100-supplement/04-05-tone-bias-and-audience.png)

## Why This Section Matters

Bias in a message usually hides at the word level, not the paragraph level. A draft can have a fine structure and still carry words that assume a cause you cannot know ("obviously did not study"), label a person instead of a behavior ("lazy"), or pressure a reader who has done nothing wrong ("immediately"). This section teaches you to scan a draft word by word, flag the loaded or audience-wrong terms, and log a specific replacement for each, so the edit is a visible decision rather than a vibe.

The reason to work at the word level is that this is where you can be precise and accountable. "Make it less harsh" is not a checkable instruction. "Replace 'lazy habits' with 'could use more practice on fractions' because the original labels the child" is. A find-and-replace log turns tone work into a list of decisions someone else can audit and agree or disagree with.

## Opening Case

An AI drafts a note to parents whose children failed a quiz: "Your child obviously did not study and failed the quiz badly. These lazy habits will hurt them. They need to fix this immediately." Three words carry the damage. "Obviously did not study" asserts a cause the writer cannot observe. "Lazy habits" labels the child rather than naming a behavior. "Immediately" applies pressure that does not match a single quiz.

A word-level scan replaces each one with something observable and proportional: the assumed cause becomes a fact ("scored 4 out of 10"), the label becomes a behavior ("could use more practice on fractions"), and the pressure becomes a reasonable window ("over the next two weeks"). The structure barely changes; the words do all the harm and all the repair.

## Core Concept

Loaded language falls into a few repeatable categories. Recognizing the category tells you how to fix it.

| Bias pattern | What it does | Repair move |
|--------------|--------------|-------------|
| Unobservable cause | claims a reason the writer cannot know (motivation, effort) | replace with what you can observe (a score, a count) |
| Person label | judges the person, not the action ("lazy," "careless") | name the specific behavior instead |
| False urgency | applies pressure out of proportion to the stakes | set a realistic timeframe |
| Audience mismatch | uses words wrong for the reader (jargon for a parent) | match the reader's vocabulary |

The deepest of these is the unobservable cause, because it is the easiest to write without noticing. A writer cannot see whether a child studied; they can only see a score. Swapping the inferred cause for the observed fact is both fairer and more useful, since the parent can act on a fact but cannot act on an accusation.

Audience is the frame that decides whether a word is wrong. "Quotient" is fine for a colleague and wrong for a parent with no math background. So the scan is not "find harsh words" in the abstract; it is "find words wrong for this reader," which means you must name the reader before you scan.

## Worked Example

Tone and bias work is concrete word-level editing: you scan a draft for loaded or audience-wrong words and log a find-and-replace decision for each one. A freshman does this as a two-column edit table so every change is visible. Follow the steps.

Step 1. The AI drafted this note for parents of students who failed a quiz:

```text
Your child obviously did not study and failed the quiz badly.
These lazy habits will hurt them. They need to fix this immediately.
```

Step 2. Scan for loaded words and audience mismatches. Log each FIND with the reason and a REPLACE:

| Find (loaded word) | Why it is a problem | Replace with |
|--------------------|---------------------|--------------|
| "obviously did not study" | assumes a cause you cannot know | "scored 4/10 on Tuesday's quiz" |
| "lazy habits" | labels the child, blames | "could use more practice on fractions" |
| "immediately" | pressuring tone for parents | "over the next two weeks" |

Step 3. Apply the replacements to produce the audience-fit version:

```text
Your child scored 4 out of 10 on Tuesday's fractions quiz. With a
little more practice on fractions over the next two weeks, this is
very fixable. Here are two things you can try at home.
```

Step 4. Log the one rejected change. The AI also suggested replacing "your child" with the student's first name everywhere. You rejected it because in a privacy-sensitive parent email the relationship word "your child" is clearer and safer than repeating the name. The find-and-replace table plus that rejection is the evidence: a reader can see every word you changed and why.

Replace inferred causes with observed facts, labels with behaviors, and pressure with proportion. A message is audience-fit when every flagged word has a logged replacement and a reason a reviewer could check.

Run these three checks on each flagged word:

1. **Observability check:** Does the word state something you can actually see (a score, a date), or does it assume an inner cause (effort, attitude)?
2. **Behavior check:** Does the word name what the person did, or does it label who they are?
3. **Audience check:** Is the word right for this specific reader's vocabulary and stakes, or is it borrowed from a different audience?

If a word claims to know why someone acted, it almost certainly fails the observability check and should be replaced with what you observed.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is thinking polite words equal unbiased words. "Unfortunately your child is simply not a math person" is polite and still carries a fixed-trait label that the observability and behavior checks both reject. Courtesy does not remove bias; specificity does.

The second misreading is over-correcting into vagueness. Stripping every strong word can leave a mush that says nothing ("there may be some areas to look at"). The goal is not blandness; it is replacing an unfair word with a precise one. "Scored 4/10" is both strong and fair.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the scan to a recommendation a student asks AI to write about a peer. The draft says: "She is a natural genius who never has to try, unlike most students." Scan it. "Natural genius who never has to try" is an unobservable-cause claim and, oddly, a backhanded one, since it implies she does not work. "Unlike most students" is an audience mismatch in a recommendation, where comparing down to others reads as unkind. Replace the first with an observed behavior ("she revised her project three times after feedback") and cut the comparison entirely.

The rewrite reads: "She revised her project three times after feedback, and each version was clearer than the last." It is shorter, it names what she did, and it removes both the trait label and the comparison. The walkthrough shows that even praise can carry bias: "genius" sounds flattering but assumes a cause and erases the effort that the observed behavior actually credits her for.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: **flagged word** on the left, **category and replacement** on the right. A strong reader does not write "this sounds harsh"; they write "'lazy' is a person-label, replace with the behavior 'could use more fraction practice.'" Naming the category makes the fix repeatable.

A strong reader of Tone, Bias, and Audience can answer four questions:

1. Which words assume a cause the writer cannot observe?
2. Which words label the person instead of naming a behavior?
3. Which words are wrong for this specific reader, and what would fit?
4. Which strong word should stay because it is precise rather than loaded?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The artifact is strong when a reviewer can read your find-and-replace table and judge each swap independently, because every row names the flagged word, the bias category, and the replacement. Keep the original draft verbatim so the reader can see what each replacement is responding to.

A useful closing note for the artifact would read: "I replaced 'obviously did not study' with the observed score because I cannot know why the child scored low. I rejected the AI's suggestion to use the student's first name throughout, because in a parent email the relationship word 'your child' is both clearer and safer for privacy." That ties one swap to the observability check and one rejection to an audience-and-privacy reason.

## Transfer Case

Move the scan from a parent email to a public web page, since CAT 100 students publish to GitHub Pages. On a public page the audience is unknown and permanent, so the audience check tightens: a word that assumes shared context ("as everyone knows") fails because not every visitor shares it, and any word naming a real person raises a privacy flag the moment the page is public. The categories transfer, but the audience-mismatch check becomes stricter because you cannot predict or take back who reads a published page. Understanding the method means raising the bar for the new, wider audience rather than reusing the parent-email standard.
