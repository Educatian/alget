# Reflection on Teacher Judgment

![Reflection on Teacher Judgment textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat100-supplement/04-06-reflection-on-teacher-judgment.png)

## Why This Section Matters

Most reflections students write are feelings paragraphs: "I learned a lot, the AI was helpful, I feel more confident." That kind of reflection proves nothing, because it contains no decisions a reader can examine. This section teaches you to reflect by logging the moments where you accepted or overrode AI advice, with the reason for each, so your judgment becomes countable instead of merely claimed.

Judgment shows up at the override points. Anytime you take AI advice without thinking, no judgment was exercised; anytime you reject or change it, you made a call you can be asked to defend. A decision log captures those calls in rows: the situation, what the AI said, what you decided, and why. The ratio of overrides to accepts becomes a piece of evidence on its own, showing whether you were steering or just following.

## Opening Case

Two students submit reflections on the same week of AI work. The first writes: "Using AI was really helpful this week and I feel more confident. I will keep using it." The second writes a four-row log: the AI told them to write "tell the parent their child failed," and they overrode it because the tone was too harsh for a first quiz; the AI told them to teach fractions before decimals, and they accepted it because it matched the pasted standards; the AI told them to say a student was "unmotivated," and they overrode it because motivation is not observable.

The first reflection is unfalsifiable; nothing in it could be wrong. The second can be audited line by line, and it shows the student overrode the AI twice on principle and accepted it once on evidence. Only the second demonstrates judgment.

## Core Concept

A decision log records judgment as a set of discrete calls, each with a reason that points to a standard.

| Column | What it captures | Why it matters |
|--------|------------------|----------------|
| Situation | the moment a decision was needed | anchors the call in something concrete |
| AI said | the advice given | shows what you were judging against |
| I decided | ACCEPT or OVERRIDE | makes the call explicit |
| Why | the reason, tied to a standard | converts a preference into a defensible judgment |

The reason column is where judgment lives. "Override, too harsh" is weak; "override, because the advice assumed a cause I cannot observe" points to a principle that would apply again. Good reasons reference an observable standard (the pasted standards, what you can actually see, the audience's needs), not just personal taste.

The accept/override ratio is itself evidence. A log that is all accepts suggests you followed without judging; a log that is all overrides may suggest you ignored useful help. A mix, each with a reason, is the signature of someone weighing advice rather than rubber-stamping or reflexively rejecting it.

## Worked Example

A reflection on teacher judgment is a concrete decision log, not a feelings paragraph. You record each moment where the AI advised something and you decided to accept or override it, with the reason. A freshman writes this as a four-column table so the judgment is countable. Follow the steps.

Step 1. Write the weak reflection most students submit first:

```text
I learned a lot from using AI this week. It was really helpful and
I feel more confident now. I will keep using it.
```

That has zero decisions in it, so a reader cannot see any judgment.

Step 2. Replace it with a decision log. One row per moment the AI advised you:

| Situation | AI said | I decided | Why |
|-----------|---------|-----------|-----|
| Quiz feedback wording | "Tell them they failed" | OVERRIDE | too harsh for a first quiz; used "scored 4/10, here is the plan" |
| Lesson order | "Teach fractions before decimals" | ACCEPT | matches the standards I pasted |
| Parent email cause | "Say the student is unmotivated" | OVERRIDE | I cannot observe motivation; stated the missing assignments instead |

Step 3. Count the pattern. Out of 3 AI suggestions, you overrode 2 and accepted 1. That ratio is itself evidence: it shows you were judging, not just following.

Step 4. Write one sentence the log now supports: "I overrode the AI twice when its advice assumed a cause I could not observe, and accepted it once when it matched the standards." The decision log plus that count is the evidence: a reader can audit every judgment call, not just a feeling.

## Decision Rule

Reflect by logging decisions, not feelings, and give every decision a reason tied to a standard. A reflection counts as evidence of judgment only when a reader can audit each accept and override independently.

Run these three checks on your log:

1. **Decision check:** Does each row name a real ACCEPT or OVERRIDE, rather than a general impression?
2. **Reason check:** Does each reason point to a standard (observability, the pasted standards, audience needs) instead of "it felt better"?
3. **Pattern check:** Does your accept/override ratio show weighing rather than blanket trust or blanket rejection?

If a row's reason would be just as true for the opposite decision, the reason is not doing its job and the judgment is not yet visible.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is that more confidence equals better reflection. "I feel confident" is a feeling, and a confident wrong decision is worse than a hesitant right one. The log cares about the decision and its reason, not the mood around it.

The second misreading is that overriding the AI more often proves more independence. Overriding good advice for no reason is not judgment; it is contrarianism. The accept row in your log is just as important, because accepting advice that matches a standard is also a defensible call.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the decision log to a moment where the right call is to accept. The AI suggests breaking a long lesson into three ten-minute chunks with a check-in between each. Your instinct is to override, because you wanted to look independent. But the reason check stops you: the advice matches a real standard (short segments with frequent checks support attention), and you have no observed reason to reject it. So you log ACCEPT, with the reason "matches the attention guidance in the module and I have no evidence against it."

That row is valuable precisely because it is an accept. It shows you can recognize good advice and take it, which is half of judgment. A log full of overrides would have hidden this skill. The walkthrough makes the point that judgment is not resistance; it is matching each decision to the evidence and standard, whichever way that points.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: **decision** on the left, **the standard the reason invokes** on the right. A strong reader does not write "good reflection"; they write "the override is defensible because the reason invokes observability, a standard that would apply again." Naming the standard tests whether the reason is principled or just a preference.

A strong reader of Reflection on Teacher Judgment can answer four questions:

1. How many real decisions does this reflection contain, not how many feelings?
2. For each override, what standard does the reason invoke?
3. For each accept, what evidence justified taking the advice?
4. Does the accept/override pattern show weighing, or blanket trust or rejection?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The artifact is strong when a reviewer can audit every row independently and judge whether each reason holds, because each row names the situation, the advice, the decision, and a standard-based reason. Include accepts as well as overrides so the log shows weighing rather than reflexive rejection.

A useful closing note for the artifact would read: "I overrode the AI twice where its advice assumed a cause I could not observe (motivation, effort), and accepted it once where it matched the standards I pasted. I rejected the AI's offer to add a confident-sounding summary line because confidence is not a decision and would have hidden the actual ratio." That ties the pattern to a principle and defends one rejection.

## Transfer Case

Move the decision log from classroom advice to a job-application context, where AI now suggests resume edits. The same structure applies: each suggested edit is a row, and you ACCEPT or OVERRIDE with a reason. But the standard shifts from "what I can observe about a student" to "what I can defend about myself in an interview." An AI suggestion to add "led a team of ten" gets an OVERRIDE if you cannot back it up, for the same kind of reason an unobservable-cause claim got overridden in the classroom. The log structure and the discipline of standard-based reasons transfer; the standard you measure against changes with the audience.
