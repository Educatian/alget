# Charts That Answer Questions

![Charts That Answer Questions textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat100-supplement/03-03-charts-that-answer-questions.png)

## Why This Section Matters

A chart is not decoration added to finished data; it is an argument. Before you pick a chart type, you have to know the question the chart is supposed to answer, because the question determines the shape. "Which region sold the most?" is a comparison and wants bars. "How did sales change over the year?" is a trend and wants a line. Choosing the chart before the question is how people end up with a pie chart that hides the very comparison they meant to show.

This section also teaches the honest-axis rule, the single most abused setting in everyday charts. Where a value axis starts decides how large a difference looks, and starting it above zero can make a small gap look like a landslide. Reading and building charts that answer a question truthfully is the skill here, not making them colorful.

## Opening Case

You have four regions with Q1 revenue: West 48000, East 31000, South 29000, North 22000. You highlight the data, click the chart button, and Excel hands you a default column chart titled "Q1 Revenue." It is technically a chart, but it answers nothing on its own. A viewer still has to do the work of deciding what the bars mean.

Now compare two finishing moves on the same chart. In one, the title stays "Q1 Revenue" and the axis quietly starts at 20000, so North's bar nearly disappears and West looks five times larger than it is. In the other, the axis starts at 0 and the title reads "West Led Q1 Revenue, More Than Double North." Same data, two very different impressions. The difference between them is the subject of this section: a chart that answers a question honestly versus one that merely exists.

## Core Concept

The chart type follows from the kind of question, and there is a short, reliable map between them:

| Question type | Right chart | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Compare separate categories | Bar or column | Length is easy to compare side by side |
| Show change over time | Line | The connected slope reads as a trend |
| Show parts of one whole | Pie (sparingly) | Only when slices sum to 100% and there are few of them |
| Relate two numeric variables | Scatter | Position shows correlation |

Most everyday business questions are category comparisons, so bars are the default and pies are the exception. A pie fails the moment you have more than four or five slices or want a precise comparison, because the eye reads bar length far more accurately than slice angle.

Two principles keep a chart honest. First, the value axis on a bar or column chart must start at zero, because bar length is the visual encoding and a truncated axis exaggerates differences. Second, the title should state the finding, not just label the data. "West Led Q1 at 48K" is a claim-first title; "Q1 Revenue" is only a caption. A claim-first title turns the chart into evidence for a specific statement a reader can check against the bars.

## Worked Example

Suppose you have quarterly revenue by region and you want a chart that answers one question.

```
   A        B
1  Region   Q1 Revenue
2  West     48000
3  East     31000
4  South    29000
5  North    22000
```

Step 1. State the question first: "Which region led Q1 revenue?" That is a comparison across separate categories, so the right chart is a bar (or column) chart, not a line or pie chart.

Step 2. Build the chart from A2:B5. Each region gets one bar. Make sure the value axis starts at 0, so West (48000) reads as roughly twice North (22000), which matches the data. If the axis started at 20000, North's bar would nearly vanish and the gap would look far larger than it is.

Step 3. Replace the default title 'Q1 Revenue' with a claim-first title: 'West Led Q1 Revenue at 48K, More Than Double North'. Now the title states the finding and the bars are the evidence.

The auditable decision chain: question -> chart type (bar for category comparison) -> honest axis (start at 0) -> claim-first title. A reader can check each link against the four numbers above.

## Decision Rule

Write the question before you build the chart, then let the question pick the chart type and the title. If you cannot state the one question your chart answers, you are decorating data, not analyzing it.

Run a chart through this three-point honesty check before sharing it:

1. **Type check:** Does the chart type match the question (bars for category comparison, line for change over time)?
2. **Axis check:** Does the value axis start at zero so bar lengths are proportional to the numbers?
3. **Title check:** Does the title state the finding the bars support, not just name the dataset?

If a reader could read the opposite conclusion from your chart than the data supports, one of these three failed.

## Common Misreadings

The most damaging misreading is thinking a truncated axis is a styling choice. Starting a bar chart's value axis at 20000 instead of 0 is not zooming in; it visually triples a real gap and misleads anyone who trusts the bars. The zero baseline is mandatory for bars because length is the encoding.

A second misreading is reaching for a pie chart to compare categories. A pie asks the eye to judge angles, which it does poorly, so a four-region revenue comparison that is obvious in a bar chart becomes a guessing game in a pie. Reserve pies for a small number of parts of a single whole, and prefer bars whenever the goal is comparison.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the rules to a different dataset: monthly active users for an app across January through June, rising from 1,200 to 4,100. First state the question: "How did active users change over the first half of the year?" That is change over time, so the right chart is a line, not bars; the connected slope shows the growth trend at a glance.

Build the line from the month and user columns, then check the axis. Because this is a line chart of a trend rather than a magnitude comparison, the zero-baseline rule is softer, but you should still avoid an axis range so tight that normal month-to-month wobble looks like a crisis. Label the vertical axis "Monthly active users" so the units are unmistakable.

Finish with a claim-first title: "Monthly Active Users More Than Tripled, January to June." Now the title states the finding, the line is the evidence, and a reader can trace the headline back to the six plotted points.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

A strong reader audits a chart they did not make by asking what trick, if any, is being played. Look first at the axis, then at the chart type, then at the title:

1. Where does the value axis start? If it is above zero on a bar chart, the differences are exaggerated.
2. Does the chart type fit the question, or is a comparison hidden inside a pie?
3. Does the title assert a finding the chart actually supports, or does it claim more than the bars show?

When you can spot a misleading axis or a mismatched chart type in someone else's figure, you will stop building those mistakes into your own.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A strong chart artifact carries its evidence on its face: a claim-first title, labeled axes with units, a zero baseline where bars demand it, and a source note saying where the numbers came from. The trace should name the question the chart answers and one honest-design choice you made. A model line reads: "The question was which region led Q1, so I used a bar chart with the axis at 0; the title states 'West Led, More Than Double North,' which a reader can verify against the four bars."

The weak version is a chart with the default title and a hidden truncated axis. The strong version is one where a skeptical reader can confirm the headline claim by reading the bars themselves, with no need to reopen the data.

## Transfer Case

The same question-first discipline transfers to a relationship chart, where the right form is a scatter rather than a bar. Suppose you want to know whether hours studied relate to exam scores across a class. The question is "do two numeric variables move together?", so neither bars nor a line fits; a scatter plot, with hours on the horizontal axis and scores on the vertical, lets the cloud of points reveal whether the relationship is positive, flat, or noisy. Recognizing that this question demands a scatter, and resisting the urge to force it into the familiar bar chart, shows you are matching chart to question rather than defaulting to one shape.
