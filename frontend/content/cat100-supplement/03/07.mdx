## Core Concept

The core idea in this section is that **Narrating a Data Finding** should be treated as a relationship among purpose, constraint, evidence, and revision. Purpose names what the learner or audience needs to accomplish. Constraint names what limits the decision. Evidence names the source that makes the decision defensible. Revision shows what changed after the evidence was considered.

Three course anchors shape the reasoning here:

- **NACE career readiness** helps you decide what counts as a meaningful learning or performance demand.
- **ISTE digital citizenship** helps you notice when a design or technology choice creates risk, burden, or unsupported assumptions.
- **data-ink and visual evidence** helps you check whether the artifact works for varied learners, audiences, or use contexts.

The anchors should do work in your artifact. If NACE career readiness or ISTE digital citizenship does not change a decision, it is only a citation rather than evidence of judgment.

## Worked Example

Suppose your analysis produced this small summary table.

```
   A          B
1  Day type   Avg daily sales
2  Weekday    500
3  Weekend    700
```

Weekend sales (700) are `(700 - 500) / 500 = 0.40`, so 40% higher than weekday sales.

Step 1. The weak narration describes process: "We pulled four weeks of sales and split them by day type." That tells the reader what you did, not what you found.

Step 2. The strong narration leads with the finding and its implication: "Weekend sales averaged 700 per day versus 500 on weekdays, about 40% higher (4 weeks, March data). Shifting one staff member to weekends would match coverage to demand."

Step 3. Audit the wording. Do not write "weekend sales skyrocketed"; 40% is a clear lift, not a record-breaking surge, so keep the verb honest. Do not write "the weekend caused higher sales"; the data shows a correlation over four weeks, and a March promotion could also explain part of it. Name that limit.

The auditable narration: finding first (40% higher), exact evidence (700 vs 500, March, 4 weeks), faithful magnitude (no inflation), and a correlation flagged as correlation rather than proven cause.

## Decision Rule

Use the audience first, the evidence second, and the tool third. A digital product is justified only when another person can see what changed, why it changed, and what evidence supports the change.

Use the following check when the artifact looks finished but the reasoning may still be hidden:

1. **Purpose check:** What should the audience be able to do after reading or using the artifact?
2. **Evidence check:** Which exact source, observation, annotation, rubric line, data pattern, or policy boundary justifies the revision?
3. **Revision check:** What changed in the artifact, and what suggestion did you reject or modify?

If you cannot answer all three questions, the artifact may be complete, but it is not yet ready as evidence of learning.

Next, connect the artifact to the course anchors: **NACE career readiness**, **ISTE digital citizenship**, **data-ink and visual evidence**. The point is not to cite every anchor. The point is to let one anchor change the artifact. If the anchor does not change wording, layout, evidence, timing, or feedback, it has not yet become design reasoning.

Finally, inspect the revision trail. A weak submission says the artifact was improved. A strong submission shows the earlier version, the issue noticed, the support requested, the decision accepted or rejected, and the reason the final version is better for the intended learner or audience.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: **claim** on the left, **evidence** on the right. The claim is the decision you think the section is teaching. The evidence is the exact phrase, example, annotation, support move, or artifact revision that makes the claim inspectable. By the end of CAT 100 03.07, your notes should make it possible to reconstruct your reasoning without asking you to explain it again.

A strong reader of Narrating a Data Finding can answer four questions:

1. What is the real decision hidden inside this topic?
2. Which constraint makes the decision difficult?
3. What evidence would persuade a skeptical reviewer?
4. What would you revise if the audience, learner, classroom, or tool changed?
