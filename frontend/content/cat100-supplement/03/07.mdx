# Narrating a Data Finding

![Narrating a Data Finding textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat100-supplement/03-07-narrating-a-data-finding.png)

A correct number sitting in a cell persuades no one. The analysis is only finished when you can say, in a sentence a busy reader will actually read, what you found and why it matters. Narrating a data finding is the step that turns a pivot result into a decision someone can act on, and it is where careful analysts most often slip, either by burying the finding in process or by overstating it for effect.

This section teaches a disciplined way to write the sentence. Lead with the finding, not the procedure. Attach the exact evidence (the numbers, the sample, the time window). Keep the verb faithful to the magnitude, so a 40% rise is not a "surge." And label a correlation as a correlation rather than smuggling in a claim of cause. Each rule keeps your sentence honest enough that the underlying data would survive a skeptic checking it.

## Learning Targets

- Compute a percentage change correctly as (new - old) / old and state it without rounding-up inflation.
- Write a finding-first sentence that leads with the result before the procedure.
- Match the verb to the magnitude (a 40% gap is 'higher,' not 'skyrocketed') and flag a correlation as a correlation, naming a competing explanation rather than asserting cause.
- Accept or reject AI rewrites of a data sentence based on whether they inflate magnitude or smuggle in causal verbs, logging each call.

## Opening Case

Your analysis shows weekend sales averaging 700 per day against 500 on weekdays. You write it up two ways. The first: "We pulled four weeks of sales and broke them out by day type to see whether there was a difference." The second: "Weekend sales ran about 40% higher than weekdays (700 vs 500 per day, four weeks of March data), so shifting one staffer to weekends would match coverage to demand."

The first sentence describes your process and leaves the reader to find the point. The second states the finding, cites the evidence, and proposes an action. Same data, but only one of them is a narration a manager can use. Closing that gap, from "here is what we did" to "here is what we found and what to do," is the whole task of this section.

## Core Concept

A strong data narration is built from four parts, in this order:

| Part | What it does | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Finding first | States the result up front | "Weekend sales ran about 40% higher than weekdays" |
| Exact evidence | Anchors the claim in numbers and scope | "700 vs 500 per day, four weeks of March data" |
| Faithful magnitude | Matches the verb to the size | "40% higher" (a clear lift), not "skyrocketed" |
| Honest causation | Distinguishes correlation from cause | "higher on weekends" (a pattern), not "weekends caused" |

The most technical of these is the magnitude. A percentage change is computed as (new minus old) divided by old: (700 minus 500) divided by 500 equals 0.40, so 40% higher. Stating the percent correctly matters because readers anchor on it, and rounding 40% up to "nearly double" or down to "a bit more" both mislead.

The causation rule is the easiest to violate by accident. Your data shows weekend and high sales occurring together, which is correlation. Calling it "weekends cause higher sales" claims a mechanism the data did not test; a March promotion or payday timing could explain part of the gap. Naming the alternative explanation, and the limit it implies, is what makes the narration trustworthy rather than merely confident.

## Worked Example

Suppose your analysis produced this small summary table.

```
   A          B
1  Day type   Avg daily sales
2  Weekday    500
3  Weekend    700
```

Weekend sales (700) are `(700 - 500) / 500 = 0.40`, so 40% higher than weekday sales.

Step 1. The weak narration describes process: "We pulled four weeks of sales and split them by day type." That tells the reader what you did, not what you found.

Step 2. The strong narration leads with the finding and its implication: "Weekend sales averaged 700 per day versus 500 on weekdays, about 40% higher (4 weeks, March data). Shifting one staff member to weekends would match coverage to demand."

Step 3. Audit the wording. Do not write "weekend sales skyrocketed"; 40% is a clear lift, not a record-breaking surge, so keep the verb honest. Do not write "the weekend caused higher sales"; the data shows a correlation over four weeks, and a March promotion could also explain part of it. Name that limit.

The auditable narration: finding first (40% higher), exact evidence (700 vs 500, March, 4 weeks), faithful magnitude (no inflation), and a correlation flagged as correlation rather than proven cause.

## Decision Rule

Lead with the finding, back it with exact numbers, keep the verb honest, and flag correlation as correlation. If your first sentence describes what you did instead of what you found, rewrite it.

Audit any data sentence against these three tests:

1. **Finding-first test:** Does the opening clause state the result, or does it narrate the procedure?
2. **Evidence test:** Are the supporting numbers, the sample size, and the time window all present and correct?
3. **Restraint test:** Does the verb match the magnitude, and is any cause-language actually supported, or should it say "is associated with"?

If a reader could check your sentence against the data and find it inflated, the restraint test failed.

## Common Misreadings

A frequent misreading is mistaking process for finding. "We analyzed four weeks of sales by day type" feels like a conclusion but only reports activity; the reader still has to extract the point. The finding is the comparison itself (weekends about 40% higher), stated before any description of method.

A second misreading is treating vivid verbs as harmless polish. "Sales skyrocketed" for a 40% rise, or "weekends drive revenue" for a four-week correlation, each smuggles a claim past the data. The honest verb for 40% is "higher" or "rose," and the honest framing for co-occurrence is "associated with," reserving causal language for when you have actually isolated the cause.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the four parts to a different finding: a study-skills workshop where attendees averaged a 6-point higher midterm score than non-attendees (82 vs 76, across 30 students). A weak write-up says, "We compared midterm scores between students who attended the workshop and those who did not." It reports the comparison existed, not its result.

Rewrite finding-first: "Workshop attendees scored about 6 points higher on the midterm (82 vs 76, n equals 30)." Now attach the restraint. Six points on a 100-point exam is a meaningful but modest gap, so do not call it a "dramatic boost." And resist "the workshop raised scores"; attendance was self-selected, so motivated students may both attend workshops and study more. The faithful version adds the limit: "attendees scored higher, though because attendance was voluntary, motivation may explain part of the gap."

The finished narration leads with the result, cites 82 vs 76 over 30 students, keeps the verb proportionate, and labels the self-selection limit, so a skeptic can check every word against the numbers.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

A strong reader audits a data claim the way a fact-checker does, separating what the numbers show from what the sentence asserts. Practice these moves on any finding you read:

1. Recompute the headline percentage from the raw numbers and confirm it is stated faithfully.
2. Underline every verb and ask whether it overstates the magnitude.
3. Flag any sentence that implies cause and ask whether the design could actually support it.

When "sales jumped 50%" makes you immediately check the two numbers and then ask "compared to what baseline, over what period?", you read narration as a claim to be verified, not a story to be enjoyed.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A strong narration artifact pairs the finished sentence with the evidence and the limit, so the claim is auditable. The trace should show the finding-first headline, the numbers behind it, and the one caveat you chose to keep. A model line reads: "Headline: weekend sales about 40% higher (700 vs 500 per day, four weeks, March); I kept the verb at 'higher' rather than 'surged,' and I noted a March promotion as a competing explanation so no one reads it as proven cause."

The weak version is a punchy sentence with no numbers and an unearned causal verb. The strong version lets a reviewer recompute the percentage, confirm the sample, and see that the language stops exactly where the evidence does.

## Transfer Case

The same narration discipline transfers from a number to a trend you describe in words. Suppose your chart shows monthly active users rising every month but with the increases shrinking. A careless narration says "growth is accelerating," which the slowing increases contradict. The faithful version leads with the real finding ("users grew every month, but the monthly gain shrank from 600 to 150"), cites the figures, and resists the upbeat verb. The data shifted from a two-group comparison to a trend over time, but the rules, finding first, exact numbers, honest verb, no overreach, are identical, which shows you can narrate any finding rather than only the one in the worked example.
