# Data Ethics in Small Datasets

![Data Ethics in Small Datasets textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat100-supplement/03-06-data-ethics-in-small-datasets.png)

When a dataset has thousands of rows, one person's record disappears into the crowd. When it has seven rows, every record is a recognizable individual, and the ethical stakes of how you handle and report it rise sharply. A small dataset is the most common kind a student actually works with, a club survey, a class poll, a team roster, and it is precisely where privacy and honesty are easiest to violate by accident.

This section teaches two related disciplines for small data. The first is protecting identity: removing a name is not the same as anonymizing, because a rare combination of attributes can still point to one person. The second is reporting honestly: a percentage over seven people, or an average over a group of one, can mislead a reader into seeing a pattern that is really just noise. Both are decisions you make at the spreadsheet, and both protect real people.

## Opening Case

You survey seven members of a campus club and want to share the results. You delete the name column, feeling responsible, and report that "57% chose option A." Both moves feel careful. Both are quietly unsafe. The row for the only computer-science major living in Hall C still identifies that one person even with the name gone, and "57%" makes seven opinions sound like a representative measurement.

These are not exotic mistakes; they are the default outcome of treating a small dataset like a big one. The work of this section is to slow down at exactly these moments, to recognize when a "deidentified" row is still re-identifiable and when a tidy percentage is hiding a tiny, fragile sample.

## Core Concept

Two ideas do the heavy lifting here. The first is re-identification through quasi-identifiers. A direct identifier (a name, an ID number) names someone outright, but a quasi-identifier is any attribute that, combined with others, narrows the field to one person: major, dorm, age, hometown. In a large dataset many people share each combination; in a seven-row table a single rare combination can be unique. So anonymizing means looking at combinations of columns, not just deleting the name.

The second idea is honest reporting at small n (the number of observations). At small n, two habits protect the reader:

| Practice | Why it matters at small n |
| --- | --- |
| Report raw counts, not just percentages | "4 of 7" is honest; "57%" implies a precision the sample lacks |
| Name the sample size and that it is not representative | Seven club members do not speak for the campus |
| Never publish a statistic over a group of one | An average of one both exposes the person and fakes a pattern |
| Suppress or merge thin categories | A subgroup with n of 1 should be combined, not reported alone |

The throughline is that small numbers demand both more privacy care and more humility in what you claim, because each row is a person and each subgroup may be a single voice.

## Worked Example

Suppose you survey 7 club members and start with this sheet.

```
   A        B     C        D            E
1  Name     Age   Major    Dorm         Pick
2  removed  19    Nursing  Hall B       A
3  removed  20    Biology  Hall A       A
4  removed  19    History  Off-campus   B
5  removed  21    Nursing  Hall B       A
6  removed  22    CompSci  Hall C       A
7  removed  20    Biology  Hall A       B
8  removed  19    Art      Hall B       B
```

Step 1. The name column is blank, but row 6 is still re-identifiable: only one CompSci major lives in Hall C. Deleting the name did not make the dataset anonymous. Combine or generalize the major and dorm columns (for example, group dorms into 'on-campus' vs 'off-campus') so no single combination points to one person.

Step 2. Report the headline honestly. 4 of 7 picked A. Writing '57% chose A' hides that the whole survey was 7 people. The honest statement is '4 of 7 respondents (a small, non-representative sample) chose A'.

Step 3. If you split 'Pick' by Major, several majors have an n of 1. An average or percentage over a group of one both exposes that individual and pretends a single answer is a pattern. Suppress those cells or merge the categories.

The auditable decision: anonymize by combinations not just the name, report raw counts on small n, and never publish a group of one. Each step protects a real person in a 7-row table.

## Decision Rule

Protect the person and respect the sample size. Before you share any small-dataset finding, check that no row is uniquely identifiable, no group of one is reported, and no percentage hides how few people it represents.

Apply this three-point ethics screen to a small dataset:

1. **Re-identification check:** Could any combination of remaining columns single out one individual? If so, generalize or merge those columns.
2. **Group-of-one check:** Does any reported average or percentage cover a subgroup with n of 1? If so, suppress or combine it.
3. **Honest-magnitude check:** Are you reporting raw counts and naming the sample size, rather than a bare percentage that implies precision?

If a friend in the dataset could recognize their own row from your published summary, the re-identification check failed.

## Common Misreadings

The most consequential misreading is "I deleted the names, so it's anonymous." Deletion removes the direct identifier but leaves the quasi-identifiers untouched, and in a seven-row table the lone nursing major in Hall B is as identifiable as if you had printed her name. Anonymizing means flattening rare combinations, often by grouping dorms into on- and off-campus or collapsing majors into broad fields.

A second misreading is preferring percentages because they look professional. "57% chose A" sounds like a poll result; "4 of 7 respondents chose A" tells the truth that the whole study was seven people. At small n the raw count is the honest form, and dropping the sample size from the sentence is itself a small deception.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the screen to a different small dataset: a six-person internship cohort's exit feedback, with columns Name, Department, Hometown, and a 1-to-5 satisfaction score. You want to publish a short summary for next year's applicants.

Start with re-identification. Delete Name, then look at the survivors: only one intern came from the marketing department, and only one listed a small hometown. Either column alone re-identifies someone, so generalize Hometown to a region and report Department only in aggregate, not row by row. Next, the group-of-one check: if you split satisfaction by department, marketing has n of 1, so reporting "marketing scored 2/5" both exposes that intern and treats one opinion as a department verdict. Merge departments into a single overall figure instead.

Finally, honest magnitude. Rather than "67% were satisfied," write "4 of 6 interns rated their experience 4 or higher (small cohort, not representative)." The summary now informs applicants without exposing any single intern or overstating six data points.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

A strong reader of a small-data report instinctively asks who could be identified and how fragile the numbers are. Train these reflexes:

1. Scan for any subgroup small enough that one person defines it, and treat a reported value over that group as a red flag.
2. Mentally reconstruct whether a known individual's row could be picked out from the published attributes.
3. Convert every percentage back to a count over the stated n, and notice when the n is missing.

When a clean-looking "73% agree" makes you immediately ask "out of how many?", you are reading the ethics, not just the result.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A strong small-data artifact documents the privacy and honesty decisions, not just the polished chart. The trace should name what you generalized to prevent re-identification, which thin groups you suppressed, and why you reported counts with the sample size. A model line reads: "I grouped dorms into on- and off-campus because the Hall C plus computer-science combination identified one member; I reported '4 of 7 chose A' rather than '57%' so no reader overreads a seven-person poll."

The weak version shares a tidy percentage and a deidentified-looking table. The strong version makes the protective choices visible, so a reviewer can confirm that no individual is exposed and no claim outruns the sample.

## Transfer Case

The same ethics screen transfers from a survey to an open-ended dataset, where the risk hides in the text. Suppose you collect short written comments from eight tutoring clients to share themes. There is no name column, but a comment that mentions a specific course, a specific time slot, and a personal detail can identify the writer as surely as a name. The re-identification check now means scanning quotes for identifying specifics and paraphrasing them, and the group-of-one check means not attributing a unique, recognizable comment to its lone author. The data type changed from numbers to text, but the duty, prevent re-identification and do not over-claim from few voices, is the same, which shows the screen is about the people, not the cell format.
