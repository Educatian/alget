## Core Concept

The core idea in this section is that **Module 3 Data Story Studio** should be treated as a relationship among purpose, constraint, evidence, and revision. Purpose names what the learner or audience needs to accomplish. Constraint names what limits the decision. Evidence names the source that makes the decision defensible. Revision shows what changed after the evidence was considered.

Three course anchors shape the reasoning here:

- **NACE career readiness** helps you decide what counts as a meaningful learning or performance demand.
- **ISTE digital citizenship** helps you notice when a design or technology choice creates risk, burden, or unsupported assumptions.
- **data-ink and visual evidence** helps you check whether the artifact works for varied learners, audiences, or use contexts.

Read the anchors as competing checks on quality. One may highlight the learning demand, another may expose a risk, and another may help you test transfer.

## Worked Example

This capstone chains every skill from Module 3 into one verifiable data story. Start from a raw export of six rows.

```
   A       B       C
1  Region  Order   Sales
2  west    1001    400
3  East    1002    250
4  West    1003    600
5  south   1004    150
6  East    1005    300
7  West    1006    200
```

Step 1 (clean). Standardize Region case so `west`/`West` and `south`/`South` are consistent: West, East, South. Confirm Sales are numbers, not text.

Step 2 (summarize). Build a pivot with Region in Rows and Sum of Sales in Values: West = 1200, East = 550, South = 150. Grand Total = 1900.

Step 3 (chart). The question is "which region led?", a category comparison, so use a bar chart with the value axis starting at 0. West's bar is more than double East's, matching 1200 vs 550.

Step 4 (narrate). Write a claim-first headline tied to the numbers: "West led Q1 sales at 1,200, more than double East (550); source: store export, 6 orders." Note the small n so no one over-reads it.

Step 5 (reconcile). The headline number (1,200 for West) must equal the West row in the pivot and the height of the West bar. If the headline said 2,400, that mismatch means a stale pivot range or a filter, and you fix it before publishing.

The auditable package: cleaned data, pivot summary, honest chart, claim-first headline, source note, and a reconciliation check. A reviewer can re-trace every number from the six raw rows.

## Decision Rule

Use the audience first, the evidence second, and the tool third. A digital product is justified only when another person can see what changed, why it changed, and what evidence supports the change.

A strong submission should survive three quick tests:

1. **Purpose check:** What should the audience be able to do after reading or using the artifact?
2. **Evidence check:** Which exact source, observation, annotation, rubric line, data pattern, or policy boundary justifies the revision?
3. **Revision check:** What changed in the artifact, and what suggestion did you reject or modify?

If you cannot answer all three questions, the artifact may be complete, but it is not yet ready as evidence of learning.

## Common Misreadings

Another misreading is to write about the tool instead of showing what the tool helped you revise.

In this course, quality means another reader can verify what changed and why.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Read this section as a small professional decision cycle, not as a list of terms. First, locate the work product: **Excel pivot-table finding with unit labels, source note, and claim-first chart title**. Then ask what problem the artifact is supposed to solve inside spreadsheet data stories. A strong reader can explain the artifact in plain language before naming any theory: who will use it, what decision it supports, and what would count as evidence that it improved.

Next, connect the artifact to the course anchors: **NACE career readiness**, **ISTE digital citizenship**, **data-ink and visual evidence**. The point is not to cite every anchor. The point is to let one anchor change the artifact. If the anchor does not change wording, layout, evidence, timing, or feedback, it has not yet become design reasoning.

Finally, inspect the revision trail. A weak submission says the artifact was improved. A strong submission shows the earlier version, the issue noticed, the support requested, the decision accepted or rejected, and the reason the final version is better for the intended learner or audience.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: **claim** on the left, **evidence** on the right. The claim is the decision you think the section is teaching. The evidence is the exact phrase, example, annotation, support move, or artifact revision that makes the claim inspectable. By the end of CAT 100 03.08, your notes should make it possible to reconstruct your reasoning without asking you to explain it again.

A strong reader of Module 3 Data Story Studio can answer four questions:

1. What is the real decision hidden inside this topic?
2. Which constraint makes the decision difficult?
3. What evidence would persuade a skeptical reviewer?
4. What would you revise if the audience, learner, classroom, or tool changed?
