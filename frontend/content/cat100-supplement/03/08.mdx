# Module 3 Data Story Studio

![Module 3 Data Story Studio textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat100-supplement/03-08-module-3-data-story-studio.png)

This is the capstone of the module, where the separate skills become one pipeline. Cleaning, formulas, pivots, charts, ethics, and narration are not five unrelated lessons; they are stages of a single workflow that turns a raw export into a data story a reader can trust. The studio asks you to run that whole pipeline end to end on one dataset and to show that every number ties back to the original rows.

The skill that only appears at the full-pipeline level is reconciliation: making sure the headline number, the pivot cell, and the chart bar all agree, because each stage can introduce a mismatch the previous stage hid. A clean sheet can still feed a stale pivot; an honest pivot can still feed a misleading chart; an accurate chart can still get a wrong headline. The data story is finished only when the chain holds from raw row to published claim.

## Opening Case

You have a raw store export of six orders. By the end of this studio you will hand a reader a single slide: a claim-first headline, an honest bar chart, a source note, and a small-sample caveat. But between the export and the slide are five decisions, and a slip at any one of them quietly corrupts the slide while leaving it looking polished.

Suppose your headline says West led at 2,400 but the pivot row for West reads 1,200. The slide looks finished; the chain is broken. That mismatch is almost always a stale pivot range or an active filter, and a reader who re-traces your numbers will catch it even though the chart is beautifully formatted. The studio is where you learn to catch it first, by reconciling the headline against the pivot against the bar before anything ships.

## Core Concept

The data-story pipeline has five stages, each drawing on one earlier section, plus a reconciliation gate at the end:

| Stage | Draws on | What it produces | Failure it prevents |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Clean | 03.01 | A tidy table, consistent labels, numeric values | Split categories, skipped numbers |
| Summarize | 03.04 | A pivot with the right aggregation | Answering the wrong question |
| Chart | 03.03 | An honest figure for the real question | A misleading axis or chart type |
| Narrate | 03.07 | A claim-first, faithful headline | Overstated or buried findings |
| Protect | 03.06 | A sample-size caveat where n is small | Over-reading a tiny dataset |

The reconciliation gate sits across all five. Its rule is simple and strict: the number in your headline must equal the matching pivot cell, which must equal the height of the matching chart bar. If West is 1,200 in the pivot, the West bar must read 1,200 and the headline must say 1,200. Three independent representations of the same fact, forced to agree, is what makes a data story checkable. When they disagree, the disagreement points to the exact stage that failed, usually a stale pivot range or a filter left on.

## Worked Example

This capstone chains every skill from Module 3 into one verifiable data story. Start from a raw export of six rows.

```
   A       B       C
1  Region  Order   Sales
2  west    1001    400
3  East    1002    250
4  West    1003    600
5  south   1004    150
6  East    1005    300
7  West    1006    200
```

Step 1 (clean). Standardize Region case so `west`/`West` and `south`/`South` are consistent: West, East, South. Confirm Sales are numbers, not text.

Step 2 (summarize). Build a pivot with Region in Rows and Sum of Sales in Values: West = 1200, East = 550, South = 150. Grand Total = 1900.

Step 3 (chart). The question is "which region led?", a category comparison, so use a bar chart with the value axis starting at 0. West's bar is more than double East's, matching 1200 vs 550.

Step 4 (narrate). Write a claim-first headline tied to the numbers: "West led Q1 sales at 1,200, more than double East (550); source: store export, 6 orders." Note the small n so no one over-reads it.

Step 5 (reconcile). The headline number (1,200 for West) must equal the West row in the pivot and the height of the West bar. If the headline said 2,400, that mismatch means a stale pivot range or a filter, and you fix it before publishing.

The auditable package: cleaned data, pivot summary, honest chart, claim-first headline, source note, and a reconciliation check. A reviewer can re-trace every number from the six raw rows.

## Decision Rule

Run the five stages in order and do not publish until the reconciliation gate passes. Cleaning before summarizing, summarizing before charting, and reconciling before sharing is not bureaucracy; each stage assumes the one before it is correct.

Before the data story ships, confirm the chain end to end:

1. **Trace-back check:** Can every number in the headline be re-derived from the raw rows by re-running clean and summarize?
2. **Three-way match check:** Does the headline number equal the pivot cell and the chart bar for the same category?
3. **Caveat check:** Does the story name its source and, when n is small, say so plainly?

If the headline, the pivot, and the bar do not all show the same figure, stop and find which stage drifted before you do anything else.

## Common Misreadings

The most common capstone error is polishing the slide before reconciling the numbers. A crisp chart with a confident headline can still carry a 2,400-versus-1,200 mismatch from a stale pivot, and formatting effort spent before the three-way match just makes a wrong story look more credible. Reconcile first, format last.

A second misreading is assuming that because each stage worked alone, the pipeline works together. The pivot may have been correct yesterday, but if you cleaned a new batch of rows this morning without refreshing, the chart now disagrees with the data. Integration introduces failures no single stage could, which is exactly why the reconciliation gate exists.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Run the full pipeline on a different raw export: a semester of help-desk tickets with columns Building, Date, and Minutes-to-resolve, mixed case and a few durations typed as text. The question is "which building's tickets took longest on average?"

Clean first: standardize Building case so `Library` and `library` merge, and strip the `min` suffix from text durations so Minutes is numeric. Summarize next: a pivot with Building in Rows and Minutes in Values, aggregation set to Average, not Sum, because the question is about typical resolution time. Chart third: a bar chart with the axis at 0, since this is a category comparison, so the longest building's bar is honestly proportional. Narrate fourth: "Science Hall tickets took longest to resolve, averaging 47 minutes versus 22 in the Library (one semester, 60 tickets)." Protect: 60 tickets is modest and one semester, so note it is not a permanent ranking.

Now the reconciliation gate. The headline 47 minutes must equal Science Hall's pivot row and the height of its bar. If the bar reads 47 but the headline says 52, you mistyped the headline; if the pivot reads 38, your chart is built on a stale range. Only when all three agree, traceable back to the cleaned tickets, is the data story done.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

A strong reader of a finished data story works backward from the headline to the raw data, checking that the chain holds at every link. The audit path:

1. Take the headline number and find it in the chart, then in the pivot.
2. From the pivot cell, identify which source rows feed it and re-sum or re-average them by hand.
3. Confirm the source note and sample size match what the pivot actually covers.

When you can take any published claim and walk it back to the original rows without the author's help, you are reading the pipeline's integrity, not just its surface.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A strong capstone artifact exposes the whole chain and an explicit reconciliation step, not just the polished slide. The trace should list the cleaning fixes, the pivot aggregation, the chart choice, the headline, the caveat, and the three-way match. A model line reads: "Cleaned region case and stripped text from Sales; pivot Sum by region gives West 1,200; the West bar reads 1,200 and the headline says 1,200; source is the store export of six orders, a small sample, so I flagged it."

The weak version submits a clean-looking slide and asserts the numbers are right. The strong version lets a reviewer re-derive the headline from the raw rows and see the headline, pivot, and bar agreeing, so the entire story is checkable from export to claim.

## Transfer Case

The same pipeline-with-reconciliation transfers to any tool that turns records into a summarized claim, including the dashboard tools in later modules. Move the help-desk data into a reporting dashboard and the stages persist: you still clean the source, define an aggregation, choose a visual, write a headline, and note the sample, and you still must confirm that the dashboard's big number equals the underlying query equals the displayed chart. The interface changed from Excel to a dashboard, but the integrity discipline, every number traceable to source and three representations forced to agree, is what carries over, which is the real product of this module.
