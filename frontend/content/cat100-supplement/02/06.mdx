# Revision Logs

![Revision Logs textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat100-supplement/02-06-revision-logs.png)

## Why This Section Matters

"I revised my resume" is a claim no one can check. A revision log turns that claim into a record: a dated, located list of what you changed, why, and what prompted each change. The skill matters in two ways. First, it makes your improvement visible to an instructor or reviewer who needs to see judgment, not just a polished final file. Second, and more practically, it protects you from your own edits; when a change turns out to be wrong, a log lets you trace back to the version that worked instead of trying to remember what you did three drafts ago.

This is the same instinct behind version control in software (Git commit messages are revision logs), behind a lab notebook in science, and behind tracked changes in a shared document. CAT 100 teaches the plain-table version so the habit is yours before the tools get fancy. Your deliverable demonstrates that you can keep a change history a stranger could read and act on.

## Learning Targets

## Opening Case

Theo changed his resume five times in a week. On Friday a mentor asks, "the version you sent Tuesday had a line about the inventory project, where did it go?" Theo has no idea. He overwrote the file each time, so the Tuesday wording is gone, and he cannot remember whether he cut the line on purpose or by accident. He spends an hour rebuilding something he already had. A one-line log entry ("06/03, cut inventory bullet, felt redundant with the warehouse bullet") would have answered the mentor in five seconds and let Theo restore the line if he changed his mind.

The case shows that the cost of not logging is paid later, in lost work and unanswerable questions. The log is cheap insurance written at the moment of the change, when the reason is still fresh.

## Core Concept

A usable revision log is a table with one row per change and three load-bearing columns: **what changed** (located precisely, "Summary, line 2," not "the top part"), **why** (the reason or judgment), and **what prompted it** (the feedback, observation, or tool suggestion that triggered the edit). A date or version stamp on each row turns the table into a timeline you can walk backward.

Two properties separate a real log from a vague note:

| Property | Strong log | Weak note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Locatable | "Experience, bullet 1: added '~80 customers/shift'" | "Improved my experience section" |
| Traceable in time | Dated rows, originals kept | One overwritten file |

The reason precise location matters is that a reviewer (or future you) must be able to find the exact spot the entry describes. The reason you keep originals rather than overwriting is that a change history only has value if the earlier states still exist; a log that points to versions you deleted is a list of ghosts. Polish does not enter here at all, the log is not meant to be pretty, it is meant to be auditable.

## Worked Example: Building a Three-Column Revision Log

A revision log turns "I improved my resume" into a record a reader can trace. The reliable shape has three columns: **what changed, why, and what prompted it.**

**Step 1. Capture each edit as one row, in order.**

| Date | Location and change | Why / prompt |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 06/02 | Summary, line 2: "good with computers" to "proficient in Excel and SQL" | Career-center feedback flagged the phrase as vague |
| 06/03 | Experience, bullet 1: added "served ~80 customers per shift" | Needed a measurable result; pulled the number from my POS reports |
| 06/04 | Skills: removed "Microsoft Word (expert)" | AI tool suggested it as filler; I rejected the "expert" label as not defensible |

**Step 2. Check that each row is locatable.** Every row names a section and a line, not "fixed some stuff." A reviewer could open the resume and find each edit.

**Step 3. Make the AI role visible.** Row 06/04 records that the edit started as a suggestion from an AI assistant (such as ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot, or Claude) and that you rejected the inflated part. The log now shows your judgment over the tool, not just the tool's output. Because model versions change, record which tool and version produced the suggestion as part of "what prompted it."

**Step 4. Keep the history, do not overwrite.** Because each version is dated, if the 06/03 number turns out wrong you can trace back to the 06/02 version instead of losing it.

The deliverable is the table itself: three columns, one row per change, AI decisions included.

## Decision Rule

Write the log entry at the moment of the change, not from memory afterward.

1. **Locate the change exactly:** name the section and line, so a reader can find it.
2. **State the reason and the prompt:** why you made it and what triggered it (feedback, a metric, an AI suggestion). One sentence each is enough.
3. **Flag any AI involvement explicitly:** if a tool suggested the edit, record what you accepted and what you rejected, so your judgment over the tool is on the record.
4. **Preserve the prior version:** date the rows and keep originals; never overwrite the only copy of a draft you might want back.

A log you can read a month later and act on, restore a line, answer a reviewer, defend a choice, is the standard. A list of vague "fixed things" entries fails it.

## Common Misreadings

One misreading is treating the log as busywork to do at the end. A log reconstructed from memory after the fact loses the prompts and reasons that gave it value; you end up writing "improved several lines" because the specifics are gone. The log is a real-time tool or it is nothing.

A second misreading is logging only the final, successful changes and hiding the edits you later reversed. But the reversals are often the most informative entries: they show what you tried, why it did not work, and how your judgment developed. A truthful log includes the dead ends.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Suppose career-center feedback flags your summary as vague, and you make three edits over three days. Day one: in the summary, line two, you change "good with computers" to "proficient in Excel and SQL," prompted by that feedback. Log it with the date, the exact location, and the prompt. Day two: in experience, bullet one, you add "served about 80 customers per shift," prompted by needing a measurable result, with the number pulled from your point-of-sale reports. Log it, naming the source of the number. Day three: an AI assistant (such as ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot, or Claude) suggests labeling your Word skills as "expert"; you reject the inflated label and instead remove the line, and you log both the suggestion and your rejection.

Read the resulting table. Every row names a section and a line, so a reviewer could open the resume and find each edit. The day-three row shows the AI's role and your override, which turns "I used AI" into a defensible decision. Because each row is dated and the prior versions are kept, if the day-two customer count later proves wrong, you can trace back to the day-one version instead of losing it. The table itself, three columns, one row per change, AI decisions included, is the deliverable; nothing about it needs to be polished, only complete and locatable.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

A strong reader recognizes the revision log as the same idea they will meet again as Git commit messages, document version history, and lab notebooks, and treats the CAT 100 table as the training-wheels version of a career-long habit. Seeing the pattern across tools is the sign the concept landed.

A strong reader also writes log entries a stranger could act on, not shorthand only they understand. "Fixed bullet 2" means nothing to a reviewer; "added customer-count metric to bullet 2 from POS report" means everything. The audience for the log is someone who was not there, and writing for that reader is the skill.

## Transfer Case

The discipline of a dated, located, reasoned change history transfers anywhere edits accumulate over time. A group project benefits from a shared change log so teammates know who changed what and why; a budget spreadsheet benefits from a notes column recording each adjustment's reason; a piece of code benefits from commit messages that a future maintainer can read. The format scales from a plain table to full version-control software, but the three questions, what changed, why, and what prompted it, are the same ones you practiced on a resume here.
