# AI Feedback You Should Reject

![AI Feedback You Should Reject textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat100-supplement/02-05-ai-feedback-you-should-reject.png)

## Why This Section Matters

AI writing tools are fluent and confident, and that combination is exactly what makes them dangerous for a resume. A tool will happily suggest you add a leadership award you never won, inflate a GPA, or stuff a keyword to "beat the applicant tracking system," and it will phrase the suggestion as helpful expertise. The earlier sections taught you to use AI critique; this one teaches the inverse and more important skill, recognizing which AI suggestions to refuse. "The AI said so" is never a defense in an interview, and a fabricated line is your problem, not the tool's, the moment a recruiter asks about it.

This matters because the cost is asymmetric. A rejected good suggestion costs you a small improvement; an accepted bad one can cost you the job, or get an offer rescinded when a background check or a single interview question exposes it. The deliverable is a critique log where the rejected suggestions, each with a reason, carry as much weight as the accepted ones.

**Recent context (2026).** The tools students actually use for resume rewriting are general assistants such as ChatGPT, Claude, Microsoft Copilot, and Google Gemini, plus the AI resume features now bundled into LinkedIn, Microsoft 365, and Google Workspace. All of them produce confident fabrications, so the defensibility test this section teaches is tool-agnostic: it applies unchanged no matter which assistant wrote the suggestion.

## Opening Case

Lena pastes her resume into an AI assistant and asks it to "make it more competitive." It returns a polished version that reads beautifully, and buried inside are two changes she did not ask for: it relabeled her "volunteer tutor" role as "Lead Education Coordinator," and it added "fluent in Spanish" because her name looked Hispanic to the model. Both sound impressive. Both are false. If Lena accepts the clean-looking draft without auditing it line by line, she walks into an interview defending a title she never held and a language she does not speak.

The case captures the trap: bad AI suggestions do not arrive flagged as bad. They come wrapped in the same confident, well-formatted prose as the good ones, which is why blanket acceptance, not malice, is the usual path to a fabricated resume.

## Core Concept

The governing tool of this section is the **defensibility test**: keep a suggestion only if you could honestly defend the resulting claim when a recruiter asks about it. The test cuts cleanly through fluent phrasing because it ignores how good the suggestion sounds and asks only whether the claim it creates is true and yours. Apply it to every suggestion, including the ones you like.

It helps to know the common categories of AI feedback that fail the test:

| Category | What it looks like | Why it fails |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fabrication | Adds an award, title, or skill you do not have | The claim is false |
| Inflation | Upgrades "member" to "lead," "good" to "expert" | Overstates a real fact |
| Keyword stuffing | Repeats buzzwords to "beat the ATS" | Signals no real skill; modern systems flag it |
| Identity guessing | Assumes a language, demographic, or background | Invents a fact about you |

Notice these are different from the suggestions you should keep, which are truthful cleanups: removing a dead line, replacing a weak verb with an accurate stronger one, fixing formatting. The skill is sorting, not blanket trust or blanket suspicion. A good suggestion makes a true claim easier to read; a bad one makes a false claim easier to believe.

## Worked Example: Sorting Four AI Suggestions With the Defensibility Test

You paste your resume into an AI tool and it returns four suggestions. The skill in this section is deciding which to keep. Run each through one test: **could I defend the resulting claim if a recruiter asked about it?**

**Step 1. List the raw suggestions.**

| # | AI suggestion |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | "Remove 'References available upon request'; it is outdated and wastes a line." |
| 2 | "Add a leadership award to strengthen your profile." |
| 3 | "Repeat the word 'synergy' three times so you pass the applicant tracking system." |
| 4 | "Change your GPA from 3.1 to 3.8 so you clear the cutoff." |

**Step 2. Apply the defensibility test to each.**

| # | Defensible if questioned? | Decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Yes; trimming a dead line is truthful and standard | KEEP |
| 2 | No; the award does not exist | REJECT |
| 3 | No; stuffed keyword reflects no real skill | REJECT |
| 4 | No; falsifying a GPA is dishonest and checkable | REJECT |

**Step 3. Record the rejections, not just the keep.** Your log should read:

```
Kept #1 (truthful cleanup). Rejected #2, #3, #4 because each would create a
claim I could not defend honestly in an interview. Net change: removed one
outdated line; no fabricated content added.
```

The point of this section is that "the AI said so" is never the reason. The defensibility test is, and three of four confident suggestions failed it.

## Decision Rule

Audit every AI suggestion before accepting any of them, even the whole-document rewrites.

1. **Run the defensibility test:** Could I truthfully answer a recruiter who asked me to elaborate on this exact claim? If no, reject it.
2. **Name the failure category:** Is it fabrication, inflation, keyword stuffing, or identity guessing? Naming it makes the rejection clear in your log.
3. **Check whole-document rewrites line by line:** A clean-looking rewrite can smuggle a false claim into the middle. Compare against your original.
4. **Log the rejection with its reason:** "Rejected, fabricated award" is the evidence that you exercised judgment.

The asymmetry guides the default: when unsure whether a flattering claim is defensible, leave it out. A missed embellishment costs nothing; an indefensible one can cost the offer.

## Common Misreadings

The most damaging misreading is "if I did not write it, I am not responsible for it." A recruiter does not care that an AI added the fake title; you submitted it, so you own it. Outsourcing the words does not outsource the accountability.

A second misreading is overcorrecting into refusing all AI help. The goal is not suspicion of every suggestion; truthful cleanups (trimming dead lines, sharpening accurate verbs) are worth keeping. Rejecting good edits along with the bad ones throws away the tool's real value. The skill is the sort, keep the defensible, refuse the rest.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Walk through one whole-document rewrite. You ask a tool to "polish my resume," and it returns a cleaner version. Reading line by line against your original, you find four changes: it tightened a wordy summary (good), it changed "assisted the manager" to "co-managed the department" (inflation, you did not co-manage), it added "Certified Project Manager" (fabrication, no such certification), and it inserted "leadership, leadership, dynamic leadership" into the skills line (keyword stuffing).

Apply the defensibility test to each. The tightened summary survives, you can defend every word. The other three fail: you cannot defend a title you did not hold, a certification you never earned, or a stuffed word that names no real skill. Your log records "accepted summary tightening; rejected co-managed (inflation), Certified Project Manager (fabrication), and repeated leadership (keyword stuffing)." The polished draft looked uniformly good, but one of its four changes was honest. The walkthrough shows why a per-line audit, not a glance at the formatting, is the only safe way to accept an AI rewrite.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

A strong reader is most skeptical precisely when a suggestion flatters them. The pleasant jolt of seeing yourself described as a "lead" or an "expert" is the signal to slow down and run the defensibility test, not the signal to accept. Comfortable suggestions are where fabrication hides.

A strong reader also distinguishes a writing tool's two reliability levels: AI is generally trustworthy about surface phrasing and generally untrustworthy about facts (your titles, dates, certifications, skills). Weighting the advice accordingly, light verification on wording, hard verification on every factual claim, is what reading this section well produces.

## Transfer Case

The defensibility test transfers to every place AI generates content you will put your name on. An AI-drafted cover letter may invent a connection to the company you do not have; an AI-summarized research finding may overstate what the study showed; an AI-written email may promise a deadline you cannot meet. The category names shift, but the question is constant: can I stand behind this claim if challenged? If you can apply that test to a context this section never mentioned, you have learned the skill rather than the resume example.
