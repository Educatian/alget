![Module 2 Branding Studio textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat100-supplement/02-08-module-2-branding-studio.png)

## Why This Section Matters

This is the capstone of Module 2, where the separate pieces (resume bullets, a bio, the two-AI critique workflow, LinkedIn and portfolio alignment, the AI suggestions you rejected, your revision log, and peer feedback) come together into one branding package. The skill the studio tests is the consistency pass: laying your finished artifacts side by side and catching the contradictions a recruiter would catch, before they do. A package can have a strong resume and a strong LinkedIn and still fail, if the two disagree about your graduation date or your title.

It matters because this is how your materials will actually be read in the world: not one document in isolation, but several at once, by someone deciding whether the story holds together. The deliverable is the assembled package plus an AI-use log, and the test for "ready" is not how polished any single piece looks but whether any two pieces agree on the facts that matter.

## Learning Targets

- Build a cross-artifact comparison table (name, title, dates, headline proof) and identify contradictions that live between artifacts rather than inside them.
- Distinguish artifact polish from evidence-based revision.
- Document a bounded AI/software support move with accepted and rejected suggestions.
- Resolve cross-artifact contradictions toward the truth and complete an AI-use log before declaring the package ready.

## Opening Case

Jordan finishes the module feeling done: the resume is sharp, the bio reads well, the LinkedIn is filled out, the portfolio is up. But laying all four next to each other for the studio pass reveals trouble. The resume and bio say "graduating May 2026"; LinkedIn still says "Dec 2025" from an earlier plan. The portfolio lists no title at all, and the LinkedIn headline says vaguely "data projects" while everything else points to a specific Tableau dashboard. Each artifact looked finished on its own; together they tell three slightly different stories about the same person.

The case shows why the capstone is a consistency check rather than a polishing pass. The errors that survive into a final package are usually not within any one document but between documents, exactly the seams a careful recruiter inspects.

## Core Concept

The studio pass is a **cross-artifact consistency audit**. You build a comparison table with one row per artifact and one column per fact that a reader cross-references: name, title or role, graduation or key dates, and headline accomplishment. Then you scan down each column looking for cells that disagree. Any column with two different values is a contradiction to resolve, and you resolve it by fixing the facts toward the truth, not by making the prettiest version win.

The discipline is summarized in one principle and one readiness test:

| Fact column | What must be true | Common drift |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Name | One consistent display form | "Jordan Ellis" vs "Jordan E." |
| Title / role | Same across artifacts | "major" vs "student" vs blank |
| Dates | Identical graduation/role dates | An old plan left on one platform |
| Headline proof | The same concrete accomplishment leads | A specific proof on one, vague on another |

The readiness test is blunt: pick any two of your artifacts and confirm they agree on all four facts. If they do, and your AI-use log explains where a tool helped, the package is ready to submit. Polishing fonts on a package that still has a date mismatch is effort spent on the wrong layer; the consistency of the facts is the layer that decides whether the package earns trust.

## Worked Example: A Final Consistency Pass on the Brand Package

The studio capstone is the whole package: resume, bio, LinkedIn, portfolio, plus an AI-use log. The deliverable skill is the consistency pass that catches contradictions before a recruiter does.

**Step 1. Lay the four facts side by side, one row per artifact.**

| Artifact | Name | Title | Graduation | Headline accomplishment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Resume | Jordan Ellis | MIS major | May 2026 | Tableau sales dashboard |
| Bio | Jordan Ellis | MIS student | May 2026 | Tableau sales dashboard |
| LinkedIn | Jordan E. | MIS major | Dec 2025 | "data projects" |
| Portfolio | Jordan Ellis | (none listed) | (none) | Tableau dashboard |

**Step 2. Circle the contradictions.** LinkedIn says "Dec 2025" while everything else says "May 2026," and the LinkedIn headline is vaguer than the dashboard proof used elsewhere. The portfolio lists no title.

**Step 3. Fix the facts, not the fonts.**

```
- LinkedIn graduation: Dec 2025 -> May 2026 (matches resume/bio)
- LinkedIn headline: "data projects" -> "MIS student | Built a Tableau sales dashboard"
- Portfolio: add title "MIS major, expected May 2026"
- Display name: standardize "Jordan Ellis" across all four
```

**Step 4. Complete the AI-use log row.**

```
Used ChatGPT to brainstorm 5 LinkedIn headlines. Chose the one matching my real
dashboard work, rejected 2 that claimed "expert analyst," rewrote in my voice.
```

Any current assistant (ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot, or Claude) fits the brainstorm step; what matters is that the log names the tool you actually used.

**Step 5. Declare readiness by the right test.** The package is ready because any two artifacts now agree on name, title, date, and proof, and the log explains where a tool helped. That, not polish, is the submit signal.

## Decision Rule

Run the studio pass in this order before you declare the package ready.

1. **Tabulate the four facts** (name, title, dates, headline proof) with one row per artifact, so the columns line up for scanning.
2. **Circle every column that disagrees** and resolve it toward the truth, then update the artifacts that were wrong.
3. **Fix facts before fonts:** finish all the consistency edits before touching any design polish, so you do not waste styling effort on a draft that still contradicts itself.
4. **Complete the AI-use log** so any place a tool helped (brainstorming headlines, tightening a bullet) is on the record, with what you rejected.

The submit signal is the readiness test, not a feeling of doneness: any two artifacts agree on all four facts, and the log explains the tool's role. Until then, the package is finished-looking, not finished.

## Common Misreadings

One misreading is that finishing each piece equals finishing the package. The whole point of the capstone is that contradictions live between artifacts, not inside them, so a stack of individually strong documents can still fail the audit. Done-per-piece is not done-per-package.

A second misreading is reaching for design polish to signal completeness. A new template or font makes the package feel professional while leaving a graduation-date mismatch untouched, which is the one thing a recruiter would actually flag. Polish is the last layer; the fact audit is the layer that determines whether the package can be trusted.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Audit a four-artifact package. The comparison table shows name as "Jordan Ellis" three times and "Jordan E." once; title as "MIS major," "MIS student," "MIS major," and blank; graduation as May 2026 everywhere except LinkedIn's leftover "Dec 2025"; and the headline proof as the Tableau dashboard everywhere except LinkedIn's vague "data projects." Scanning the columns surfaces four contradictions at once, none visible while looking at any single document.

Resolve toward the truth. Standardize the display name to "Jordan Ellis" across all four. Fix LinkedIn's graduation to May 2026 to match the resume and bio. Add the missing portfolio title, "MIS major, expected May 2026." Rewrite the LinkedIn headline from "data projects" to "MIS student building a Tableau sales dashboard," so the strongest proof leads everywhere. Then complete the log row: used AI to brainstorm five LinkedIn headlines, chose the one matching the real dashboard work, rejected two that claimed "expert analyst," rewrote the keeper in your own voice. Now any two artifacts agree on name, title, date, and proof, and the log explains the tool's role, which is the readiness standard the studio is built to enforce.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

A strong reader audits across documents rather than within them, putting the artifacts in one view and reading down each fact column instead of admiring each page in turn. The contradictions that matter are only visible in the comparison, so the comparison is where attention belongs.

A strong reader also recognizes the capstone as the place every prior skill in the module gets used at once: the evidence discipline from the resume section, the AI-rejection judgment, the alignment check, and the revision log all converge here. Treating the studio as a synthesis, not a fresh task, is the sign the module landed.

## Transfer Case

The cross-artifact consistency audit is how any multi-piece deliverable should be finished. A job application's resume, cover letter, and references must agree on dates and titles; a course project's report, slides, and demo must agree on the headline result; a small organization's website, brochure, and social profiles must agree on its mission and contact details. The four fact columns change with the deliverable, but the move, tabulate the facts, scan for disagreement, resolve toward truth, then polish, is the same final discipline you practiced on your branding package here.
