## Core Concept

The core idea in this section is that **Professional Bio Drafting** should be treated as a relationship among purpose, constraint, evidence, and revision. Purpose names what the learner or audience needs to accomplish. Constraint names what limits the decision. Evidence names the source that makes the decision defensible. Revision shows what changed after the evidence was considered.

Three course anchors shape the reasoning here:

- **NACE career readiness** helps you decide what counts as a meaningful learning or performance demand.
- **ISTE digital citizenship** helps you notice when a design or technology choice creates risk, burden, or unsupported assumptions.
- **data-ink and visual evidence** helps you check whether the artifact works for varied learners, audiences, or use contexts.

Read the anchors as competing checks on quality. One may highlight the learning demand, another may expose a risk, and another may help you test transfer.

## Worked Example: Drafting a Bio With the Role-Focus-Proof Pattern

A strong short bio follows a simple three-part pattern: **who you are, what you focus on, one concrete proof.** Watch it built from a weak draft.

**Step 1. The weak first draft.**

```
I am a passionate, results-driven student who loves technology and is
always looking to grow and learn new things every single day.
```

Nothing here is checkable. Swap in any name and it still fits.

**Step 2. Gather raw facts the student can defend.**

| Slot | Fact |
| --- | --- |
| Who you are | Sophomore, Management Information Systems |
| Focus | Data visualization |
| Proof | Built a Tableau sales dashboard for a 30-person student org |

**Step 3. Assemble the pattern, in first person for a LinkedIn About.**

```
I am a sophomore studying Management Information Systems, focused on data
visualization. Last semester I built an interactive Tableau dashboard that
tracked event sales for a 30-person student organization.
```

**Step 4. Audit against the pattern.** Sentence opens with the role (sophomore, MIS), names the focus (data visualization), and lands one concrete proof (Tableau dashboard, 30-person org). Every adjective from the weak draft is gone, and a reader now has something specific to ask about.

**Step 5. Note the AI boundary.** If an AI draft had added "five years leading cross-functional teams," the student strikes it (a sophomore has no such record) and logs the removed claim.

## Decision Rule

Use the audience first, the evidence second, and the tool third. A digital product is justified only when another person can see what changed, why it changed, and what evidence supports the change.

A strong submission should survive three quick tests:

1. **Purpose check:** What should the audience be able to do after reading or using the artifact?
2. **Evidence check:** Which exact source, observation, annotation, rubric line, data pattern, or policy boundary justifies the revision?
3. **Revision check:** What changed in the artifact, and what suggestion did you reject or modify?

If you cannot answer all three questions, the artifact may be complete, but it is not yet ready as evidence of learning.

## Common Misreadings

Another misreading is to write about the tool instead of showing what the tool helped you revise.

In this course, quality means another reader can verify what changed and why.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Read this section as a small professional decision cycle, not as a list of terms. First, locate the work product: **resume before-after revision with truthful evidence, role fit, and AI-use note**. Then ask what problem the artifact is supposed to solve inside professional identity materials. A strong reader can explain the artifact in plain language before naming any theory: who will use it, what decision it supports, and what would count as evidence that it improved.

Next, connect the artifact to the course anchors: **NACE career readiness**, **ISTE digital citizenship**, **data-ink and visual evidence**. The point is not to cite every anchor. The point is to let one anchor change the artifact. If the anchor does not change wording, layout, evidence, timing, or feedback, it has not yet become design reasoning.

Finally, inspect the revision trail. A weak submission says the artifact was improved. A strong submission shows the earlier version, the issue noticed, the support requested, the decision accepted or rejected, and the reason the final version is better for the intended learner or audience.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: **claim** on the left, **evidence** on the right. The claim is the decision you think the section is teaching. The evidence is the exact phrase, example, annotation, support move, or artifact revision that makes the claim inspectable. By the end of CAT 100 02.03, your notes should make it possible to reconstruct your reasoning without asking you to explain it again.

A strong reader of Professional Bio Drafting can answer four questions:

1. What is the real decision hidden inside this topic?
2. Which constraint makes the decision difficult?
3. What evidence would persuade a skeptical reviewer?
4. What would you revise if the audience, learner, classroom, or tool changed?
