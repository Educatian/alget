# LinkedIn and Portfolio Alignment

![LinkedIn and Portfolio Alignment textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat100-supplement/02-04-linkedin-and-portfolio-alignment.png)

## Why This Section Matters

A recruiter who is interested in you rarely stops at one document. They open your resume, then look you up on LinkedIn, then click the portfolio link in your signature. If those three sources tell three different stories (a title that does not match, a graduation date that has drifted, an accomplishment that appears in one place and vanishes in another), the recruiter does not assume a typo. They assume carelessness, or worse, that one version is inflated. This section is about cross-platform consistency: making sure that wherever a reader checks, they find the same true facts. This cross-checking is the documented norm, not a worst case: roughly seven in ten employers report researching candidates on social and professional networking sites before hiring (CareerBuilder), and research on "cybervetting" finds recruiters routinely fold applicants' online profiles into selection decisions (Jacobson & Gruzd, 2020).

The skill matters because your professional presence is now distributed across systems you update at different times. You fix the resume for an application, forget LinkedIn, never touch the portfolio, and the three slowly diverge. The deliverable is a before-and-after alignment of one role across resume, LinkedIn, and portfolio, with an AI-use note, so a reviewer can confirm the three now agree.

## Learning Targets

- Run a cross-platform alignment audit (inventory, detect, reconcile, diff) on one role across resume, LinkedIn, and portfolio, anchoring to the truthful version.
- Identify the four anchors (display name, current title or role, dates, headline accomplishment) and verify they do not contradict across platforms.
- Document a bounded AI/software support move with accepted and rejected suggestions.
- Use feedback, support requests, and revision notes to improve the artifact before sharing it.

## Opening Case

Andre applies for an internship. His resume lists him as "Treasurer, Coding Club," his LinkedIn still says "Member" from before his election, and his portfolio does not mention the club at all. The recruiter, doing a normal background check, opens all three and sees a promotion on one page that two others contradict. Nothing here is dishonest; Andre simply updated the resume and forgot the rest. But the recruiter has no way to know that, and the mismatch reads as a credibility problem at the exact moment Andre needed to look reliable.

The case shows that consistency is itself a form of evidence. A reader trusts a claim more when independent sources confirm it, and trusts it less when they conflict, regardless of which version is actually true.

## Core Concept

Alignment means that the verifiable facts about you, name, title, dates, and headline accomplishment, match across every platform a reader might check. The procedure has three moves: **inventory** (list how a single role currently reads on each platform side by side), **detect** (circle the mismatches), and **reconcile** (pick the one truthful version and make all platforms match it). The anchor is always the true version, never the most flattering one.

Each platform has a different job, which is why they drift but also why alignment is not the same as copy-paste:

| Platform | Primary job | What "aligned" looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Resume | Tailored snapshot for one application | Concise role + metric |
| LinkedIn | Searchable, public, networked profile | Same role + metric, fuller wording |
| Portfolio | Proof you can show, not just claim | The role plus a link to the actual work |

Alignment is not making the three identical; it is making them consistent. The resume can be terse and the portfolio can show the artifact, but the title, the dates, and the core accomplishment must not contradict each other. Polishing one platform's design while leaving a date mismatch in place fixes nothing a recruiter cares about.

## Worked Example: Running a Cross-Platform Alignment Audit

Alignment means a recruiter who opens your resume, LinkedIn, and portfolio finds the same facts in all three. Audit one role across the three platforms.

**Step 1. List the same role as it currently appears on each platform.**

| Platform | How the role reads now |
| --- | --- |
| Resume | Treasurer, Coding Club (Aug 2024 to present) |
| LinkedIn | Member, Coding Club |
| Portfolio | (no mention) |

**Step 2. Spot the mismatches.** The resume says Treasurer; LinkedIn says Member; the portfolio is silent. A recruiter cross-checking these sees three different stories and cannot tell which is true.

**Step 3. Pick the truthful version and make all three match.** The student really is Treasurer, so that is the anchor.

| Platform | Aligned version |
| --- | --- |
| Resume | Treasurer, Coding Club (Aug 2024 to present); manage a \$2,400 annual budget |
| LinkedIn | Treasurer, Coding Club. I manage the club's \$2,400 budget and monthly expense reports. |
| Portfolio | "Roles" section now lists: Treasurer, Coding Club, with a link to the budget tracker spreadsheet I built. |

**Step 4. Add verifiable evidence to the portfolio.** The screenshot-only project page now links to the live budget tracker, so a reviewer can confirm the work.

**Step 5. Log the AI boundary.** When LinkedIn's built-in AI writing assistant (or a chatbot like ChatGPT, Claude, or Copilot) proposed the headline "Award-Winning Developer," the student rejected it (no awards exist) and kept a headline the three platforms can all back up. LinkedIn's AI suggestions optimize for engagement and keywords, not truth, so its headline drafts must pass the same cross-check as any other AI output.

## Decision Rule

Before you call your presence aligned, run the cross-check.

1. **Pick the truth, not the best version.** When two platforms disagree, the deciding factor is what is actually true, then update the others to match it.
2. **Check the four anchors on every platform:** display name, current title or role, dates, and headline accomplishment. These are what a reader cross-references.
3. **Make the portfolio show, not just tell.** A claimed project should link to the live work, repository, or file, so the portfolio adds proof the resume only states.
4. **Diff after editing.** Read the three side by side one last time; an edit to one platform often opens a new mismatch with another.

If a recruiter could open any two of your platforms and find a contradiction, the work is not done, however polished each page looks on its own.

## Common Misreadings

One misreading is treating alignment as "make all three identical." That overcorrects: a portfolio that just pastes the resume wastes the chance to link the actual work, and a LinkedIn that copies the terse resume loses the room it has for fuller context. Aligned means non-contradictory, not cloned.

A second misreading is editing only the document you are currently looking at. You fix the resume for an application and consider the job done, leaving LinkedIn stale. Alignment is a cross-platform check by definition; updating one source in isolation is what created the drift in the first place.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Take one accomplishment and align it across the three. On the resume it reads "Built a budget tracker for the Coding Club." On LinkedIn there is no mention of it, and the portfolio has a screenshot labeled "spreadsheet project" with no context. Inventory makes the gaps obvious: the same real work appears in three different states of completeness. Reconcile by anchoring to the truth (Andre did build it as Treasurer) and bringing each platform up to a consistent version.

Resume stays terse: "Treasurer, Coding Club: built a budget tracker managing the club's \$2,400 annual budget." LinkedIn gets the fuller, searchable version: "As Treasurer of the Coding Club, I built a spreadsheet budget tracker for our \$2,400 annual budget and monthly expense reporting." The portfolio keeps the screenshot but adds the title, the role, and a link to the working file so a reviewer can open it. Now all three name the same role, the same dollar figure, and the same artifact; the portfolio additionally proves it. The dollar amounts are written with a backslash so the page renders \$2,400 rather than treating it as math.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

A strong reader puts on the recruiter's hat and actively tries to break their own consistency: they open the platforms in three tabs and look specifically for the four anchors disagreeing. Finding your own mismatch before a recruiter does is the whole point.

A strong reader also understands why platforms differ in voice even when they agree on facts. LinkedIn's first-person, searchable space rewards keywords a recruiter might search; the portfolio rewards a clickable proof; the resume rewards brevity. Knowing the platform's job lets you keep the facts aligned while adapting the wording, rather than flattening everything into one bland version.

## Transfer Case

Cross-platform consistency is a habit you will use far beyond job materials. A grad-school application's CV, statement, and recommender briefing must agree on your dates and projects; a small business's website, Google listing, and social profiles must agree on hours and address; a research project's abstract, slides, and paper must agree on the headline result. The anchors change with the domain (hours and address instead of title and graduation date), but the discipline of inventory, detect, reconcile around the true version is the same one you practiced here.
