# Peer Review for Professional Materials

![Peer Review for Professional Materials textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat100-supplement/02-07-peer-review-for-professional-materials.png)

## Why This Section Matters

"Looks good!" is the most useless thing you can say to a classmate who asked you to review their resume. It names no location, no problem, and no fix, so the writer leaves with exactly what they came with. This section teaches how to give peer feedback that the writer can actually use, and, just as important, how to do it without violating the writer's privacy. Reviewing professional materials is different from reviewing an essay, because resumes carry phone numbers, addresses, and employment history, and "let me run this through an AI to check it" can expose a peer's personal data to a system neither of you controls.

The skill matters on both ends. Good feedback you give makes you a better self-reviewer of your own work, because you internalize what specific, actionable comments look like. And handling a peer's document responsibly, keeping their data off public tools and leaving the final call to them, is a digital-citizenship habit you will need any time you handle someone else's information at work.

## Opening Case

Nina swaps resumes with a classmate for peer review. To save time, she pastes his entire resume, name, phone number, home address, and all, into a free AI tool and asks it to "list everything wrong." The tool returns useful phrasing notes, but Nina has just handed a stranger's personal contact information to a system whose data practices she never read, for a person who never consented to it. The phrasing feedback was fine; the privacy breach was not, and it was entirely avoidable, she could have stripped the contact block first or reviewed it herself.

The case shows the two responsibilities that ride together in peer review: making the feedback useful and keeping the other person's data safe. Strong feedback delivered through a careless data move is still a failure, because the cost lands on someone who trusted you with their document.

## Core Concept

Useful peer feedback follows a simple shape: **location, problem, question.** Name where the issue is ("Experience, bullet two"), name what is wrong with it ("does not say what you did or the scale"), and pose it as a question that hands the decision back to the writer ("what was your actual task, and how many events?"). The question framing matters because the document belongs to the writer; your job is to surface issues, not to seize the pen.

Two boundaries govern professional-materials review specifically:

| Boundary | What it requires | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ownership | Comment and question; do not rewrite for them | The writer must be able to defend every claim |
| Privacy | Strip personal data before any tool use; do not share it | A peer's contact info and history are theirs, not yours to expose |

The hardest feedback to give well is on a risky claim, a line like "Led the team" that the writer may not be able to defend. The move is not to correct it silently but to raise it as a question ("were you the formal lead, or one contributor?"), because only the writer knows the truth and only the writer will be in the interview. Polishing a peer's wording while leaving an indefensible claim, or exposing their data to fix a comma, both miss the point.

## Worked Example: Turning a Vague Reaction Into Usable Peer Feedback

You are reviewing a classmate's resume bullet:

```
Helped with events for the student government.
```

**Step 1. Write the unhelpful comment first, so you can see what to avoid.**

```
Looks good!
```

This gives the writer nothing. It names no location and no fix.

**Step 2. Rewrite it as specific, actionable feedback using "location + problem + question."**

```
Experience, bullet 2: "Helped with events" does not say what you did or the
scale. What was your actual task, and how many events or attendees? A recruiter
will want a verb and a number here.
```

**Step 3. Raise the risky claim as a question, not a correction.** Suppose another line reads "Led the team." You do not rewrite it; you ask:

```
Were you the formal lead, or one contributor? If a recruiter asks in an
interview, which answer can you defend?
```

**Step 4. Respect the data boundary.** You do NOT paste the peer's full resume, with their phone number and address, into a public AI tool. If you use a tool to check phrasing, you remove the personal details first.

**Step 5. Hand back a decision-ready set.** The writer ends with three located comments they can accept or decline, ownership intact, and their private data never exposed.

## Decision Rule

Before you hand back peer feedback, check both the usefulness and the privacy of how you produced it.

1. **Make each comment actionable:** does it name a location, a problem, and a question or specific fix? "Looks good" and "fix this" both fail.
2. **Raise risky claims as questions, not corrections:** when a line may not be defensible, ask whether the writer can back it, rather than rewriting it for them.
3. **Protect their data:** if you use any tool to check phrasing, remove the name, phone, address, and employer details first, or review it yourself.
4. **Leave the decision with the owner:** end with comments they can accept or decline, not a rewritten document they must adopt.

Feedback that is specific but exposes a peer's data fails the rule, and so does feedback that protects their data but says nothing useful. Both must hold.

## Common Misreadings

One misreading is that helpful means nice. Vague praise feels kind but leaves the writer worse off than honest, located comments. The kindest review is the one the writer can actually act on.

A second misreading is that the privacy boundary is paranoia. It is not abstract: a resume carries exactly the data (full name, phone, address, work history) that fuels identity theft and unwanted contact, and pasting it into a free tool can store it on servers you cannot audit. Treating a peer's contact block as sensitive is the normal professional standard, not an overreaction.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Review one weak bullet end to end. Your classmate wrote "Helped with events for the student government." First, write the unhelpful version on purpose so you can see what to avoid: "Looks good!" names nothing. Now apply location, problem, question: "Experience, bullet two: 'helped with events' does not say what you did or the scale. What was your actual task, and how many events or attendees? A recruiter will want a verb and a number here." That comment points to a spot, diagnoses it, and hands the writer a question only they can answer.

Next, suppose another line reads "Led the team." Do not rewrite it; raise it: "Were you the formal lead, or one contributor? If a recruiter asks, which answer can you defend?" Then handle the tool boundary: if you want to check phrasing with an AI assistant, you remove the classmate's name, phone, and address first, because their personal data is not yours to upload. You finish by handing back three located comments the writer can accept or decline, ownership intact and private data never exposed. The walkthrough shows the two responsibilities working together: the feedback is usable and the data stayed safe.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

A strong reader notices the difference between a comment that improves the document and a comment that just performs reviewing. "Strong action verbs!" performs; "bullet two needs a number" improves. Reading this section well sharpens your ear for the difference in your own comments.

A strong reader also treats the privacy boundary as a default, not a special case. Before any document touches a tool, they ask "is there personal data here, and whose is it?" Building that reflex, rather than remembering it only when prompted, is what separates digital citizenship from a checklist.

## Transfer Case

The location-problem-question shape and the data boundary transfer to every review you will ever do of someone else's work. Editing a friend's grad-school essay, commenting on a coworker's draft proposal, or reviewing a teammate's slide deck all reward specific, located, question-framed feedback that leaves ownership with the author. And any document holding someone else's personal information, a colleague's contract, a customer's form, demands the same data restraint you practiced on a peer's resume. The content changes; the dual obligation to be useful and to be careful with others' data does not.
