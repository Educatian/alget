# Resume Claims and Evidence

![Resume Claims and Evidence textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat100-supplement/02-01-resume-claims-and-evidence.png)

## Why This Section Matters

Every line on a resume is a claim about you, and a recruiter reads it as a promise you will have to keep in an interview. The line "Managed social media" claims you ran something; "Improved sales" claims you caused a result. Most first-draft resumes are full of claims with no evidence attached, so the reader cannot tell a real accomplishment from a hopeful phrase. This section teaches the move that separates a strong resume from a generic one: attaching a verifiable fact (a number, a scope, a named tool or outcome) to every claim you make.

The skill is concrete because recruiters scan fast. Eye-tracking research on recruiters (Ladders, 2018) found an average initial resume screen of about 7.4 seconds, up from roughly 6 seconds in 2012, though the exact number is debated; peer-reviewed eye-tracking work (Pina et al., 2023) confirms the first pass is a fast visual scan either way. A claim that cannot be checked at a glance is wasted space. Your deliverable, a before-and-after revision of one section plus an AI-use note, is the place where you prove you can turn a duty list into evidence and keep yourself honest while doing it.

## Learning Targets

- Convert a duty-list resume bullet into an accomplishment using the action + scope + result pattern, backed by a verifiable fact.
- Distinguish artifact polish from evidence-based revision.
- Document a bounded AI/software support move with accepted and rejected suggestions.
- Diagnose which failure mode a weak bullet has (vague, inflated, or borrowed) and apply the matching fix without inventing facts.

## Opening Case

Maria has a line on her resume that reads "Responsible for inventory." It is true, but it is also what every stockroom worker could write. When a recruiter asked about it in a mock interview, Maria suddenly remembered the details: she had cut a recurring count error by reconciling the spreadsheet against the delivery logs every Friday, and the store stopped running out of two best-selling items. None of that was on the page. The accomplishment existed; the evidence for it was missing.

That gap is the heart of this section. The facts that make you hireable usually already exist in your memory, your pay stubs, your old schedules, or your project files. The work is not inventing impressive-sounding phrases; it is excavating the real evidence and writing it down so the reader does not have to interview you to find it.

## Core Concept

A resume bullet has two parts that students routinely collapse into one: the **claim** (what you say you did) and the **evidence** (the checkable fact that backs it). A claim without evidence is a duty; a claim with evidence is an accomplishment. The reliable pattern for an accomplishment bullet is **action verb plus scope plus result**: a verb that shows judgment (trained, reconciled, redesigned, not "helped" or "responsible for"), a scope that bounds it (how many, how often, how long), and a result the reader can picture.

Three quality tests sharpen each bullet:

| Test | Question it asks | Failure looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Verifiability | Could you defend this fact if asked? | "Boosted sales 30%" with no source |
| Specificity | Does a number or named scope appear? | "Helped many customers" |
| Ownership | Did you do this, or just stand near it? | "Was part of a team that..." |

Notice what these tests rule out. They reject inflation (claims you cannot defend), and they reject vagueness (claims too soft to check). Polish, fixing fonts, tightening spacing, adding strong adjectives, does not pass any of these tests, which is why a beautiful resume can still be a weak one. The evidence, not the styling, is what survives a recruiter's question.

## Worked Example: Turning One Bullet Into Evidence

Work through one real resume bullet, step by step, so you can see what "evidence" means in practice.

**Step 1. Start with the weak bullet the student first wrote.**

```
Cashier at Smoothie King. Helped customers and kept the store clean.
```

This is a duty list. Any cashier could write it. There is no action verb that shows judgment, no number, and no scope.

**Step 2. Pull the facts the student can actually defend.** From their pay stubs and a quick memory pass:

| Fact | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Shifts per week | 4 |
| Length of employment | 14 months |
| Trained new hires | 5 |
| Drive-thru order accuracy goal | 95% |

**Step 3. Rewrite the bullet so each phrase maps to a fact.**

```
Trained 5 new cashiers over 14 months and held drive-thru order accuracy
at or above the store's 95% goal across 4 shifts per week.
```

**Step 4. Audit the rewrite.** Every clause now points to a row in the table: "trained 5" maps to trained-new-hires, "14 months" to length, "95% goal" to accuracy, "4 shifts per week" to shifts. A recruiter can ask about any phrase and the student has a truthful answer. That traceability, not the polish, is what makes the bullet count as evidence.

**Step 5. Record the AI boundary.** If a resume assistant (ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot, or a LinkedIn resume tool) had suggested "Boosted store revenue 30%," the student would reject it, because no row in the table supports that number, and log the rejection in the AI-use note. Specific tools and model versions change from year to year; the reject-and-log discipline does not.

## Decision Rule

For each bullet, run it through the claim-evidence test in order: name the claim, then ask whether a checkable fact supports it. Keep the bullet only if you can answer yes truthfully.

1. **Claim check:** What exactly am I saying I did? Underline the verb; if it is "helped," "responsible for," or "involved in," it probably hides a stronger action.
2. **Evidence check:** Which number, scope, tool, or outcome proves it? Point to the pay stub, schedule, project file, or supervisor who could confirm it.
3. **Defense check:** If a recruiter asked "tell me more about this," do I have a truthful, specific answer ready?

If a bullet fails the evidence check, your choices are to dig up the missing fact or to delete the bullet, never to invent the fact. A shorter resume of defensible claims beats a longer one of empty ones.

## Common Misreadings

The most common error is treating a stronger adjective as evidence. Changing "helped customers" to "passionately served customers" adds heat, not light; the reader still cannot check anything. Evidence is a fact (a count, a percentage against a stated goal, a named system), not an intensifier.

A second misreading runs the other way: assuming honesty means modesty. Refusing to state a real achievement because it "sounds like bragging" buries evidence you are entitled to claim. The standard is not quiet, it is truthful and specific.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the pattern to a single bullet from a campus job. Suppose the draft reads "Worked the front desk at the rec center." Start with the claim check: the verb "worked" describes presence, not judgment, so the bullet currently claims almost nothing. Move to the evidence: ask what you actually did and what is countable. You checked in members, you handled the equipment-rental log, and on busy nights the desk processed around 120 check-ins in a three-hour shift. Now rebuild with action plus scope plus result: "Processed about 120 member check-ins per evening shift and maintained the equipment-rental log with zero unreturned items over the semester."

Run the tests. Verifiability: the check-in count comes from the front-desk software, and the zero-loss record is on the rental sheet. Specificity: a number and a time window appear. Ownership: you, not "the team," kept the log. The bullet went from a presence statement to an accomplishment without a single invented fact, because the facts were already in the rec center's own records. That is the whole move, repeated section by section.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

A strong reader of this section does not just nod at "use evidence." They start hunting their own paper trail before writing anything: old schedules for hours and shift counts, pay stubs for tenure, project folders for deliverables, performance notes for results. Evidence-first revision feels less like writing and more like sourcing.

A strong reader can also tell the difference between the three failure modes and name which one a given bullet has: is it vague (no number), inflated (a number you cannot defend), or borrowed (a team result claimed as solely yours)? Diagnosing the specific failure is what tells you the specific fix.

## Transfer Case

The same claim-evidence discipline moves straight into other documents in this module and beyond it. A LinkedIn "About" section, a scholarship application, a grad-school statement, and a performance self-review all reward the action-scope-result pattern, and all punish unverifiable claims. The constraint shifts with the audience: a scholarship committee may care about service hours and impact, while a tech recruiter cares about tools and measurable outcomes, so the same true facts get foregrounded differently. If you can re-aim which evidence leads without inventing new facts, you have understood the section rather than memorized one bullet.
