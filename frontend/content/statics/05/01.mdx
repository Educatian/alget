# Centroids, Distributed Loads, and Moments of Inertia

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this section, you will be able to:

1. **Locate** the centroid of a composite area using the area-weighted average, with negative areas for cutouts
2. **Replace** a distributed load along a beam with an equivalent point load at the load's centroid
3. **Compute** area moments of inertia for composite shapes using the parallel-axis theorem and composite-bodies method
4. **Distinguish** area moment of inertia ($\text{m}^4$, statics bending) from mass moment of inertia ($\text{kg·m}^2$, dynamics rotation)

---

## Opening: Why an I-Beam Beats a Square

Take two steel beams that use the *exact same amount of material* per meter of length. One is a solid square; the other rolls that material into an I-shape, pushing most of it into a top and bottom flange far from the center. Load them identically and the I-beam deflects a fraction as much and carries far more before it yields. Nothing changed but *where* the area sits.

That single fact, that bending resistance depends on the distribution of area and not just its amount, is why centroids and moments of inertia exist as a topic. The centroid tells you where the area's "first moment" balances; the moment of inertia (the "second moment") tells you how effectively that area resists bending about an axis. Engineers spend the rest of their careers exploiting the gap between the I-beam and the square.

> **The Big Picture:** Every cross section you will ever size in Mechanics of Materials enters the stress formula as a moment of inertia. This section is where you learn to compute it.

---

## Why Distributed Quantities Need Their Own Toolkit

Up to this chapter, statics has lived in the world of point forces and rigid-body equilibrium. Real engineering geometry is not concentrated at points. The weight of a beam is distributed along its length. The pressure on a dam is distributed across its face. The mass of a rotating shaft is distributed across its cross section. To analyze these systems, you replace the distribution with a **single equivalent point** that captures its first moment (centroid) or its second moment (moment of inertia).

This module covers the four computations every undergraduate statics student should be able to do: locating a centroid, locating a center of mass, computing an area moment of inertia, and applying the parallel-axis theorem. Beer and Johnston devote two chapters (5 and 9) to this material; Hibbeler devotes Ch. 9. Their structure is the structure of this module.

---

## Centroid: The First-Moment Average of Geometry

The **centroid** of an area is the geometric average of every point in the region, weighted by area. For an area A in the xy-plane,

$$\bar{x} = \frac{1}{A}\int_A x\,dA, \quad \bar{y} = \frac{1}{A}\int_A y\,dA$$

For composite shapes (rectangle + triangle + circle, with cutouts), use the **composite-bodies method**: list the constituent shapes, their individual centroids, and their individual areas (negative for cutouts), then weight-average.

$$\bar{x} = \frac{\sum_i A_i \bar{x}_i}{\sum_i A_i}, \quad \bar{y} = \frac{\sum_i A_i \bar{y}_i}{\sum_i A_i}$$

The centroid is a property of geometry only. It does not depend on the material's density.

---

## Center of Mass and Center of Gravity

The **center of mass** weights position by mass, not area:

$$\bar{x}_{\rm cm} = \frac{\int x\,dm}{\int dm} = \frac{\int x\,\rho\,dV}{\int \rho\,dV}$$

For a body with uniform density, the center of mass coincides with the centroid of the volume. For a body with variable density (composite materials, gradient layers), the two differ, and you must use the mass-weighted form.

The **center of gravity** further weights by the local gravitational field. On Earth's surface, gravity is essentially uniform, so center of gravity coincides with center of mass. For systems extending across significant gravitational gradients (orbital structures), the two diverge.

For static equilibrium of beams, plates, and machine parts, the center of mass is the point at which the body's weight can be replaced by a single concentrated force without changing equilibrium.

## Distributed Loads: Replace by Equivalent Point Load

A distributed load w(x) along a beam can be replaced by an equivalent concentrated force whose magnitude is the area under the load curve, applied at the centroid of that area:

$$F_{\rm eq} = \int_0^L w(x)\,dx, \quad x_{\rm eq} = \frac{\int_0^L x\,w(x)\,dx}{F_{\rm eq}}$$

For a uniform distributed load $w_0$ over length L, $F_{\rm eq} = w_0 L$ at the midpoint. For a triangular load that ramps from 0 to $w_0$ over length L, $F_{\rm eq} = w_0 L / 2$ at $x = 2L/3$ from the zero end.

This replacement is the bridge between "the beam carries 5 kN/m" and the equilibrium equations you can actually solve.

## Area Moment of Inertia: The Second Moment

The **area moment of inertia** about an axis is the integral of squared distance to that axis, weighted by area:

$$I_x = \int_A y^2\,dA, \quad I_y = \int_A x^2\,dA$$

This is sometimes called the *second moment of area* to avoid confusion with the *mass moment of inertia* (rotational inertia in dynamics). For a rectangle of width b and height h about its base,

$$I_{\rm base} = \frac{bh^3}{3}$$

About its centroidal axis,

$$I_{\rm c} = \frac{bh^3}{12}$$

The area moment of inertia is the geometric quantity that determines how a beam resists bending. Higher $I$ means stiffer beam, lower stress for the same load. This is why I-beams have most of their material far from the neutral axis: they maximize $I$ for a given amount of material.

## Parallel-Axis Theorem

A central tool: the moment of inertia about any axis equals the moment of inertia about the **parallel centroidal axis** plus a correction:

$$I = I_{\rm c} + A d^2$$

where d is the distance between the two parallel axes. This is the *parallel-axis theorem* (sometimes called the Steiner theorem for masses).

### A Short Derivation

The theorem is not an axiom; it falls out of the definition in two lines. Let the centroidal axis be at $y = 0$ and the new parallel axis be a distance $d$ away, so a strip at centroidal coordinate $y$ sits at distance $(y + d)$ from the new axis. By definition,

$$I = \int_A (y + d)^2\,dA = \int_A y^2\,dA + 2d\int_A y\,dA + d^2\int_A dA$$

Examine the three terms. The first is $\int_A y^2\,dA = I_{\rm c}$, the centroidal moment of inertia. The middle term contains $\int_A y\,dA$, the *first moment of area about the centroidal axis*, which is zero by the very definition of the centroid (that is what makes it the centroid). The last term is $d^2 \int_A dA = A d^2$. The cross term vanishing is the entire reason the formula is so clean:

$$I = I_{\rm c} + 0 + A d^2 = I_{\rm c} + A d^2$$

Because $A d^2 \ge 0$, the centroidal value $I_{\rm c}$ is necessarily the smallest moment of inertia among all parallel axes. The same algebra, with $dA$ replaced by $dm$, produces the mass version $I = I_{\rm cm} + M d^2$.

Two consequences. First, the centroidal axis is always the axis of *minimum* I (the $Ad^2$ term is non-negative). Second, the theorem makes composite-shape problems tractable: compute each piece's centroidal I, then transfer to a common axis with $Ad^2$, and sum.

For composite shapes (I-beam built from three rectangles, or a circle with a hole) the workflow is:

1. Pick a common reference axis.
2. For each piece, compute centroidal $I_i$ from the standard tables.
3. Compute the offset $d_i$ from each piece's centroid to the reference axis.
4. Sum $I_{\rm total} = \sum_i (I_i + A_i d_i^2)$, with cutouts entering as negative $A_i$.

This is the workflow. It does not change with shape; only the table values change.

## Mass Moment of Inertia: The Dynamics Bridge

In dynamics, the **mass moment of inertia** plays the role mass plays in linear dynamics:

$$I_{\rm mass} = \int r^2\,dm$$

It governs how torque produces angular acceleration ($\tau = I\alpha$). Physically, it is the body's resistance to changes in rotational motion. Like its area cousin, it has a parallel-axis theorem:

$$I = I_{\rm cm} + Md^2$$

For a slender rod of length L and mass M about its centroid, $I_{\rm cm} = ML^2/12$. About one end, $I_{\rm end} = ML^2/3$. The factor-of-four difference between the two reflects the parallel-axis correction.

For undergraduate statics, the area moment of inertia is the working concept. The mass moment of inertia returns in dynamics chapters on rigid-body rotation.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Centroid of a Rectangle with a Circular Hole

**Problem:** A rectangular plate is 120 mm wide and 80 mm tall, with the origin at its lower-left corner. A circular hole of radius 20 mm is drilled with its center at $(90, 40)$ mm. Find the centroid $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$ of the remaining material.

**Step 1: List the pieces** (the hole enters with *negative* area).

| Piece | Area $A_i$ (mm²) | $\bar{x}_i$ (mm) | $\bar{y}_i$ (mm) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Full rectangle | $120 \times 80 = 9600$ | 60 | 40 |
| Hole (negative) | $-\pi (20)^2 = -1256.6$ | 90 | 40 |

**Step 2: Total area.**

$$A = 9600 - 1256.6 = 8343.4 \text{ mm}^2$$

**Step 3: Area-weighted average.**

$$\bar{x} = \frac{(9600)(60) + (-1256.6)(90)}{8343.4} = \frac{576000 - 113094}{8343.4} = \boxed{55.48 \text{ mm}}$$

$$\bar{y} = \frac{(9600)(40) + (-1256.6)(40)}{8343.4} = \boxed{40.0 \text{ mm}}$$

The hole pulls the centroid from $x = 60$ mm toward the solid side, landing at $55.48$ mm. The $y$-coordinate stays at 40 mm because the hole sits on the horizontal centerline. Removing material always shifts the centroid *away* from the removed region.

---

### Example 2: Rectangle Moment of Inertia, Centroidal and About the Base

**Problem:** A rectangular cross section has width $b = 60$ mm and height $h = 120$ mm. Find the area moment of inertia about its horizontal centroidal axis and about its base.

**Step 1: Centroidal axis** (standard result $I_c = bh^3/12$).

$$I_c = \frac{(60)(120)^3}{12} = \frac{60 \times 1{,}728{,}000}{12} = \boxed{8.64 \times 10^6 \text{ mm}^4}$$

**Step 2: About the base, by the parallel-axis theorem** $I = I_c + A d^2$, with $d = h/2 = 60$ mm and $A = bh = 7200$ mm²:

$$I_{\text{base}} = 8.64 \times 10^6 + (7200)(60)^2 = 8.64 \times 10^6 + 25.92 \times 10^6 = \boxed{34.56 \times 10^6 \text{ mm}^4}$$

As a check, the closed form is $I_{\text{base}} = bh^3/3 = (60)(120)^3/3 = 34.56 \times 10^6$ mm⁴. They agree, and $I_{\text{base}} = 4 I_c$ exactly, the signature of the $Ad^2$ correction for a rectangle.

---

### Example 3: I-Beam Moment of Inertia vs. a Solid Square

**Problem:** An I-beam has a top flange 100 mm × 10 mm, a bottom flange 100 mm × 10 mm, and a web 10 mm × 80 mm, total height 100 mm. Compute the centroidal moment of inertia $I_x$ about the horizontal axis, and compare it to a solid square of equal area.

**Step 1: Centroid.** By symmetry, the centroidal axis is at mid-height, $\bar{y} = 50$ mm.

**Step 2: Composite-bodies + parallel axis.** Measure each piece's centroid from the bottom: top flange at $y = 95$, web at $y = 50$, bottom flange at $y = 5$.

| Piece | $I_{c,i} = bh^3/12$ (mm⁴) | $A_i$ (mm²) | $d_i$ (mm) | $A_i d_i^2$ (mm⁴) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Top flange | $100(10)^3/12 = 8{,}333$ | 1000 | 45 | $2{,}025{,}000$ |
| Web | $10(80)^3/12 = 426{,}667$ | 800 | 0 | 0 |
| Bottom flange | $8{,}333$ | 1000 | 45 | $2{,}025{,}000$ |

**Step 3: Sum.**

$$I_x = (8333 + 426667 + 8333) + (2025000 + 0 + 2025000) = \boxed{4.49 \times 10^6 \text{ mm}^4}$$

**Step 4: Compare to a solid square of equal area.** Total area $= 1000 + 800 + 1000 = 2800$ mm², so the square side is $s = \sqrt{2800} = 52.9$ mm, giving $I_{\text{sq}} = s^4/12 = 0.65 \times 10^6$ mm⁴.

The I-beam delivers about **seven times** the bending stiffness of a square using the same material, purely by moving area to the flanges where $d^2$ is large. This is Example 0's "why I-beams win" made quantitative.

### Example 4: Equivalent Point Load for a Triangular Distribution

**Problem:** A beam carries a triangular distributed load that ramps from 0 at the left to $w_0 = 6$ kN/m at the right over a length $L = 4$ m. Find the magnitude and location of the equivalent point load.

**Step 1: Magnitude = area under the load curve** (area of the triangle).

$$F_{\text{eq}} = \frac{1}{2} w_0 L = \frac{1}{2}(6)(4) = \boxed{12 \text{ kN}}$$

**Step 2: Location = centroid of the triangle.** For a triangle with its apex (peak) at the right end, the centroid sits $2L/3$ from the zero end:

$$x_{\text{eq}} = \frac{2L}{3} = \frac{2(4)}{3} = \boxed{2.67 \text{ m from the left (zero) end}}$$

This single 12 kN force, placed 2.67 m from the left, is statically equivalent to the entire distribution for the purpose of finding reactions, the bridge between "the beam carries a 6 kN/m triangular load" and the equilibrium equations you can actually solve.

## Common Errors and Their Diagnoses

- **Confusing area moment with mass moment.** Both have the symbol $I$ in different contexts. Read the units: area moment is m⁴, mass moment is kg·m².
- **Forgetting the parallel-axis correction.** When summing composite pieces, the centroidal $I_i$ is about each piece's own centroid; you must add $A_i d_i^2$ to bring it to the common axis.
- **Reversing the sign convention on cutouts.** A circle removed from a rectangle enters with negative area (and zero $I$ from the cutout's centroidal contribution, plus negative $A d^2$). Forgetting this overestimates I.
- **Using the centroid as the center of mass for non-uniform density.** The two coincide only for uniform density. For composite materials, weight by mass.

## Engineering Applications

The three quantities in this section (centroid, equivalent load, moment of inertia) are not academic exercises; each one feeds directly into a downstream design calculation an engineer cannot skip.

| Application | Quantity Used | Why It Matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Beam selection (steel W-shapes) | Area moment of inertia $I$ | Deflection $\delta \propto 1/I$ and bending stress $\sigma = Mc/I$; sizing a floor joist or crane girder starts here |
| Welded plate girders and aircraft spars | Composite $I$ via parallel axis | Built-up sections push area to the flanges to maximize $I$ per kilogram of material |
| Retaining walls and dam faces | Equivalent point load of a triangular pressure | Hydrostatic pressure grows linearly with depth; the resultant acts at $2L/3$ down from the surface, setting the overturning moment |
| Snow and wind loads on roofs | Equivalent point load of a distributed load | Code loads are given as kN/m²; reactions need the resultant and its line of action |
| Rotating shafts and flywheels | Mass moment of inertia $I_{\rm mass}$ | $\tau = I\alpha$ sizes the motor torque and sets the stored energy $\tfrac{1}{2}I\omega^2$ |
| Machine-part balancing | Center of mass | An unbalanced center of mass off the spin axis creates a rotating force that fatigues bearings |

> **Engineering in Action:** When a structural engineer opens a steel manual and reads off $I_x = 4.49 \times 10^6\ \text{mm}^4$ for a section, that number was produced by exactly the composite-bodies and parallel-axis procedure in Example 3. The table just saves you from recomputing it each time.

## Summary

### Key Equations

| Concept | Equation |
| --- | --- |
| Composite centroid | $\bar{x} = \dfrac{\sum A_i \bar{x}_i}{\sum A_i}$ |
| Equivalent point load | $F_{\text{eq}} = \displaystyle\int w(x)\,dx$ at the load's centroid |
| Rectangle (centroidal) | $I_c = bh^3/12$ |
| Rectangle (base) | $I_{\text{base}} = bh^3/3$ |
| Parallel-axis theorem | $I = I_c + A d^2$ |

### Key Takeaways

- The centroid is the area-weighted average of geometry; cutouts enter with negative area.
- A distributed load is equivalent to a point load equal to the area under the curve, applied at that area's centroid.
- The area moment of inertia ($\text{m}^4$) governs bending stiffness; the mass moment of inertia ($\text{kg·m}^2$) governs rotational dynamics.
- The parallel-axis theorem $I = I_c + Ad^2$ makes the centroidal axis the minimum-$I$ axis.
- Pushing area far from the neutral axis (the I-beam strategy) maximizes $I$ for a fixed amount of material.
