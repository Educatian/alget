## Opening: Why Equilibrium Matters

On November 7, 1940, the Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapsed spectacularly - not because engineers forgot equilibrium, but because equilibrium alone was not enough to capture the full dynamic behavior. That historical failure still teaches the same lesson: **before engineers can analyze motion, vibration, or instability, they must first understand how forces balance.**

Everyday structures remain safe only because the forces acting on them are in equilibrium. A suspended light fixture, a cable-supported sensor, and a bracket bolted to a wall all survive because the net force on the idealized system is zero.

> **The Big Picture:** Mastering equilibrium analysis gives you the first reliable test for whether a load path is plausible, whether a support reaction makes sense, and whether a structural model is physically self-consistent.

> **Bio-Inspired Link: Anchoring in Chaos**  
> Mangrove trees offer a useful mental model. Instead of relying on one deep root to resist waves and storm surge, they spread load through a network of prop roots. The lesson for engineering is simple: stability often comes from distributing force through multiple paths rather than concentrating it in one brittle element.

## What Is Equilibrium?

### The Physical Concept

Imagine a book resting on a table. It does not accelerate because the downward weight and upward table reaction balance exactly.

This is **equilibrium**: a state where the net effect of all forces is zero.

| State | Condition | Physical Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Static Equilibrium | $\sum \vec{F} = 0$ and $v = 0$ | Object at rest, stays at rest |
| Dynamic Equilibrium | $\sum \vec{F} = 0$ and $v = \text{const}$ | Object moves at constant velocity |

> **Key Insight:** Equilibrium does not mean "no forces." It means the forces balance.

### The Particle Model

In this chapter, we model objects as **particles** - idealized bodies where all forces act at a single point.

That simplification is useful when:

- the object's size is small relative to the geometry of interest
- rotational effects can be ignored
- the goal is to study translational balance only

Later chapters will extend this idea to rigid bodies, where moment balance matters too.

## The Equilibrium Condition

### Vector Form

For a particle in equilibrium,

$$
\boxed{\sum \vec{F} = 0}
$$

This is Newton's first law written in statics language.

### Component Form

In 2D problems, engineers usually split the vector equation into components:

$$
\sum F_x = 0
$$

$$
\sum F_y = 0
$$

These are algebraic sums. Forces acting opposite the chosen positive direction must carry a negative sign.

## Forces in Equilibrium Problems

Before solving, identify **all** external forces on the particle.

| Force Type | Symbol | Direction | When It Appears |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Weight | $W$ or $mg$ | Always downward | Any object with mass |
| Tension | $T$ | Along the cable, away from the object | Ropes, strings, cables |
| Normal Force | $N$ | Perpendicular to the surface | Contact with a surface |
| Friction | $f$ | Parallel to the surface | Rough contact surfaces |
| Applied Force | $P$ or $F$ | Given by the problem | External loading |

> **Golden Rule for Tension:** Cables can only pull, never push. Tension always acts away from the object along the cable direction.

## Free Body Diagrams: The Master Skill

The **Free Body Diagram (FBD)** is the central skill in introductory statics. A correct FBD does not guarantee a correct answer, but an incorrect FBD almost guarantees a wrong one.

### Six-Step FBD Procedure

1. **Isolate** the body of interest
2. **Choose** a clear coordinate system
3. **Draw** weight vertically downward
4. **Add** cable, contact, or support forces in the correct directions
5. **Label** unknowns symbolically before solving
6. **Check** whether every force is external to the isolated body

### Why This Step Matters

A free body diagram is not just a sketch. It is the modeling step that translates a physical situation into solvable equations.

Good analysts pause here and ask:

- Did I isolate the right object?
- Did I accidentally include an internal force?
- Does each force have a physical source?
- Does each assumed direction make sense?

## Worked Examples

### Example 1.1: Vertical Hanging Weight

**Problem:** A 25 kg lamp hangs from a vertical cable. Find the cable tension.

**Step 1: Draw the FBD**

The lamp has two forces:

- upward cable tension $T$
- downward weight $W = mg$

**Step 2: Apply Equilibrium**

$$
\sum F_y = 0
$$

$$
T - W = 0
$$

$$
T = W = mg
$$

**Step 3: Compute the Value**

$$
T = (25 \text{ kg})(9.81 \text{ m/s}^2) = \boxed{245.25 \text{ N}}
$$

This is the simplest equilibrium pattern: one support force balancing one known load.

---

### Example 1.2: Single Inclined Cable

**Problem:** A 50 kg crate is suspended by a cable at $45^\circ$ above the horizontal. Find the cable tension.

**Step 1: Resolve the Force**

The cable tension has components:

| Component | Expression |
| --- | --- |
| $T_x$ | $T \cos 45^\circ$ |
| $T_y$ | $T \sin 45^\circ$ |

Only the vertical component balances the weight.

**Step 2: Apply Vertical Equilibrium**

$$
\sum F_y = 0
$$

$$
T \sin 45^\circ - mg = 0
$$

$$
T = \frac{mg}{\sin 45^\circ}
$$

**Step 3: Calculate**

$$
T = \frac{(50)(9.81)}{0.707} = \boxed{693.67 \text{ N}}
$$

Notice that $T > W$. That is not a mistake. Only part of the cable force acts vertically, so the full tension must be larger than the supported weight.

> **Think About It:** As the cable angle gets closer to horizontal, the required tension increases dramatically. This is one reason shallow cable angles can create very large internal forces.

Use the explorer below to feel that effect directly. Drive the cable angle down toward the horizontal and watch the required tension climb far above the supported weight, while the horizontal pull on the anchor grows even faster.

### Example 1.3: Two-Cable System

**Problem:** A 100 kg crate is suspended by two cables. Cable A makes $30^\circ$ with the horizontal and Cable B makes $60^\circ$ with the horizontal on the opposite side. Find $T_A$ and $T_B$.

**Step 1: Write Horizontal Equilibrium**

$$
T_A \cos 30^\circ - T_B \cos 60^\circ = 0
$$

$$
T_A (0.866) = T_B (0.5)
$$

$$
T_A = 0.577 T_B
$$

**Step 2: Write Vertical Equilibrium**

$$
T_A \sin 30^\circ + T_B \sin 60^\circ - W = 0
$$

$$
T_A (0.5) + T_B (0.866) = 981
$$

**Step 3: Substitute and Solve**

$$
(0.577 T_B)(0.5) + 0.866 T_B = 981
$$

$$
1.155 T_B = 981
$$

$$
T_B = \boxed{849.4 \text{ N}}
$$

Then,

$$
T_A = 0.577(849.4) = \boxed{490.1 \text{ N}}
$$

This result is physically informative. The shallower cable carries the larger force because it contributes less vertical support per unit tension.

**Quick generalization.** For two cables at angles $\alpha$ and $\beta$ measured from the horizontal on opposite sides of a weight $W$, the same two equations solve in closed form:

$$
T_A = \frac{W \cos\beta}{\sin(\alpha + \beta)}, \qquad T_B = \frac{W \cos\alpha}{\sin(\alpha + \beta)}
$$

Substituting $\alpha = 30^\circ$, $\beta = 60^\circ$, and $W = 981$ N gives $T_A = 981(0.5)/\sin 90^\circ = 490.5$ N and $T_B = 981(0.866)/1 = 849.4$ N, matching the step-by-step result above. The formula also shows why support becomes dangerous as the cables flatten toward each other: as $(\alpha + \beta) \to 0$, $\sin(\alpha+\beta) \to 0$ and both tensions grow without bound.

## Engineering Applications

| Application | Why Equilibrium Matters |
| --- | --- |
| Suspension Bridges | Cable forces must balance deck and live loads |
| Tower Cranes | Counterweights and booms must balance safely |
| Cable Cars | Support and tension systems must carry cabin loads |
| Sensor Mounts | Small brackets and cable systems still require force balance |

> **Engineering in Action:** The same equilibrium logic used on a hanging lamp also appears in bridge hangers, lifting rigs, antenna stays, and instrument-support assemblies. The scale changes, but the force-balance logic does not.

## Summary

### Key Equations

| Concept | Equation |
| --- | --- |
| Equilibrium (vector) | $\sum \vec{F} = 0$ |
| Equilibrium (2D) | $\sum F_x = 0$, $\sum F_y = 0$ |
| Weight | $W = mg$ |
| Tension Components | $T_x = T \cos\theta$, $T_y = T \sin\theta$ |

### Key Takeaways

- Equilibrium means the net force is zero.
- The free body diagram is the essential setup tool.
- Always define a coordinate system before writing equations.
- Tension acts along the cable, away from the object.
- A plausible answer should make physical sense in both sign and magnitude.
