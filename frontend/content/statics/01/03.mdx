# Two-Force and Three-Force Members

> **"Recognizing special cases saves time and reduces errors."**  
> Ferdinand P. Beer, *Vector Mechanics for Engineers*

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this section, you will be able to:

1. **Identify** two-force members in a structure and justify the identification from the loading
2. **Prove** the two-force member principle from $\sum \vec{F} = 0$ and $\sum \vec{M} = 0$
3. **Recognize** three-force member conditions (concurrency and the parallel special case)
4. **Use** concurrency to fix unknown reaction directions and **solve** for their magnitudes

## Opening: The Shortcut Hidden in Every Truss

Look at the underside of a steel railway truss bridge, or at the lattice boom of a tower crane lifting a precast panel. Almost every straight bar you see is loaded at only its two pinned ends and nowhere in between. That single fact is enormously powerful: it tells you, *before you write a single equation*, that the force in each bar acts straight along the bar. You instantly know the **line of action**; only the magnitude and the sign (tension or compression) remain.

This is not a trick. It is a consequence of equilibrium applied to a body loaded at exactly two points. When engineers analyze a 30-member roof truss, they are not solving 30 independent vector problems; they are exploiting the two-force member property at every joint so the unknowns collapse to scalar axial forces.

The same insight has a sharp safety edge. When a "two-force member" quietly stops being one (a worker hangs a load at mid-span, a bracket gets welded on, a moment leaks in through a stiff connection that was assumed pinned), the real force no longer runs along the bar. The bar now sees bending it was never sized for. Many connection failures trace back to an idealization that was true on the drawing and false in the field.

> 🎯 **Why This Matters**: In truss analysis, nearly every member is a two-force member. Recognizing the special case is what turns an intractable structure into a sequence of simple joint equations.

## Two-Force Members

### Definition

A **two-force member** is a rigid body that:

1. Has forces applied at **only two points** (call them $A$ and $B$),
2. Has **no moments (couples)** applied anywhere, and
3. Is in **equilibrium**.

Self-weight is treated as negligible, or lumped into the joint loads, so that nothing acts between $A$ and $B$.

### The Principle and Its Proof

For a two-force member in equilibrium, the two end forces must be **equal in magnitude, opposite in direction, and collinear** along line $AB$. This follows directly from the two equilibrium laws, applied in order.

**Force balance.** Let the resultant at $A$ be $\vec{F}_A$ and at $B$ be $\vec{F}_B$. Then

$$
\sum \vec{F} = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \vec{F}_A + \vec{F}_B = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \vec{F}_A = -\vec{F}_B
$$

So the two forces are already equal in magnitude and opposite in direction. They could still, in principle, be parallel but offset (forming a couple).

**Moment balance.** Take moments about point $A$. The force at $A$ has zero moment arm, so only $\vec{F}_B$ contributes:

$$
\sum \vec{M}_A = \vec{r}_{AB} \times \vec{F}_B = 0
$$

A cross product vanishes only when the two vectors are parallel. Therefore $\vec{F}_B$ must be parallel to $\vec{r}_{AB}$, the line from $A$ to $B$. Combined with the force-balance result, both forces lie **along line $AB$**: they are collinear. This rules out the offset-couple possibility.

$$
\boxed{\vec{F}_A = -\vec{F}_B \text{, both directed along line } AB}
$$

A two-force member is therefore either pulled apart (**tension**) or pushed together (**compression**). There is no third option for an ideal straight member.

### Visual Representation

```
Two-Force Member:

        F_A                           F_B
    ←────●═════════════════════════●────→
         A                         B

    • Forces at only 2 points (A and B)
    • Forces are equal, opposite, collinear
    • Member is in tension (as shown) or compression
```

### Identifying Two-Force Members

| Condition | Two-Force Member? |
|-----------|-------------------|
| Pin-connected at both ends, no other loads | ✅ Yes |
| Weight is negligible (or lumped at the pins) | ✅ Yes |
| Has a force applied in the middle | ❌ No |
| Has a distributed load along its length | ❌ No |
| Transmits a moment through a fixed/welded connection | ❌ No |

> **Golden Rule:** If anything pushes, pulls, or twists the member *between* its two end pins, the line-of-action shortcut is invalid. Verify isolation before you assume.

## Applications of Two-Force Members

### Trusses

In an idealized truss with pinned joints and weightless members, **every member** is a two-force member, so each carries only an axial force.

```
        P (load)
          ↓
          ●
         ╱ ╲
        ╱   ╲
       ╱     ╲
      ●───────●
      A   B   C
```

Each member (AB, BC, AC) experiences only axial force (tension or compression). This is exactly why the method of joints works: at every pin, the member forces are known *directions* and the joint equations $\sum F_x = 0$, $\sum F_y = 0$ solve for scalar magnitudes.

### Link Mechanisms

Connecting links, tie rods, hydraulic-cylinder rods, and crane pendants are typically two-force members between their pin joints.

```
    ●══════════●══════════●
    │          │          │
   Pin        Link       Pin
```

## Worked Example: Two-Force Members

### Example 3.1: Member Forces at a Loaded Joint

**Problem:** At joint $B$ of a symmetric A-frame, a vertical load $P = 1000\ \text{N}$ acts downward. Two pin-ended members, $BA$ and $BC$, run from $B$ down to supports $A$ and $C$. Each member makes an angle $\theta = 30^\circ$ with the horizontal. Both are two-force members. Find the force in each member and state tension or compression.

```
              P = 1000 N
                  ↓
                  B
                 ╱ ╲   θ = 30° from horizontal
               ╱     ╲
             ╱         ╲
            A           C
```

**Solution.**

*Step 1: Confirm the two-force condition.* Members $BA$ and $BC$ are loaded only at their end pins, so each force acts along the member. Let $F$ be the (common, by symmetry) magnitude, and assume both members are in **compression** so they push the joint $B$ upward and outward.

*Step 2: Free body diagram of joint B.* Three forces act on the pin at $B$: the downward load $P$, and the two member pushes directed from $B$ toward... away from $A$ and $C$ (upward-inward on the joint if compressive). Each member force has components $F\cos\theta$ horizontal and $F\sin\theta$ vertical.

*Step 3: Horizontal equilibrium.* By symmetry the two horizontal components are equal and opposite:

$$
\sum F_x = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad F\cos 30^\circ - F\cos 30^\circ = 0 \;\; \checkmark
$$

This is automatically satisfied and confirms the symmetry.

*Step 4: Vertical equilibrium.* The two vertical components together support the load:

$$
\sum F_y = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad 2F\sin 30^\circ - P = 0
$$

$$
F = \frac{P}{2\sin 30^\circ} = \frac{1000}{2(0.5)} = \boxed{1000\ \text{N (compression)}}
$$

*Step 5: Interpret.* Each member carries $1000\ \text{N}$. The compression sign is correct: the members must push up on $B$ to hold the load, which means $B$ pushes down on them, squeezing each member. Had we found a negative value, the true state would have been tension. Note also that the force per member here equals the full load $P$ because $2\sin 30^\circ = 1$; at shallower angles each member force would grow rapidly.

## Three-Force Members

### Definition

A **three-force member** is a rigid body that:

1. Has forces applied at **exactly three points**, and
2. Is in **equilibrium**.

### The Principle and Its Proof

For a three-force member in equilibrium, the three forces must be **coplanar** and either **concurrent** (their lines of action meet at one point) **or all parallel**.

**Why concurrent.** Suppose two of the forces, $\vec{F}_1$ and $\vec{F}_2$, are not parallel. Extend their lines of action until they intersect at a point $O$. Now take moments about $O$:

$$
\sum \vec{M}_O = 0
$$

Both $\vec{F}_1$ and $\vec{F}_2$ pass through $O$, so each contributes zero moment about $O$. The only remaining force is $\vec{F}_3$, so its moment about $O$ must also be zero:

$$
\vec{r}_{O \to 3} \times \vec{F}_3 = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \vec{F}_3 \text{ passes through } O
$$

All three lines of action therefore meet at the single point $O$. The **parallel** case is the limit in which $O$ recedes to infinity: if two forces are parallel they never intersect, and force balance then forces the third to be parallel as well.

### Visual Representation

```
Three-Force Member:

              F₁
               ↓
        ●──────●
       ╱        ╲
      ╱     O    ╲     ← Concurrent point
     ↙            ↘
   F₂              F₃

   All three lines of action meet at point O
```

> **Practical payoff:** Concurrency converts an unknown reaction with *both* an unknown magnitude and an unknown direction into a problem where the direction is fixed by geometry. That is one fewer unknown, often enough to make the problem statically determinate by inspection.

## Worked Examples: Three-Force Members

### Example 3.2: Reaction Direction by Parallelism

**Problem:** A beam is supported by a pin at $A$ and a roller at $B$, with a concentrated vertical load $P$ at point $C$. Determine the direction of the pin reaction at $A$.

```
        P
        ↓
    ●───C───●
    A       B
    △       ○
```

**Solution.**

*Step 1: Identify the three forces.* (1) Load $P$ at $C$, vertical and downward. (2) Roller reaction $R_B$ at $B$, vertical and upward (a roller reaction is perpendicular to its surface). (3) Pin reaction $R_A$ at $A$, direction unknown.

*Step 2: Apply the three-force principle.* Forces $P$ and $R_B$ are both vertical, hence parallel; their lines of action never intersect. By the parallel special case, the third force $R_A$ must also be **vertical**.

$$
\boxed{R_A \text{ is vertical}}
$$

We learned the *direction* of $R_A$ without any moment equation, purely from the geometry of the other two forces.

### Example 3.3: Concurrency Fixes a Pin Reaction (Full Numbers)

**Problem:** A rigid bar $AB$ of length $L = 3\ \text{m}$ and negligible weight is pinned to a wall at the bottom end $A$ and inclined at $60^\circ$ above the horizontal. A horizontal cable runs from the top end $B$ back to the wall, and a load $W = 500\ \text{N}$ hangs from $B$. Find the cable tension $T$ and the magnitude and direction of the pin reaction at $A$.

```
        wall
        │
   T ←──●  B
        │ ╲
        │  ╲  bar AB, 60° from horizontal
        │   ╲      ↓ W = 500 N (at B)
        │    ╲
        ●─────╲
        A  (pin)
```

**Solution.**

*Step 1: Locate the three forces.* The bar is a three-force member. Two of the forces, the cable tension $T$ (horizontal) and the weight $W$ (vertical), both act at point $B$. The third force is the pin reaction at $A$.

*Step 2: Use concurrency.* Since $T$ and $W$ both pass through $B$, their lines of action already intersect at $B$. By the three-force principle, the pin reaction at $A$ **must also pass through $B$**, so its line of action is directed straight along the bar from $A$ to $B$ (at $60^\circ$).

*Step 3: Geometry of B relative to A.*

$$
\Delta x = L\cos 60^\circ = 3(0.5) = 1.500\ \text{m}, \qquad \Delta y = L\sin 60^\circ = 3(0.8660) = 2.598\ \text{m}
$$

*Step 4: Moment about A to find $T$.* About $A$, only the two forces at $B$ contribute. The weight $W$ (down) acts at horizontal arm $\Delta x$; the tension $T$ (horizontal) acts at vertical arm $\Delta y$. Balancing the two moments:

$$
\sum M_A = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad T\,\Delta y = W\,\Delta x
$$

$$
T = \frac{W\,\Delta x}{\Delta y} = \frac{500(1.500)}{2.598} = \boxed{288.7\ \text{N}}
$$

*Step 5: Pin reaction by force balance.* The pin must balance the horizontal pull of the cable and the vertical weight:

$$
\sum F_x = 0 \Rightarrow R_{Ax} = T = 288.7\ \text{N}, \qquad \sum F_y = 0 \Rightarrow R_{Ay} = W = 500\ \text{N}
$$

$$
R_A = \sqrt{R_{Ax}^2 + R_{Ay}^2} = \sqrt{288.7^2 + 500^2} = \boxed{577.4\ \text{N}}
$$

$$
\alpha = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{R_{Ay}}{R_{Ax}}\right) = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{500}{288.7}\right) = \boxed{60.0^\circ}
$$

*Step 6: Verify against the principle.* The reaction comes out at exactly $60.0^\circ$ above the horizontal, which is the direction of line $AB$. This is the concurrency prediction confirmed by independent calculation: the three forces really do meet at $B$. The agreement is a built-in check on the arithmetic.

## Comparison Table

| Property | Two-Force Member | Three-Force Member |
|----------|------------------|-------------------|
| Number of force points | 2 | 3 |
| Force requirement | Equal, opposite, collinear | Concurrent or parallel |
| Common examples | Truss members, links, tie rods | Beams, brackets, frames |
| Known before solving | Force line of action (along AB) | Reaction direction (toward concurrency point) |
| Unknowns that remain | Magnitude and sign | Magnitudes (directions fixed by geometry) |

## Problem-Solving Strategy

### When You See Two Force Points

1. Check: is it *truly* loaded only at two points, with no couple? Confirm by isolating the member.
2. If yes: the force acts along line $AB$; you know its direction immediately.
3. Solve joint equilibrium for the scalar magnitude; the sign tells you tension or compression.

### When You See Three Force Points

1. Identify the lines of action of the two known (or more constrained) forces.
2. Find where those two lines intersect, the concurrency point $O$.
3. The third force must pass through $O$; this fixes its direction.
4. Take moments about $O$ (or about a known force's point) to peel off one unknown at a time, then finish with $\sum F_x = 0$ and $\sum F_y = 0$.

## Engineering Applications

| Application | Member Type | Why the Special Case Helps |
|-------------|-------------|----------------------------|
| Roof and bridge trusses | Two-force | Every bar is axial-only, so the method of joints reduces to scalar equations |
| Crane pendants and tie rods | Two-force | Tension line is known from geometry; only magnitude must be sized |
| Hydraulic-cylinder rods, linkage bars | Two-force | Force runs along the rod axis, setting bearing and buckling design loads |
| Pin-supported beams and brackets | Three-force | Concurrency fixes the pin-reaction direction before any moment equation |
| A-frame and gusset connections | Three-force | Reaction line of action is read off the geometry, simplifying joint design |

> **Engineering in Action:** A crane pendant cable and a bridge diagonal are the same idea at different scales. Both are two-force members, so the design question collapses to a single number per member: how much axial force, in tension or compression, and is the section strong enough to carry it.

## Summary

### Key Equations

| Concept | Result |
|---------|--------|
| Two-force balance | $\vec{F}_A = -\vec{F}_B$, collinear along $AB$ |
| Two-force collinearity | $\vec{r}_{AB} \times \vec{F}_B = 0$ |
| Three-force concurrency | All lines of action meet at one point $O$ |
| Reaction magnitude (Ex. 3.3) | $R_A = \sqrt{R_{Ax}^2 + R_{Ay}^2}$ |

### Key Takeaways

- ✅ **Two-force members** are loaded at only two points → the force is equal, opposite, and collinear along the member.
- ✅ The collinearity comes from $\sum M = 0$: an offset couple is impossible.
- ✅ **Three-force members** in equilibrium have forces that are concurrent (or, as a limit, parallel).
- ✅ Concurrency fixes an unknown reaction's *direction* from geometry, removing one unknown before you compute.
- ✅ Always re-check that nothing loads the member between its end points; a hidden mid-span load destroys the shortcut.

### Quick Reference

| Member Type | Condition | Consequence |
|-------------|-----------|-------------|
| Two-Force | 2 force points only | Forces along line AB |
| Three-Force | 3 force points | Lines of action meet at one point |
