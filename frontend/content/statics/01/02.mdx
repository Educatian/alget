# Free Body Diagrams

> **"The free body diagram is the language through which mechanics problems become solvable."**  
> - R.C. Hibbeler, *Engineering Mechanics: Statics*

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this section, you will be able to:

1. **Draw** complete and accurate free body diagrams for particles and simple rigid bodies
2. **Identify** all external forces acting on an isolated object
3. **Apply** a reliable six-step FBD workflow before writing equations
4. **Recognize** common diagram errors that lead to wrong equilibrium equations

---

## Why Free Body Diagrams Matter

In the previous section, equilibrium was expressed in a compact mathematical form:

$$
\sum \vec{F} = 0
$$

That equation looks simple, but it only helps after the correct forces are placed in it. The free body diagram is the step that converts a physical situation into a defensible model.

> **Engineering Reality:** Many statics errors are not algebra errors. They begin much earlier, when an engineer omits a support reaction, draws tension in the wrong direction, or forgets that a surface can only push normal to itself.

That is why experienced analysts slow down at the diagram stage. A careful free body diagram protects every later step.

> **A Failure That Started on the Diagram:** The 1981 Hyatt Regency walkway collapse in Kansas City killed 114 people. A design change doubled the load that one set of connector rods had to carry, but the connection was never re-drawn as an isolated free body. Had the affected hanger-rod and box-beam been isolated and the actual transferred force placed on the diagram, the doubled demand would have been obvious before fabrication. The arithmetic was not exotic; the missing step was an honest free body diagram of the changed connection.

---

## What a Free Body Diagram Actually Is

A **free body diagram** is a simplified sketch of one isolated object showing:

- the body of interest,
- all **external** forces acting on it,
- and a coordinate system for reference.

The key word is **free**. We imagine cutting the body away from everything touching it. Every removed connection is then replaced by the force or moment that connection exerts.

| Physical Connection | What Appears in the FBD |
| --- | --- |
| Cable or rope | Tension along the cable |
| Smooth surface | Normal force perpendicular to the surface |
| Rough surface | Normal force plus friction force |
| Pin support | Two reaction components |
| Fixed support | Two reaction components plus a moment |

This replacement step is the heart of modeling. It is where physical intuition enters the mechanics workflow.

---

## The Six-Step FBD Workflow

Use the same sequence every time:

### Step 1: Select the Body

Decide exactly what object you are analyzing. Draw it as simply as possible: a dot, block, bar, or beam.

### Step 2: Isolate It

Mentally cut the object away from cables, supports, and surrounding bodies.

### Step 3: Choose Axes

Define a coordinate system before resolving forces. Standard horizontal and vertical axes work in many problems, but rotated axes are often helpful on inclined surfaces.

### Step 4: Add Body Forces

The most common body force is weight:

$$
W = mg
$$

Weight always acts vertically downward toward Earth's center.

### Step 5: Add Contact and Connection Forces

At every cut connection, replace the support with the correct force model:

- cable -> tension along the cable
- smooth contact -> normal reaction only
- rough contact -> normal and friction
- pin -> two reaction components

### Step 6: Label Known and Unknown Quantities

Mark symbols, angles, and known magnitudes clearly before solving.

Drag the steps into the correct order to internalize the workflow. Notice that isolating the body must come before you can decide which connection forces to add.

## Force Identification Checklist

Before moving on to equilibrium equations, check each category deliberately.

| Category | Question to Ask | Typical Force |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gravity | Does the object have mass? | Weight $W = mg$ |
| Cables | Is the object suspended or tied? | Tension $T$ |
| Contact | Is it touching a surface? | Normal $N$, friction $f$ |
| Support | Is it pinned, fixed, or guided? | Reaction components |
| Applied Load | Did the problem state a push or pull? | External force $P$ or $F$ |

This checklist matters because missing a single external force can invalidate the entire solution.

## Choosing Axes: Why a Tilted Frame Pays Off

A free body diagram becomes useful only when forces are resolved into components. On an inclined plane, two choices exist, and they are not equally convenient.

If we use standard horizontal/vertical axes, the normal force $N$ points along the incline normal, so it splits into two components and *both* equilibrium equations contain *both* unknowns. If instead we rotate the axes so that $x$ runs along the slope and $y$ runs normal to it, then $N$ lies entirely on the $y$ axis and only the weight needs resolving. The two equations then decouple, and each one isolates a single unknown.

For a plane inclined at angle $\theta$, a vertical weight $W$ resolves in the tilted frame as:

$$
W_x = W \sin\theta \quad \text{(down the slope)}
$$

$$
W_y = W \cos\theta \quad \text{(into the slope)}
$$

This small modeling choice removes most of the algebra before it ever appears. We use it directly below.

## Worked Example 1: Block Held on a Smooth Incline

**Problem:** A 20 kg block rests on a smooth incline at $\theta = 30^\circ$. A cable parallel to the incline keeps it from sliding. Draw the FBD, then find the cable tension $T$ and the normal reaction $N$.

**Step 1: Isolate and draw the FBD.** Cutting the block free, three external forces appear: the weight $W = mg$ acting vertically down, the normal reaction $N$ perpendicular to the surface, and the cable tension $T$ acting up the slope. Because the surface is smooth, there is no friction.

**Step 2: Choose tilted axes.** Let $x$ point up the slope and $y$ point away from the surface. The weight is the only force that must be resolved:

$$
W = (20)(9.81) = 196.2 \text{ N}
$$

$$
W_x = W \sin 30^\circ = 196.2(0.5) = 98.1 \text{ N (down-slope)}
$$

$$
W_y = W \cos 30^\circ = 196.2(0.866) = 169.9 \text{ N (into surface)}
$$

**Step 3: Apply equilibrium along the slope.**

$$
\sum F_x = 0: \quad T - W\sin 30^\circ = 0
$$

$$
T = 98.1 \text{ N} \;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{T = 98.1 \text{ N}}
$$

**Step 4: Apply equilibrium normal to the slope.**

$$
\sum F_y = 0: \quad N - W\cos 30^\circ = 0
$$

$$
N = 169.9 \text{ N} \;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{N = 169.9 \text{ N}}
$$

> **Modeling Insight:** Surfaces do not push "up." They push normal to themselves. Notice $N = 169.9$ N is *less* than the weight $196.2$ N, because the slope only has to resist the component of weight pressed into it. A student who draws $N$ straight up gets $N = W$ and an immediately wrong answer.

## Worked Example 2: Hanging Mass Over a Frictionless Pulley

**Problem:** A 50 kg mass hangs from a rope that passes over a frictionless pulley and is anchored to a wall. The two rope segments leave the pulley at $90^\circ$ to each other: one segment vertical (down to the mass), the other horizontal (to the wall anchor). Find the rope tension and the resultant force the rope bundle exerts on the pulley axle.

**Step 1: FBD of the mass.** Two forces act: tension $T$ up and weight $W$ down.

$$
\sum F_y = 0: \quad T - mg = 0
$$

$$
T = (50)(9.81) = \boxed{490.5 \text{ N}}
$$

For an ideal frictionless pulley with a massless rope, the tension magnitude is identical on both rope segments, so each segment pulls with $490.5$ N.

**Step 2: FBD of the pulley.** Both rope segments pull *away* from the pulley along their own directions. Take $x$ horizontal (toward the wall anchor) and $y$ vertical (down toward the mass). The two equal tensions act along perpendicular directions, so the net rope pull on the pulley is the vector sum:

$$
R = \sqrt{T^2 + T^2} = T\sqrt{2} = 490.5\,(1.414) = \boxed{693.7 \text{ N}}
$$

directed at $45^\circ$ between the two rope segments. The axle reaction must equal and oppose this $693.7$ N pull to keep the pulley in equilibrium.

This example shows why the body of interest must be chosen carefully. The mass and the pulley belong to the same physical system, but they do **not** share a free body diagram: the mass sees one tension and gravity, while the pulley sees two tensions and an axle reaction.

> **Design Note:** A pulley that merely redirects a rope still carries a force larger than the load it serves. Bearings and brackets must be sized for the *resultant* axle force, here $693.7$ N, not for the $490.5$ N hanging weight.

## Worked Example 3: Ring Held by Two Cables and a Horizontal Pull

**Problem:** A small ring is held in equilibrium by two cables and a horizontal applied force $P$. Cable 1 pulls up-and-left at $60^\circ$ above the horizontal with tension $T_1 = 400$ N. Cable 2 is vertical and supports a 30 kg load, so $T_2 = (30)(9.81) = 294.3$ N acting downward. Find the horizontal force $P$ (acting to the right) and confirm the system is consistent.

**Step 1: FBD of the ring.** Three external forces act at the point: $T_1$ at $60^\circ$ above horizontal pointing up-left, the vertical downward load $T_2 = 294.3$ N, and the horizontal force $P$ pointing right.

**Step 2: Resolve $T_1$.**

$$
T_{1x} = T_1\cos 60^\circ = 400(0.5) = 200 \text{ N (to the left)}
$$

$$
T_{1y} = T_1\sin 60^\circ = 400(0.866) = 346.4 \text{ N (upward)}
$$

**Step 3: Horizontal equilibrium.** Taking right as positive:

$$
\sum F_x = 0: \quad P - T_{1x} = 0
$$

$$
P = 200 \;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{P = 200 \text{ N}}
$$

**Step 4: Vertical check.** Taking up as positive:

$$
\sum F_y = T_{1y} - T_2 = 346.4 - 294.3 = +52.1 \text{ N}
$$

The vertical sum is **not** zero, so with the stated values the ring is *not* in equilibrium; it has a net $52.1$ N upward imbalance. For true equilibrium the vertical cable would need to support $346.4$ N, meaning a load of $346.4 / 9.81 = 35.3$ kg. This is exactly the kind of inconsistency a disciplined FBD exposes before any hardware is built.

> **Takeaway:** Equilibrium is two equations in 2D, not one. A solution that satisfies $\sum F_x = 0$ can still fail $\sum F_y = 0$. Always close both.

## Common Errors

### Missing Weight

If the body has mass, weight must appear unless the problem explicitly neglects it.

### Wrong Tension Direction

Cables and ropes pull away from the object. They do not push toward it.

### Drawing Internal Forces

When analyzing one isolated part of a larger system, only draw forces exerted **on that part by something outside it**.

### Replacing Supports Incorrectly

A support model is a mechanical assumption. If a roller is drawn like a pin, the unknown count changes and the entire solution path changes with it.

## Design Interpretation

Free body diagrams are not just for textbook equilibrium problems. They are the first filter engineers use when checking whether a proposed design even makes physical sense.

For example:

- a bracket sketch may look symmetrical, but the FBD can reveal a large hidden moment,
- a cable layout may appear efficient, but the geometry may create an excessive tension demand,
- and a sliding mechanism may seem simple until the contact reactions show where friction and wear will concentrate.

That is why a disciplined FBD habit improves more than homework performance. It improves load-path thinking.

### Engineering Applications

| Application | What the FBD Reveals |
| --- | --- |
| Crane and hoist rigging | The resultant axle and sheave forces exceed the lifted load, so bearings must be sized to the vector sum, not the weight |
| Retaining walls and slopes | Resolving soil weight along tilted axes separates sliding demand from bearing demand |
| Bolted connections | Isolating one bracket exposes hidden moments and shear that a symmetric sketch hides (the Hyatt Regency lesson) |
| Cable-stayed and guyed masts | Shallow cable angles drive tension far above the supported load, visible only after components are drawn |
| Robotic grippers and contacts | Replacing each contact with normal-plus-friction shows where slip and wear concentrate |

> **Engineering in Action:** The same isolate-and-replace discipline scales from a 20 kg block to a 200-tonne bridge segment. The body changes; the workflow does not.

---

## A Practical Self-Check Before Solving

Ask these questions:

- Is the body clearly isolated?
- Did I include every external force source?
- Are force directions consistent with the physical connection?
- Is the coordinate system chosen for convenience?
- Are there any forces I drew without a physical cause?

If the answer to the last question is yes, pause and fix the diagram before writing equilibrium equations.

## Summary

### Key Equations

| Concept | Equation |
| --- | --- |
| Particle equilibrium (2D) | $\sum F_x = 0$, $\sum F_y = 0$ |
| Weight (body force) | $W = mg$ |
| Weight on incline (tilted axes) | $W_x = W\sin\theta$, $W_y = W\cos\theta$ |
| Resultant of two perpendicular equal tensions | $R = T\sqrt{2}$ |
| Cable component resolution | $T_x = T\cos\theta$, $T_y = T\sin\theta$ |

### Key Takeaways

- The free body diagram is the modeling foundation of statics; isolate the body before writing any equations.
- Replace every support or contact with the force it exerts: cable to tension, smooth contact to normal only, rough contact to normal plus friction, pin to two components, fixed support to two components plus a moment.
- Weight acts vertically downward, tension acts along the cable away from the body, and normal force acts perpendicular to the contact surface, not "up."
- Rotating axes to align with an inclined surface decouples the equilibrium equations and removes most of the algebra.
- A pulley or redirect point carries a resultant force larger than the load it serves; size hardware to the vector sum.
- 2D equilibrium is two equations: satisfying one does not guarantee the other, so always close both $\sum F_x$ and $\sum F_y$.
