## Learning Objectives

By the end of this section, you will be able to:

1. **Apply** the section-and-replace logic to compute internal normal force $N$, shear $V$, and bending moment $M$ at any beam cross section
2. **Use** a consistent sign convention so left-side and right-side free body diagrams tell the same physical story
3. **Construct** shear and moment diagrams using $dV/dx = -w$ and $dM/dx = V$, including jumps at point loads and couples
4. **Locate** the position and value of maximum bending moment by finding where $V$ crosses zero

## Opening: A Walkway That Forgot Its Internal Forces

On July 17, 1981, two suspended walkways inside the Kansas City Hyatt Regency collapsed onto a crowded lobby, killing 114 people. The external loads were ordinary (people standing and dancing), but a late design change doubled the internal force carried by a single connection. The supports still balanced the building's weight; equilibrium of the whole structure was never the issue. The structure failed because the **internal force at one cross section** exceeded what the connection could carry.

That is the central lesson of this chapter. Global equilibrium tells you the structure does not fly away or tip over. It says nothing about whether any individual cross section can survive the force passing through it. To size a beam, choose a connection, or predict where a member will crack, you must compute the forces acting *inside* the body.

> **The Big Picture:** Every later mechanics course, Mechanics of Materials, Structural Analysis, Machine Design, depends on the ability to find $N(x)$, $V(x)$, and $M(x)$ along a member and to read off where they peak.

## From External Equilibrium to What Happens Inside

Up to this chapter, statics has asked the same question: are the external forces on a body in equilibrium? Trusses extended that question to forces in slender two-force members. Real engineering structures (beams, frames, shafts) carry distributed loading, point loads, and moments along their length, and the question that matters becomes different. **At any cross section, what internal forces hold the two halves of the body together?**

That is the subject of internal-force analysis. Hibbeler dedicates a full chapter (Ch. 7), Beer and Johnston dedicate Ch. 7, and Meriam dedicates Ch. 5 to this topic, because every later mechanics course (Mechanics of Materials, Structural Analysis, Machine Design) depends on the ability to draw shear and moment diagrams from a beam's loading.

This module covers the three skills that make this analysis defensible: **the section-and-replace logic, sign conventions, and shear/moment diagrams as the most useful summary of an entire beam**.

A useful way to keep the three internal quantities straight is to ask what relative motion each one resists. The normal force $N$ resists the two halves sliding apart along the axis (stretching or crushing). The shear force $V$ resists the two halves sliding past each other transversely. The bending moment $M$ resists the two halves rotating relative to one another (curling the beam). Every cross section must supply all three simultaneously, and any one of them reaching its limit is enough to cause failure. That is precisely why the Hyatt connection failed even though the building as a whole was in equilibrium: global balance never guarantees that the local triplet at a single section stays within safe limits.

---

## The Section-and-Replace Logic

To find internal forces at a cross section, you imaginarily cut the body at that section, isolate one side as a free body, and replace the action of the cut-away portion with three quantities: a **normal (axial) force N**, a **shear force V**, and a **bending moment M**. (In 3D you also have torsion T and two transverse shears, but planar analysis covers most undergraduate problems.)

Step 1: solve the global equilibrium of the entire beam to find the support reactions.

Step 2: choose any cross section x. Cut the beam at x.

Step 3: draw the free-body diagram of one side (typically the left side, by convention). Include the support reactions, all distributed and point loads on that side, and the unknown internal triplet (N, V, M) at the cut.

Step 4: apply equilibrium equations to the chosen segment:

$$\sum F_x = 0, \quad \sum F_y = 0, \quad \sum M_{\text{cut}} = 0$$

This yields N(x), V(x), M(x) at that section. Repeat for any other section, or write the expressions as functions of x within each loading region.

The "section both sides should give the same answer" check is real; choosing the right side instead of the left should produce the same N, V, M with the corresponding sign convention.

---

## Sign Conventions That Match the Diagrams

Inconsistent sign conventions are the leading cause of student errors here. The standard textbook convention treats internal forces and moments as positive when they correspond to:

- **Positive shear V**: the left portion pushes upward on the right portion (or equivalently, the right portion pushes downward on the left portion). Visually, positive V tends to rotate a small segment clockwise.
- **Positive bending moment M**: produces concave-up bending (the top fiber compresses, the bottom fiber elongates). Visually, positive M makes the segment "smile" (sad face = negative M).
- **Positive normal force N**: tension in the member.

Under this convention the internal forces drawn on a left-side free body and on a right-side free body are mirror-image arrows that tell the same physical story. Mixing conventions partway through a problem is a guaranteed sign error.

## Building Shear and Moment Diagrams

Drawing V(x) and M(x) over the full beam length gives engineers two of the most useful summaries in mechanics. Three relations make them easy to construct.

**Relation 1 (load to shear):**

$$\frac{dV}{dx} = -w(x)$$

where w(x) is the distributed load (positive downward). The slope of the shear diagram equals the negative of the local distributed load.

**Relation 2 (shear to moment):**

$$\frac{dM}{dx} = V(x)$$

The slope of the moment diagram equals the shear at that point. Where V = 0, M is at a local extremum, exactly where the maximum bending stress concentrates.

**Relation 3 (jumps at point loads and couples):**

A concentrated transverse force P creates a step in V of magnitude $-P$ (downward force ⇒ shear drops). A concentrated couple $M_0$ creates a step in M of magnitude $\pm M_0$ depending on its sense.

**Where the slope relations come from.** Isolate a differential slice of beam of width $dx$ carrying distributed load $w$ (downward). The left face carries $V$ and $M$; the right face carries $V + dV$ and $M + dM$. Vertical equilibrium of the slice gives $V - w\,dx - (V + dV) = 0$, so $dV = -w\,dx$, which is Relation 1. Taking moments about the right face (and dropping the second-order term $w\,dx \cdot dx/2$) gives $M + V\,dx - (M + dM) = 0$, so $dM = V\,dx$, which is Relation 2. The two relations are therefore not separate rules to memorize; they are direct consequences of applying $\sum F_y = 0$ and $\sum M = 0$ to an infinitesimal element.

These three relations let you sketch shear and moment diagrams without writing piecewise equations every time. You can also work backwards: integrate $w$ to get $V$ (with the support reaction as the integration constant), then integrate $V$ to get $M$. Because $M$ is the integral of $V$, the area under the shear diagram between two sections equals the change in moment between them, a fast graphical check on any diagram you draw.

For a simply supported beam carrying a full-length uniform load $w$, the slope relations integrate to $V(x) = wL/2 - wx$ and $M(x) = (wL/2)x - wx^2/2$. Drag the load and span below and watch how the closed-form peak $M_{\max} = wL^2/8$ (located at midspan where $V = 0$) responds. Notice that doubling the span quadruples the peak moment while doubling the load only doubles it.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Simply Supported Beam with a Mid-Span Point Load (Symbolic)

Consider a simply supported beam of length $L$ with a point load $P$ at the midpoint. Start here to fix the *pattern*; the next examples plug in real numbers.

**Step 1: Reactions.** By symmetry, $A_y = B_y = P/2$.

**Step 2: Section in $0 < x < L/2$.** The left-side free body diagram has only $A_y$ and the unknowns at the cut:

$$V(x) = A_y = P/2, \quad M(x) = A_y \cdot x = (P/2)x$$

**Step 3: Section in $L/2 < x < L$.** The left side now also includes $P$:

$$V(x) = A_y - P = -P/2, \quad M(x) = A_y x - P(x - L/2) = (P/2)(L - x)$$

**Step 4: Diagrams.** $V$ is $+P/2$ then drops by $P$ at midspan to $-P/2$. $M$ rises linearly from 0 to $PL/4$ at midspan, then falls back to 0:

$$\boxed{M_{\max} = \frac{PL}{4} \text{ at } x = \frac{L}{2}}$$

---

### Example 2: Cantilever with a Uniform Distributed Load (Numbers)

**Problem:** A cantilever beam is fixed at the wall and free at the tip, length $L = 3$ m. It carries a uniform distributed load $w = 5$ kN/m over its full length. Find $V(x)$, $M(x)$, and the maximum shear and moment.

**Step 1: Draw the FBD and pick a coordinate.** Measure $x$ from the **free end** toward the wall. Sectioning the beam at distance $x$ and keeping the *free-end side* (no reaction on it) avoids solving the wall reactions first.

**Step 2: Section equilibrium.** The load on the kept segment is $w x$, acting at its centroid $x/2$ from the cut.

$$\sum F_y = 0: \quad V(x) - w x = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; V(x) = w x$$

$$\sum M_{\text{cut}} = 0: \quad M(x) + (w x)\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; M(x) = -\frac{w x^2}{2}$$

**Step 3: Evaluate at the wall ($x = L = 3$ m).**

$$V_{\max} = w L = (5)(3) = \boxed{15 \text{ kN}}$$

$$M_{\max} = -\frac{w L^2}{2} = -\frac{(5)(3)^2}{2} = \boxed{-22.5 \text{ kN·m}}$$

The negative sign means the top fiber is in tension (concave-down "frown"), exactly as expected at a cantilever support. The shear grows linearly from 0 at the tip to its maximum at the wall; the moment grows as a parabola. This is why cantilever beams are always deepest at the fixed end.

---

### Example 3: Simply Supported Beam with a Uniform Distributed Load (V(x) and M(x))

**Problem:** A simply supported beam of length $L = 6$ m carries a uniform load $w = 4$ kN/m over its full length. Find the reactions, write $V(x)$ and $M(x)$, and locate and evaluate $M_{\max}$.

**Step 1: Reactions.** Total load $= wL = 24$ kN, centered at midspan. By symmetry:

$$A_y = B_y = \frac{wL}{2} = \frac{(4)(6)}{2} = 12 \text{ kN}$$

**Step 2: Section at $x$ from the left support.** Keep the left FBD: it carries $A_y$ up and a distributed load $w x$ down (centroid at $x/2$).

$$V(x) = A_y - w x = 12 - 4x$$

$$M(x) = A_y x - (w x)\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) = 12 x - 2 x^2$$

**Step 3: Locate $M_{\max}$.** Maximum moment occurs where $V = 0$:

$$12 - 4x = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x = 3 \text{ m (midspan, as expected by symmetry)}$$

**Step 4: Evaluate.**

$$M_{\max} = 12(3) - 2(3)^2 = 36 - 18 = \boxed{18 \text{ kN·m}}$$

As a check, the closed-form result for a UDL on a simple span is $M_{\max} = wL^2/8 = (4)(6)^2/8 = 18$ kN·m. The two agree.

**Problem:** A simply supported beam spans $L = 8$ m. A point load $P = 10$ kN is applied at $a = 3$ m from the left support $A$. Find both reactions and the maximum bending moment.

**Step 1: Reactions** (global equilibrium, moments about $A$):

$$\sum M_A = 0: \quad B_y(8) - (10)(3) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; B_y = \frac{30}{8} = 3.75 \text{ kN}$$

$$A_y = P - B_y = 10 - 3.75 = 6.25 \text{ kN}$$

**Step 2: Where is $M_{\max}$?** For a single point load, shear is constant on each side and crosses zero *at the load*, so $M$ peaks directly under $P$.

**Step 3: Evaluate** (left FBD up to the load):

$$M_{\max} = A_y \cdot a = (6.25)(3) = \boxed{18.75 \text{ kN·m}}$$

Notice the maximum bending moment ($18.75$ kN·m) is larger than for the centered UDL of Example 3 even though the total load (10 kN) is smaller, because a concentrated load is far less forgiving than a spread-out one. That comparison is exactly the judgment a designer makes when choosing where to place equipment on a floor beam.

> **Workflow Reminder:** The procedure never changes. Solve reactions, section the beam, keep one side, apply $\sum F = 0$ and $\sum M_{\text{cut}} = 0$, then locate $M_{\max}$ where $V = 0$.

### Example 5: Simply Supported Beam with an Applied Couple (the Moment Jump)

**Problem:** A simply supported beam spans $L = 6$ m. A concentrated counterclockwise couple $M_0 = 12$ kN·m is applied at the midpoint ($x = 3$ m). There are no transverse loads. Find the reactions, the shear, and the bending moment just to the left and just to the right of the couple. This is the case Relation 3 predicts but that the earlier examples never exercised.

**Step 1: Reactions.** With no vertical applied loads, vertical equilibrium gives $A_y + B_y = 0$, so the two reactions must be equal and opposite. Taking moments about $A$ (counterclockwise positive):

$$\sum M_A = 0: \quad B_y(6) + M_0 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; B_y = -\frac{12}{6} = -2 \text{ kN}$$

$$A_y = -B_y = +2 \text{ kN downward correction} \;\Rightarrow\; A_y = -2 \text{ kN}$$

So $A_y = -2$ kN (acting downward) and $B_y = +2$ kN (acting upward). The couple is carried by a force *pair* at the two supports, which is why the reactions appear even with no transverse load.

**Step 2: Shear.** Sectioning anywhere and keeping the left side, the only transverse force is $A_y$:

$$V(x) = A_y = \boxed{-2 \text{ kN (constant over the whole span)}}$$

A pure couple produces no jump in $V$; the shear diagram is a flat line.

**Step 3: Moment just left of the couple ($x = 3^-$).** The left segment carries only $A_y$:

$$M(3^-) = A_y \cdot x = (-2)(3) = \boxed{-6 \text{ kN·m}}$$

**Step 4: Moment just right of the couple ($x = 3^+$).** Now the applied couple $M_0$ is inside the left segment and adds directly:

$$M(3^+) = A_y \cdot x + M_0 = (-2)(3) + 12 = \boxed{+6 \text{ kN·m}}$$

The moment jumps by exactly $M_0 = 12$ kN·m at the couple, with no change in shear, which is exactly Relation 3. As a final check, at the right support $M(6) = A_y(6) + M_0 = (-2)(6) + 12 = 0$, as it must be for a free end of a simple span. The takeaway for design is that an applied couple (a motor mount reacting torque, an eccentric column load delivered through a stub) creates a sudden step in the moment diagram, and the larger of the two adjacent values is what the section must be sized for.

## Common Errors and Their Diagnoses

- **Forgetting to solve reactions first.** Sectioning a beam without first finding the supports leads to under-determined free-body equations.
- **Mixing left-side and right-side conventions.** Pick a side and stay with it. The internal arrows on the left FBD point opposite to the same arrows on the right FBD by Newton's third law.
- **Treating positive shear as "force on the cut" instead of using a consistent sign rule.** The convention above (positive V rotates segment clockwise) keeps signs consistent with derived shear-moment diagram slope rules.
- **Missing the step in V at a point load.** A concentrated transverse force always produces a discontinuity in V. The diagram has a vertical jump there.
- **Looking for $M_{\max}$ everywhere instead of where V = 0.** $dM/dx = V$, so M is extremized where V crosses zero (or at a discontinuity from a point couple).

## Engineering Applications

| Application | Why Internal Forces Matter |
| --- | --- |
| Floor and roof beams | $M_{\max}$ sizes the section depth and material |
| Bridge girders | Moving live loads shift where $V = 0$ and $M_{\max}$ occurs |
| Crane booms | Combined axial $N$ and bending $M$ govern member selection |
| Shafts and axles | Internal bending plus torsion sets the fatigue life |

> **Engineering in Action:** The same section-and-replace logic that sizes a 6 m classroom floor beam also sizes a 60 m bridge girder. Only the loads and the length change; the workflow does not.

## Summary

### Key Equations

| Concept | Equation |
| --- | --- |
| Load to shear | $dV/dx = -w(x)$ |
| Shear to moment | $dM/dx = V(x)$ |
| Section equilibrium | $\sum F_x = 0,\; \sum F_y = 0,\; \sum M_{\text{cut}} = 0$ |
| UDL on simple span | $M_{\max} = wL^2/8$ |
| Central point load on simple span | $M_{\max} = PL/4$ |
| Step at an applied couple | $\Delta M = \pm M_0$ (no change in $V$) |
| Moment from shear area | $\Delta M = \int V\,dx$ (area under $V$) |

### Key Takeaways

- Solve the support reactions before sectioning the beam.
- Cut the beam, keep one side, and replace the cut-away portion with $N$, $V$, $M$.
- A consistent sign convention makes left-side and right-side free bodies agree.
- Shear steps down by $P$ at a downward point load; moment steps at a point couple.
- The maximum bending moment occurs where the shear $V$ crosses zero.
