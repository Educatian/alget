# Simple Trusses

> **Trusses achieve high structural efficiency by carrying load mainly through axial member forces.**

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this section, you should be able to:

- define a truss and identify members and joints,
- state the main assumptions used in truss analysis,
- check whether a planar truss is stable and statically determinate,
- and recognize zero-force members before starting full calculations.

---

## What Makes a Truss Special

A truss is a structure built from straight members connected at joints. In ideal statics analysis, the members carry load primarily through **axial tension or compression** rather than bending.

That is why trusses are so efficient in bridges, roofs, towers, and cranes:

- material is placed along direct load paths,
- members can remain slender,
- and repeated triangular geometry provides stiffness without filling the whole region with solid material.

This is also why truss assumptions matter. If the assumptions fail badly, the simple analysis no longer matches the real structure.

---

## Core Truss Assumptions

In introductory statics, we usually assume:

- joints are frictionless pins,
- external loads act only at joints,
- member self-weight is negligible or lumped at joints,
- and members are straight two-force members.

Under these assumptions, each member carries either:

- **tension**, pulling away from a joint,
- or **compression**, pushing toward a joint.

This simplifies the structure enormously because we do not need to model each member as a full bending beam in the first pass.

---

## Why Triangles Matter

A triangle is the simplest polygon that is geometrically stable without changing member lengths. A rectangle can deform into a parallelogram if the joints are pin-connected, but a triangle cannot do that without stretching or shortening a member.

That is why simple trusses are built by:

- starting with a triangle,
- then adding one new joint and two new members at a time.

This construction logic explains why triangular forms dominate classical truss systems.

## The Determinacy Check

For a planar truss, a common first check is:

$$
\boxed{m = 2j - 3}
$$

where:

- $m$ is the number of members,
- and $j$ is the number of joints.

Interpret the result like this:

- if $m < 2j - 3$, the truss is usually unstable,
- if $m = 2j - 3$, it is often statically determinate,
- if $m > 2j - 3$, it is usually statically indeterminate.

This is a necessary structural check, but it is not the whole story. Geometry still matters. A badly arranged truss can satisfy the count and still behave as a mechanism.

---

## Tension, Compression, and Physical Meaning

A truss calculation is not only about getting a number. You also need to interpret what the sign means.

- **Tension:** the member is being stretched
- **Compression:** the member is being squeezed

This matters because compression members are often more vulnerable to buckling, while tension members are often governed by net-section or connection strength.

So identifying whether a member is in tension or compression is already part of design judgment, not just bookkeeping.

---

## Zero-Force Members

Before solving a full truss, look for zero-force members. They appear frequently and can simplify the whole problem.

Two common rules are:

- At an unloaded joint with only two non-collinear members, both are zero-force members.
- At a joint with three members where two are collinear and there is no external load or support reaction at that joint, the non-collinear member is zero-force.

Why would engineers include a zero-force member at all?

- to stabilize the structure under a different load case,
- to improve stiffness,
- or to support the structure during construction.

## Worked Example: Checking Determinacy

Suppose a truss has 11 members and 7 joints.

Compute:

$$
2j - 3 = 2(7) - 3 = 11
$$

Since $m = 11$, the count suggests the truss is statically determinate.

That does **not** mean the analysis is finished. You would still inspect:

- whether the support arrangement is appropriate,
- whether the geometry is triangulated,
- and whether the truss has any obvious mechanism-like behavior.

## Common Truss Families

Several named truss families appear often in practice:

- **Warren truss:** repeated triangular pattern
- **Pratt truss:** diagonals typically oriented to place long diagonals in tension under common loads
- **Howe truss:** opposite diagonal orientation relative to Pratt
- **K-truss:** subdivided panels for longer spans

The naming is less important than understanding what the layout does to member-force patterns and constructability.

---

## Practical Design Heuristics

Before moving to analysis, ask:

- Is the load applied mainly at joints?
- Are the supports modeled correctly?
- Does the geometry form stable triangular load paths?
- Are there obvious zero-force members that simplify the first pass?
- Which members might be compression-critical if the loads increase?

Those questions help you read the structure before you start solving equations.
