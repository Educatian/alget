## Opening: The Roof That Reads Like a Spreadsheet

Walk into any warehouse, gymnasium, or train shed and look up. The triangulated steel overhead is a truss, and every diagonal you see is carrying a specific number of kilonewtons in either tension or compression. A roof truss in a snow-loaded region might collapse not because a member "broke" abstractly, but because one slender diagonal that the engineer expected to pull in tension actually pushed in compression and buckled. The difference between a tie that can be a thin rod and a strut that must be a stocky tube comes down to a single algebraic sign.

The method of joints is how that sign gets pinned down. It turns a whole structure into a sequence of tiny, identical equilibrium problems, one joint at a time, each one no harder than balancing forces on a particle. By the end you know not just whether the structure stands, but exactly how hard each member is working and in which sense.

> **The Big Picture:** Once you can read a truss joint by joint, you can size members, flag buckling-prone struts, and spot members that carry almost nothing. The same two scalar equations that balanced a hanging lamp now map the entire load path of a bridge or roof.

By the end of this section, you should be able to:

2. **Draw** a correct free-body diagram for an individual joint.
3. **Solve** member forces using the two joint-equilibrium equations.
4. **Interpret** the algebraic sign of each result as tension or compression.
5. **Recognize** zero-force members and use them to shorten the analysis.

## Why the Method Works

A truss is built from **two-force members**: straight bars pin-connected at their ends, loaded only at the joints. Because a two-force member carries force only along its own axis, the only unknown for each member is a single scalar magnitude (plus a sign telling us tension or compression).

Now isolate a single joint. All the member forces meeting there are concurrent: they pass through one point. A point cannot rotate under concurrent forces, so the moment equation is automatically satisfied and gives nothing new. That leaves exactly two useful equations per joint in 2D:

$$
\sum F_x = 0, \qquad \sum F_y = 0
$$

Two equations means we can solve at most **two unknown member forces** at any single joint. That single fact drives the entire strategy: always begin at a joint where no more than two members are still unknown, solve them, and let those now-known forces unlock the next joint.

> **Key Insight:** The method is not random joint-hopping. It is a deliberate sequence chosen so that every joint you visit has exactly two (or fewer) unknowns when you arrive.

At a support joint A carrying an upward reaction $A_y$, with one inclined member rising at angle $\theta$ and one horizontal member, vertical equilibrium $A_y + F_{AC}\sin\theta = 0$ fixes the inclined force, and horizontal equilibrium $F_{AB} + F_{AC}\cos\theta = 0$ then fixes the chord. ## Tension-First Sign Convention

Before you know an answer, you do not know whether a member pushes or pulls. The discipline that removes the guesswork is a fixed convention:

- **Draw every unknown member force as tension**, an arrow pointing *away from the joint*, along the member.
- Solve the equilibrium equations as written.
- **Read the sign of the result.**

| Result | Meaning | Physical sense |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Positive ($+$) | Tension, as assumed | Member pulls on the joint; it is being stretched |
| Negative ($-$) | Compression | Member pushes on the joint; it is being squeezed |

A negative number is not a mistake and does not mean you must redo the joint with a flipped arrow. The magnitude is correct; the sign simply reports the true sense. Because every member is assumed tensile everywhere, the same member force keeps one consistent symbol as you carry it from joint to joint.

> **Critical Check:** When member AC is shown as tension at joint A (arrow away from A toward C), Newton's third law requires the *same* tension to appear at joint C as an arrow pointing away from C toward A. The two arrows point in opposite global directions but represent one identical scalar $F_{AC}$.

## Standard Procedure

Use this workflow every time:

1. **Solve the support reactions** from whole-structure equilibrium ($\sum F_x = 0$, $\sum F_y = 0$, $\sum M = 0$).
2. **Pick a starting joint** with no more than two unknown member forces (a support joint is usually ideal).
3. **Draw the joint free-body diagram**, showing external loads, support reactions, and each unknown member force as tension.
4. **Resolve** each inclined member force into $x$ and $y$ components.
5. **Apply** $\sum F_x = 0$ and $\sum F_y = 0$ and solve the two unknowns.
6. **Interpret** the signs (positive = tension, negative = compression).
7. **Advance** to an adjacent joint, now using the member forces you just found.

The procedure is repetitive by design. Reliability comes from disciplined sequencing, not cleverness.

## Worked Example 1: Symmetric Triangular Truss

**Problem.** A simple triangular truss has a pin at A$(0,0)$, a roller at B$(6\text{ m}, 0)$, and an apex at C. The two inclined members AC and BC each rise at $30^\circ$ above the horizontal, so the apex height is $h = 3\tan 30^\circ = 1.732$ m. A vertical load of $10$ kN acts downward at C. Find the force in every member and label it tension or compression.

**Step 1: Support reactions.**

By symmetry of geometry and loading, each vertical support carries half the load:

$$
A_y = B_y = \frac{10}{2} = 5 \text{ kN (up)}, \qquad A_x = 0
$$

(The pin's horizontal reaction is zero because no horizontal load is applied.)

**Step 2: Start at joint A** (a support joint with only two unknowns, $F_{AB}$ and $F_{AC}$). Draw the FBD: reaction $A_y = 5$ kN up, member AB pulling horizontally toward B ($+x$), member AC pulling up the incline at $30^\circ$. Both members assumed in tension.

Vertical equilibrium first, because only $F_{AC}$ has a vertical component:

$$
\sum F_y = 0: \quad A_y + F_{AC}\sin 30^\circ = 0
$$

$$
5 + F_{AC}(0.5) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; F_{AC} = -10 \text{ kN}
$$

The negative sign means AC is in **compression**:

$$
\boxed{F_{AC} = 10 \text{ kN (C)}}
$$

Now horizontal equilibrium:

$$
\sum F_x = 0: \quad F_{AB} + F_{AC}\cos 30^\circ = 0
$$

$$
F_{AB} + (-10)(0.866) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; F_{AB} = 8.66 \text{ kN}
$$

Positive, so AB is in **tension**:

$$
\boxed{F_{AB} = 8.66 \text{ kN (T)}}
$$

**Step 3: Joint B by symmetry.** The mirror-image joint gives

$$
\boxed{F_{BC} = 10 \text{ kN (C)}}
$$

**Step 4: Verify at joint C.** The apex sees the $10$ kN load down plus both inclined members. With AC and BC each in $10$ kN compression (pushing *toward* the joint, i.e. up the inclines from C's view), the vertical check is

$$
\sum F_y = -10 + 10\sin 30^\circ + 10\sin 30^\circ = -10 + 5 + 5 = 0 \;\checkmark
$$

and the horizontal components ($10\cos 30^\circ$ left and right) cancel. The solution closes. The result also makes engineering sense: the inclined top members are squeezed (the load pushes the apex down onto them) while the bottom chord is stretched as it ties the two feet together.

## Worked Example 2: Asymmetric Loading (Full Workflow)

**Problem.** Same triangular geometry (A pin at origin, B roller at $6$ m, apex C at height $h = 1.732$ m), but now joint C carries a downward load of $10$ kN *and* a horizontal load of $4$ kN to the right. Find all member forces.

**Step 1: Support reactions (the symmetry shortcut is gone, so use full equilibrium).**

Horizontal: the pin must absorb the horizontal load.

$$
\sum F_x = 0: \quad A_x + 4 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; A_x = -4 \text{ kN} \;(\text{i.e. } 4 \text{ kN to the left})
$$

Moment about A (counterclockwise positive). The roller reaction $B_y$ acts at $x = 6$; the $10$ kN down acts at $x = 3$; the $4$ kN horizontal acts at height $h = 1.732$:

$$
\sum M_A = 0: \quad B_y(6) - 10(3) - 4(1.732) = 0
$$

$$
B_y = \frac{30 + 6.928}{6} = 6.155 \text{ kN (up)}
$$

Vertical:

$$
\sum F_y = 0: \quad A_y + B_y - 10 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; A_y = 10 - 6.155 = 3.845 \text{ kN (up)}
$$

**Step 2: Joint A** (knowns $A_x = -4$, $A_y = 3.845$; unknowns $F_{AB}$ along $+x$, $F_{AC}$ up the $30^\circ$ incline, both tension-assumed).

$$
\sum F_y = 0: \quad A_y + F_{AC}\sin 30^\circ = 0
$$

$$
3.845 + F_{AC}(0.5) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{F_{AC} = -7.69 \text{ kN} = 7.69 \text{ kN (C)}}
$$

$$
\sum F_x = 0: \quad A_x + F_{AB} + F_{AC}\cos 30^\circ = 0
$$

$$
-4 + F_{AB} + (-7.69)(0.866) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; F_{AB} = 4 + 6.66 = \boxed{10.66 \text{ kN (T)}}
$$

**Step 3: Joint B** (roller reaction $B_y = 6.155$ up; member BA now known, pulling toward A in the $-x$ direction at $10.66$ kN tension; unknown $F_{BC}$ up the incline toward C). The unit vector from B to C is $(-\cos 30^\circ, \sin 30^\circ)$.

$$
\sum F_y = 0: \quad B_y + F_{BC}\sin 30^\circ = 0
$$

$$
6.155 + F_{BC}(0.5) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{F_{BC} = -12.31 \text{ kN} = 12.31 \text{ kN (C)}}
$$

**Step 4: Horizontal check at joint B.** Member BA pulls in $-x$ ($-10.66$ kN); member BC contributes $F_{BC}\cos 30^\circ$ in the $-x$ sense, which is $(-12.31)(-0.866) = +10.66$ kN:

$$
\sum F_x = -10.66 + 10.66 = 0 \;\checkmark
$$

The check closes, confirming the reactions and member forces are mutually consistent. Notice how the $4$ kN horizontal push made the two inclined members carry *different* compressions ($7.69$ vs $12.31$ kN): the windward and leeward sides of a loaded truss rarely work equally hard, which is exactly the kind of result that drives unequal member sizing in real design.

## Zero-Force Members

Some members carry no load under a given loading. Spotting them before you compute saves time and clarifies the load path. Two rules cover most cases:

1. **Two-member joint, no external load, members not collinear:** both members are zero-force. With only two non-aligned members and nothing else at the joint, each component equation forces each member to zero.
2. **Three-member joint, two members collinear, no external load:** the third (non-collinear) member is zero-force, and the two collinear members carry equal force.

**Quick example.** Consider a joint J where a horizontal bottom chord runs straight through (members JL and JR collinear), a vertical member JV rises, and no external load or support acts at J. Taking $y$ perpendicular to the collinear chord:

$$
\sum F_y = 0: \quad F_{JV} = 0
$$

$$
\boxed{F_{JV} = 0}
$$

The vertical member carries nothing for this loading. It is not useless: it braces the long chord against buckling and will carry force under different loads. But for the present analysis it can be set aside, and the two chord segments simply transmit equal force through the joint.

> **Why this matters:** Identifying zero-force members first can collapse a frightening-looking truss into just a handful of joints you actually need to solve.

## Efficiency Tips

The method becomes much faster if you scan for these before grinding through equations:

- **Symmetry** in geometry *and* loading (solve half, mirror the rest).
- **Zero-force members** (eliminate them up front).
- **Joints already rich with known forces** (fewer unknowns to chase).
- **Repeated member angles** that reuse the same sine and cosine values.

Always choose the next joint strategically. A joint with three unknowns is simply not ready, even if it sits right next to you.

## Common Errors

Most mistakes trace back to one of these:

- starting at a joint with more than two unknowns,
- forgetting to include support reactions as external joint forces,
- mixing up or mis-measuring member angles,
- misreading a negative sign (it means compression, not "wrong math"),
- losing the consistent tension assumption when moving between joints.

A useful self-check at every joint:

- Do I have at most two unknowns here?
- Are my components aligned with the correct axes?
- Does the final sign make physical sense given how the load pushes on this region?

---

## Where the Method Becomes Less Efficient

The method of joints is reliable, but it is not always the fastest tool. It loses its edge when:

- the target member lies deep inside a large truss (you must crawl through many joints to reach it),
- several joints near the support already contain many unknowns,
- or the design question concerns only one or two critical members.

In those situations the **method of sections** can reach a single interior member force in one cut and three equations, without solving every intermediate joint. The two methods are complementary: joints for a full member-by-member map, sections for a fast answer at one location.

This is also where engineering judgment begins. A member found in compression is not just a "negative force." It may become a buckling problem, a slenderness problem, or a connection-stiffness problem. The analytical sign is the start of design thinking, not the end of it. When one diagonal consistently appears in compression while another stays in tension, member selection becomes disciplined: slender ties, stocky struts, and matched connection details follow the actual force path instead of rough intuition.

## Engineering Applications

| Application | How the method of joints is used |
| --- | --- |
| Roof and floor trusses | Map every chord and web force to size ties vs buckling-prone struts |
| Pratt / Howe / Warren bridges | Identify which diagonals carry tension vs compression across the span |
| Transmission and antenna towers | Resolve wind-induced member forces joint by joint in a space-truss simplification |
| Crane booms and lifting frames | Confirm tension-only members never see reversal under different boom angles |
| Stadium and exhibition canopies | Locate zero-force and lightly loaded members for weight optimization |

> **Engineering in Action:** The same two equations, $\sum F_x = 0$ and $\sum F_y = 0$, that solved a hanging lamp now resolve every joint of a 100-meter roof truss. Only the bookkeeping grows; the physics is identical.

## Summary

### Key Equations

| Concept | Equation |
| --- | --- |
| Joint equilibrium (2D) | $\sum F_x = 0, \;\; \sum F_y = 0$ |
| Inclined member components | $F_x = F\cos\theta, \;\; F_y = F\sin\theta$ |
| Reaction from moment balance | $\sum M_A = 0$ to isolate one support |
| Sign convention | $+ \Rightarrow$ tension, $\; - \Rightarrow$ compression |

### Key Takeaways

- Treat each joint as a particle: two scalar equations, at most two unknown members.
- Solve support reactions first; they are known external forces at the early joints.
- Assume every unknown member is in tension, then let the sign report the truth.
- A negative answer means compression, with the magnitude already correct.
- Zero-force members and symmetry can dramatically shorten the work.
- Carry each member force with a consistent symbol from joint to joint.
