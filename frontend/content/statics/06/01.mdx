# 6.1 Introduction to Trusses

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this section, you will be able to:

1. **Define** a simple truss and identify its basic elements and the two-force-member model
2. **State** the assumptions that make classical truss analysis valid
3. **Apply** the method of joints to determine the force in each member
4. **Classify** each member as being in tension or compression from the sign of the result

## Opening: The Bridge That Pulled Itself Apart

In August 2007 the I-35W bridge over the Mississippi River in Minneapolis collapsed during rush hour, killing 13 people. The investigation traced the failure to undersized gusset plates, the steel connectors at the joints where truss members meet. The members themselves were sound; the *joints* could not carry the axial forces the members delivered to them. Knowing the force in every member, and whether it pulls (tension) or pushes (compression), is exactly what would have flagged those connections.

That is the promise of truss analysis: convert a complicated structure and its loads into a clean list of axial member forces, each one a single number with a sign. A positive number means the member is being stretched; a negative number means it is being squeezed and might buckle. Designers size every member and every joint from that list.

> **The Big Picture:** The method of joints in this section is the workhorse that produces that list of member forces, joint by joint, using nothing more than the particle equilibrium you already know.

---

A **truss** is a structure made from slender members connected at joints so that loads travel primarily as axial tension or compression. This makes trusses one of the most material-efficient structural systems in engineering. Bridges, roof systems, cranes, transmission towers, and aerospace frameworks all rely on the same basic idea:

**use geometry to carry load with as little material as possible**

## Why Trusses Are So Efficient

Trusses work well because they convert complicated external loading into simpler internal member forces.

- Long spans become possible without using a solid beam everywhere.
- Members can stay slender because they mostly carry axial load.
- Triangular layouts help preserve shape and resist collapse.

This is why a truss often feels "lighter than it looks" while still carrying substantial load.

## The Core Assumptions

Classical truss analysis depends on two simplifying assumptions:

1. **Loads are applied at the joints.**
2. **Members are connected by smooth pins.**

Under these assumptions, each member behaves as a **two-force member**. That means the internal force in the member acts along the member axis only.

So each member is typically in one of two states:

- **Tension:** the member is being pulled
- **Compression:** the member is being pushed

That is a big simplification. Instead of worrying about bending in every bar, we can focus on axial force paths.

## Why Triangles Matter

A four-sided frame can distort into a parallelogram unless extra bracing is added. A triangle is different. Once the side lengths are fixed, the shape is geometrically stable.

That is why trusses are built from repeated triangular units:

- triangles lock geometry
- diagonal members redirect load
- long spans can be stabilized without filling the whole region with material

This is also why truss thinking appears so often in nature.

## Bio-Inspired Link: Nature's Trusses

Human engineers did not invent lightweight lattice structures. Nature has been using them for millions of years.

The **Venus Flower Basket** sponge builds a silica skeleton with diagonal bracing and repeating lattice patterns that strongly resemble an engineered truss. The geometry helps the sponge survive deep-ocean currents while using remarkably little material.

That same logic appears in:

- trabecular bone
- leaf venation
- lattice-like exoskeleton regions

The lesson is consistent: efficient structures do not just use strong material, they use **well-routed force paths**.

## The Method of Joints

Once the overall support reactions are known, we can solve a truss joint by joint.

### Procedure

1. Draw a free-body diagram of the entire truss.
2. Compute the support reactions using global equilibrium.
3. Pick a joint with no more than two unknown member forces.
4. Apply:

$$
\Sigma F_x = 0, \qquad \Sigma F_y = 0
$$

5. Move to the next solvable joint.

A useful convention is to assume every unknown member force is in tension. If a solved value comes out negative, that member is actually in compression.

## Engineering Intuition

When you solve a truss, you are really asking:

- Which members are carrying the load?
- Which members are stabilizing geometry?
- Which members might buckle because they are in compression?

This is why truss analysis is not just algebra. It is an early form of structural design judgment.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Symmetric Triangular Truss Under a Peak Load

**Problem:** A simple triangle truss has a pin support at $A = (0, 0)$ and a roller at $B = (6, 0)$ m, with an apex joint $C = (3, 4)$ m. A vertical load $P = 12$ kN acts downward at $C$. Find the force in each member and classify it.

**Step 1: Draw the FBD of the whole truss and find reactions.** With a symmetric geometry and a centered load, vertical equilibrium and symmetry give

$$A_y = B_y = \frac{P}{2} = \frac{12}{2} = 6 \text{ kN}, \qquad A_x = 0$$

**Step 2: Geometry of member AC.** From $A(0,0)$ to $C(3,4)$ the length is $\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5$ m, so $\cos\theta = 3/5 = 0.6$ and $\sin\theta = 4/5 = 0.8$.

**Step 3: Joint A** (only two unknowns, $F_{AB}$ horizontal and $F_{AC}$ along $AC$). Assume both in tension (pulling away from the joint).

$$\sum F_y = 0: \quad F_{AC}(0.8) + 6 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; F_{AC} = \boxed{-7.5 \text{ kN (compression)}}$$

$$\sum F_x = 0: \quad F_{AB} + F_{AC}(0.6) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; F_{AB} = -(-7.5)(0.6) = \boxed{+4.5 \text{ kN (tension)}}$$

**Step 4: Interpret.** The diagonal $AC$ is in compression (the negative sign flipped our tension assumption), exactly what you expect for a member propping up a downward peak load. By symmetry $F_{BC} = -7.5$ kN (compression) as well, and the bottom chord $AB$ ties the two feet together in tension. The compression diagonals are the members a designer would check for buckling.

### Example 2: A Loaded Joint with a Diagonal at 45 Degrees

**Problem:** A wall bracket truss has a loaded joint $B = (3, 0)$ m connected by a horizontal member to $A = (0, 0)$ and by a diagonal member to $C = (0, 3)$ m on the wall above. A load of $5$ kN acts straight down at $B$. Find both member forces.

**Step 1: Geometry.** The diagonal $BC$ runs from $B(3,0)$ to $C(0,3)$, a direction $(-3, 3)$, i.e. $45^\circ$ above the horizontal, so $\cos 45^\circ = \sin 45^\circ = 0.7071$.

**Step 2: Joint B free body diagram.** Two unknowns ($F_{BA}$ horizontal, $F_{BC}$ along the diagonal) plus the 5 kN downward load. Assume tension.

$$\sum F_y = 0: \quad F_{BC}\sin 45^\circ - 5 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; F_{BC} = \frac{5}{0.7071} = \boxed{+7.07 \text{ kN (tension)}}$$

$$\sum F_x = 0: \quad -F_{BA} - F_{BC}\cos 45^\circ = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; F_{BA} = -(7.07)(0.7071) = \boxed{-5.0 \text{ kN (compression)}}$$

**Step 3: Interpret.** The diagonal pulls up and back toward the wall to carry the load (tension); the horizontal member is pushed (compression) because the diagonal's horizontal pull would otherwise drag joint $B$ toward the wall. Notice the diagonal force (7.07 kN) exceeds the 5 kN load: a member at a shallow angle to the load must carry more than the load itself, the same lesson seen with shallow cables in Chapter 1.

> **Workflow Reminder:** Start at a joint with at most two unknowns, assume every member is in tension, write $\sum F_x = 0$ and $\sum F_y = 0$, and read the sign. Negative means compression.

## Summary

### Key Equations

| Concept | Equation |
| --- | --- |
| Joint equilibrium (2D) | $\sum F_x = 0,\; \sum F_y = 0$ |
| Member along $(\Delta x, \Delta y)$, length $\ell$ | $\cos\theta = \Delta x/\ell,\; \sin\theta = \Delta y/\ell$ |
| Sign rule | $F > 0 \Rightarrow$ tension, $F < 0 \Rightarrow$ compression (when assumed tensile) |

### Key Takeaways

- A truss carries load through slender two-force members in pure tension or compression.
- The assumptions (loads at joints, frictionless pins) make each member a two-force member.
- Triangular units are geometrically stable; that is why trusses are built from triangles.
- The method of joints solves one joint at a time, starting where there are at most two unknowns.
- Assume tension everywhere; a negative result simply means the member is in compression.
