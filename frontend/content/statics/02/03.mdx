# Equilibrium of Rigid Bodies

> **A rigid body is in equilibrium only when both forces and moments balance.**

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this section, you should be able to:

- apply the planar equilibrium equations for rigid bodies,
- identify support reactions for common support types,
- choose useful moment points,
- and solve statically determinate beam and frame problems.

---

## From Particle Equilibrium to Rigid-Body Equilibrium

For a particle, force balance is enough:

$$
\sum \vec{F} = 0
$$

But a rigid body can rotate, so force balance alone is not sufficient. A rigid body in 2D must satisfy:

$$
\sum F_x = 0,\qquad \sum F_y = 0,\qquad \sum M_O = 0
$$

These three equations capture two ideas:

- the body must not translate in $x$ or $y$,
- and it must not have a net turning tendency.

If a problem has more than three unknown reactions in 2D, equilibrium alone will not be enough.

---

## Support Reactions

Before writing equations, identify what each support can provide.

- **Pin support:** horizontal and vertical reaction components
- **Roller support:** one reaction normal to the surface
- **Fixed support:** horizontal reaction, vertical reaction, and a reaction moment
- **Cable:** tension only, directed along the cable

This is why support identification matters so much. The support model determines the unknowns before the algebra even starts.

---

## A Reliable Problem-Solving Strategy

Use the same sequence every time:

1. Draw the free-body diagram.
2. Replace supports with reaction forces and moments.
3. Choose a coordinate system and sign convention.
4. Write the three equilibrium equations.
5. Pick a smart moment point to eliminate as many unknowns as possible.
6. Solve and check whether the reactions make physical sense.

Students often rush to equations before completing the free-body diagram. That usually causes sign mistakes or missing reactions.

---

## Choosing a Good Moment Point

Moment equations become easier when the chosen point removes unknowns.

Good choices often include:

- a pin support where two unknown reactions act,
- a point where several lines of action intersect,
- or a reference point that simplifies geometry.

This is not a shortcut. It is one of the main skills in rigid-body equilibrium.

---

## Worked Example 1: Simply Supported Beam

A 6 m beam is pinned at A and on a roller at B. A 1000 N load acts at midspan.

By symmetry, we expect the vertical reactions to match, but we still verify with equilibrium:

$$
\sum M_A = 0:\quad B_y(6) - 1000(3) = 0
$$

$$
B_y = 500 \text{ N}
$$

Then,

$$
\sum F_y = 0:\quad A_y + 500 - 1000 = 0
$$

$$
A_y = 500 \text{ N}
$$

Finally,

$$
\sum F_x = 0 \Rightarrow A_x = 0
$$

So the reactions are:

$$
\boxed{A_x = 0,\; A_y = 500\text{ N},\; B_y = 500\text{ N}}
$$

---

## Worked Example 2: Cantilever Beam

A 4 m cantilever carries a 2000 N downward load at the free end.

At the fixed support A, the unknowns are:

- $A_x$
- $A_y$
- $M_A$

From equilibrium:

$$
\sum F_x = 0 \Rightarrow A_x = 0
$$

$$
\sum F_y = 0 \Rightarrow A_y - 2000 = 0 \Rightarrow A_y = 2000 \text{ N}
$$

$$
\sum M_A = 0 \Rightarrow M_A - 2000(4) = 0
$$

$$
M_A = 8000 \text{ N m}
$$

This example shows why fixed supports create a reaction moment and why rigid-body equilibrium is richer than particle equilibrium.

## Common Failure Modes

When rigid-body equilibrium problems go wrong, it is usually because of one of these issues:

- forgetting the reaction moment at a fixed support,
- using the wrong sign convention for moments,
- missing a reaction component at a pin,
- or writing moment arms using nonperpendicular distances.

A useful self-check is to ask:

- Are all support reactions included?
- Did I use perpendicular distances in moment calculations?
- Does the final answer agree with the likely direction of support action?

---

## Design Intuition

Rigid-body equilibrium is the gateway to structural analysis. Beams, brackets, frames, machines, and support systems all rely on this logic.

If you can model the forces and moments correctly, then you can:

- determine support loads,
- size connections,
- locate dangerous load paths,
- and decide whether a system is statically determinate or needs additional methods.
