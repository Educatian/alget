# Couples and Equivalent Systems

> **A couple creates rotation without creating a net force.**

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this section, you should be able to:

- define a couple and compute its moment,
- explain why a couple is treated as a free vector,
- replace a force at one point with an equivalent force-couple system at another point,
- and judge whether two planar force systems are equivalent.

---

## Why Couples Matter

Many engineering systems do not simply push in one direction. They create a **turning tendency**. A steering wheel, a wrench, a hand tightening a valve, or a motor shaft receiving a torque all involve rotational effect.

A couple is the cleanest possible model of that effect. It contains two forces that:

- have equal magnitude,
- point in opposite directions,
- and are separated by a perpendicular distance.

Because the forces cancel as a vector pair, the net force is zero. Yet because they act at different lines of action, they still create rotation.

That is the central idea:

- **net force = 0**
- **net moment is not 0**

This is why couples are so useful in statics and machine design. They isolate pure rotational effect.

---

## Defining a Couple

For a planar couple,

$$
\boxed{M = Fd}
$$

where:

- $F$ is the magnitude of one of the forces,
- and $d$ is the perpendicular distance between the two lines of action.

The direction of the couple moment is clockwise or counterclockwise depending on the tendency of rotation.

Key observations:

- The two forces do **not** have to touch the same point.
- The forces must be parallel and opposite.
- The distance used must be the **perpendicular** separation, not an arbitrary straight-line distance.

If students use the wrong distance, the numerical answer may look plausible while the physics is wrong.

---

## Why a Couple Is a Free Vector

A moment about a specific point depends on which point you choose. A couple does not.

That is why we call a couple a **free vector**. Its effect on a rigid body is the same no matter where on the body we place it.

This gives engineers several practical freedoms:

- we can slide a couple to another point on the same rigid body,
- we can combine multiple couples algebraically,
- and we can replace one couple with another of equal magnitude and direction.

This property is extremely important when simplifying force systems. It lets us move between complicated loadings and cleaner equivalent models.

---

## Equivalent Systems

Two planar force systems are **equivalent** if they produce:

- the same resultant force,
- and the same resultant moment about any point.

That means equivalent systems create the same external mechanical effect on a rigid body.

This is the basis of a common operation in statics:

- move a force from point A to point B,
- then add a compensating couple so the system remains equivalent.

If a force of magnitude $F$ is moved by a perpendicular offset $d$, then the equivalent system at the new point is:

- the same force,
- plus a couple of magnitude $Fd$.

That is not a trick. It is a rigorous equivalence statement.

---

## How to Move a Force Correctly

When relocating a force, use this checklist:

1. Keep the **same magnitude**.
2. Keep the **same direction**.
3. Place it at the **new point**.
4. Add the required **couple** to preserve the original rotational effect.

Students often make one of two mistakes:

- they move the force but forget the couple,
- or they add the couple with the wrong sign.

The sign should always match the rotational tendency created by the offset between the original and new line of action.

---

## Worked Example 1: Computing a Couple

Two 80 N forces act in opposite directions and are separated by 0.6 m.

$$
M = Fd = 80(0.6) = 48 \text{ N m}
$$

So the couple moment magnitude is:

$$
\boxed{48 \text{ N m}}
$$

If the upper force points left and the lower force points right, the sense is counterclockwise. If the directions are reversed, the sense is clockwise.

---

## Worked Example 2: Moving a Force

Suppose a 200 N downward force acts 1.5 m to the right of point $O$. We want an equivalent system at $O$.

At $O$, the equivalent system is:

- a 200 N downward force at $O$,
- plus a couple of magnitude $200(1.5)=300$ N m.

The sign of the couple depends on the original tendency of the force about $O$. If the downward force to the right of $O$ tends to rotate clockwise, the added equivalent couple at $O$ must also be clockwise.

This lets the simplified model preserve both translation and rotation effects.

## Common Mistakes to Watch For

Before moving on, check that you are not doing any of these:

- using the straight-line distance instead of the perpendicular offset,
- assuming a couple has a net force,
- forgetting that the same force must remain after relocation,
- or confusing a point moment with a free couple.

Those errors usually produce incorrect signs, incorrect units, or a system that is not truly equivalent.

---

## Design Intuition

Couples appear everywhere in real engineering:

- a wrench applies a couple to a bolt,
- a steering input applies a couple to a wheel assembly,
- a motor supplies torque to a shaft,
- and distributed loads can often be reduced to force-couple systems for analysis.

This is why couples are not just a classroom abstraction. They are the mathematical language of rotational loading.
