# Couples and Equivalent Systems

> **A couple creates rotation without creating a net force.**

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this section, you should be able to:

1. **Define** a couple and compute its moment from the force and perpendicular separation,
2. **Explain** (and prove) why a couple is a free vector whose moment is independent of the reference point,
3. **Replace** a single force at one point with an equivalent force-couple system at another point,
4. **Reduce** a general planar loading to a single resultant force and a single resultant couple,
5. **Judge** whether two planar force systems are mechanically equivalent.

---

## Opening: The Tightening Wrench

Picture a mechanic torquing a wheel lug nut with a two-handed breaker bar. One hand pushes forward at the top of the handle while the other pulls backward at the bottom. The two hand forces are nearly equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, so they almost cancel: the mechanic does not lurch sideways and the bar does not fly off. Yet the bolt turns. That turning effect, produced by two equal and opposite forces that do not share a line of action, is a **couple**.

This is not a classroom curiosity. When a torque wrench is rated at 120 N m, that rating is a couple moment, and the manufacturer assumes it can be applied anywhere along the handle because the rotational effect on the bolt is the same regardless of where you grip. The independence of grip location is the defining property of a couple, and it is exactly what lets engineers slide, combine, and simplify rotational loads when they reduce a messy real loading to a clean analysis model.

> **The Big Picture:** Real machines and structures rarely load you with a single tidy force at a convenient point. The skill of replacing a scattered set of forces with one resultant force plus one resultant couple is what turns an intimidating loading diagram into two simple equations.

---

## Why Couples Matter

Many engineering systems do not simply push in one direction. They create a **turning tendency**. A steering wheel, a wrench, a hand tightening a valve, and a motor shaft receiving a torque all involve rotational effect.

A couple is the cleanest possible model of that effect. It contains two forces that:

- have equal magnitude,
- point in opposite directions,
- and are separated by a perpendicular distance.

Because the forces cancel as a vector pair, the net force is zero. Yet because they act along different lines of action, they still create rotation.

That is the central idea:

| Quantity | Value for a couple |
| --- | --- |
| Resultant force $\sum \vec{F}$ | $0$ |
| Resultant moment $\sum M$ | $Fd \neq 0$ |

This is why couples are so useful in statics and machine design. They isolate pure rotational effect with no leftover translation to account for.

---

## Defining a Couple

For a planar couple,

$$
\boxed{M = Fd}
$$

where:

- $F$ is the magnitude of one of the two forces,
- and $d$ is the **perpendicular** distance between the two lines of action.

The direction of the couple moment is clockwise or counterclockwise depending on the tendency of rotation. We will take counterclockwise as positive throughout this section.

Key observations:

- The two forces do **not** have to touch the same point.
- The forces must be parallel, equal, and opposite.
- The distance used must be the **perpendicular** separation, not an arbitrary straight-line distance between application points.

> **Critical Check:** If you use the slant distance between the two application points instead of the perpendicular distance between the two lines of action, your number will look reasonable but be wrong. Always drop a perpendicular.

---

## Why a Couple Is a Free Vector: A Short Proof

It is easy to assert that a couple has the same moment about every point. It is more convincing to prove it.

Consider two forces forming a couple: a force $\vec{F}$ acting at point $A$ and a force $-\vec{F}$ acting at point $B$. Choose any reference point $O$, and let $\vec{r}_A$ and $\vec{r}_B$ be the position vectors from $O$ to $A$ and $B$. The total moment about $O$ is

$$
\vec{M}_O = \vec{r}_A \times \vec{F} + \vec{r}_B \times (-\vec{F})
$$

$$
\vec{M}_O = (\vec{r}_A - \vec{r}_B) \times \vec{F}
$$

The vector $\vec{r}_A - \vec{r}_B$ points from $B$ to $A$. It does **not** contain $O$ at all. So the reference point $O$ has vanished from the result. Whatever point you pick, the moment is the same fixed vector

$$
\vec{M} = \vec{r}_{B/A} \times \vec{F},
$$

whose magnitude is $Fd$ with $d$ the perpendicular distance. Because $\vec{M}$ does not depend on $O$, a couple is a **free vector**: we may slide it anywhere on the rigid body, add couples algebraically, and replace one couple by another of equal magnitude and sense.

## Equivalent Systems

Two planar force systems are **equivalent** if they produce:

- the same resultant force $\vec{R} = \sum \vec{F}$,
- and the same resultant moment $\sum M$ about any one point (and therefore about every point).

Equivalent systems create the same external mechanical effect on a rigid body: the same support reactions, the same tendency to translate, and the same tendency to rotate. They may produce different internal stresses, which is why this equivalence is a tool for support and motion analysis, not for stress analysis.

This is the basis of the most common operation in statics:

- move a force from point $A$ to a parallel line through point $B$,
- then add a compensating couple so the system remains equivalent.

If a force of magnitude $F$ is moved by a perpendicular offset $d$, the equivalent system at the new point is the same force plus a couple of magnitude $Fd$. That is not a trick; it is the direct consequence of the moment definition.

---

## How to Move a Force Correctly

When relocating a force, use this checklist:

1. Keep the **same magnitude**.
2. Keep the **same direction**.
3. Place it at the **new point**.
4. Add the required **couple** $M = Fd$ to preserve the original moment about the new point.

Students often make one of two mistakes:

- they move the force but forget the couple,
- or they add the couple with the wrong sign.

The sign should always match the rotational tendency the original force had about the new point. If the original force tended to spin the body clockwise about $B$, the added couple at $B$ must be clockwise.

## Worked Example 2.1: Computing a Couple

**Problem:** Two parallel 80 N forces act in opposite directions and are separated by a perpendicular distance of 0.6 m. The upper force points left and the lower force points right. Find the couple moment and its sense.

**Step 1: Identify the perpendicular separation.** Here $F = 80$ N and $d = 0.6$ m, already perpendicular.

**Step 2: Apply the couple definition.**

$$
M = Fd = (80)(0.6)
$$

$$
\boxed{M = 48 \text{ N m}}
$$

**Step 3: Determine the sense.** Upper force to the left, lower force to the right, gives a counterclockwise turning tendency, so the couple is $+48$ N m. If both directions were reversed, the result would be $48$ N m clockwise. The magnitude is unchanged; only the sign flips.

---

## Worked Example 2.2: Moving a Force to a New Point

**Problem:** A 200 N force points straight down and acts along a line 1.5 m to the right of point $O$. Replace it with an equivalent force-couple system at $O$.

**Step 1: Draw the situation.** Sketch $O$ at the origin. The 200 N downward force acts at $x = +1.5$ m. The position vector from $O$ to the point of application is $\vec{r} = 1.5\,\hat{i}$ m, and the force is $\vec{F} = -200\,\hat{j}$ N.

**Step 2: Keep the force.** The equivalent system carries the same 200 N downward force, now placed at $O$.

**Step 3: Compute the compensating couple as the original moment about $O$.**

$$
\vec{M}_O = \vec{r} \times \vec{F} = (1.5\,\hat{i}) \times (-200\,\hat{j}) = -300\,\hat{k} \text{ N m}
$$

The $-\hat{k}$ direction is into the page, which is the clockwise sense. So

$$
\boxed{M_O = 300 \text{ N m clockwise}}
$$

**Step 4: State the equivalent system.** A 200 N downward force at $O$ together with a 300 N m clockwise couple. This reproduces both the translation tendency (200 N down) and the rotation tendency (300 N m clockwise) of the original off-center force.

## Worked Example 2.3: Reducing Several Forces to a Resultant Force-Couple System

**Problem:** On a horizontal beam, three vertical loads act downward: 100 N at $x = 0$, 150 N at $x = 2$ m, and 250 N at $x = 5$ m, measured from the left support $O$. Reduce this system to a single resultant force and a single couple at $O$, then find the single location at which the resultant force alone (no couple) is equivalent.

**Step 1: Resultant force.** Sum the vertical forces (down is negative).

$$
R = -(100 + 150 + 250) = -500 \text{ N}, \quad \text{so } \boxed{R = 500 \text{ N downward}}
$$

**Step 2: Resultant couple about $O$.** Take counterclockwise as positive. Each downward force at a positive $x$ produces a clockwise (negative) moment about $O$.

$$
M_O = -[(100)(0) + (150)(2) + (250)(5)] = -(0 + 300 + 1250)
$$

$$
\boxed{M_O = 1550 \text{ N m clockwise}}
$$

**Step 3: Locate the single equivalent resultant.** The resultant force alone is equivalent if placed at $\bar{x}$ such that it produces the same moment about $O$:

$$
R\,\bar{x} = |M_O| \implies \bar{x} = \frac{1550}{500}
$$

$$
\boxed{\bar{x} = 3.1 \text{ m to the right of } O}
$$

A 500 N downward force placed 3.1 m from $O$ is mechanically equivalent to the three original loads. This $\bar{x}$ is the line of action of the resultant, the same idea used later to locate the centroid of a distributed load.

## Common Mistakes to Watch For

Before moving on, check that you are not doing any of these:

- using the straight-line distance between application points instead of the perpendicular offset between lines of action,
- assuming a couple has a net force (it never does),
- forgetting that the same force must remain after relocation,
- or confusing a point moment (depends on the reference) with a free couple (does not).

Those errors usually produce incorrect signs, incorrect units, or a system that is not truly equivalent.

## Engineering Applications

| Application | Why Couples and Equivalent Systems Matter |
| --- | --- |
| Bolted joints and torque wrenches | The applied torque is a couple; its rating is independent of grip location along the handle |
| Steering and control wheels | Driver input is modeled as a pure couple on the wheel hub |
| Motor and drive shafts | Transmitted torque is a couple carried along the shaft axis |
| Eccentric column loads | An off-center axial load is reduced to a centered force plus a bending couple |
| Distributed pressure and weight | A spread load is replaced by a single resultant force at its centroid for support analysis |

> **Engineering in Action:** When a crane lifts a load whose cable does not pass through the boom centerline, designers replace the offset cable force with an equivalent force at the boom axis plus a couple. The couple is exactly the bending moment the boom must be sized to resist.

## Summary

### Key Equations

| Concept | Equation |
| --- | --- |
| Couple moment (planar) | $M = Fd$ |
| Couple as a cross product | $\vec{M} = \vec{r}_{B/A} \times \vec{F}$ |
| Moment of a couple (point-independent) | $\vec{M}_O = (\vec{r}_A - \vec{r}_B)\times\vec{F}$ |
| Equivalent resultant force | $\vec{R} = \sum \vec{F}$ |
| Equivalent resultant moment | $M_O = \sum (\text{force} \times \text{lever arm})$ |
| Line of action of resultant | $\bar{x} = M_O / R$ |

### Key Takeaways

- A couple is two equal, opposite, parallel forces; it has zero net force but a nonzero moment $M = Fd$.
- A couple is a free vector: its moment is the same about every point, so it can be moved anywhere on a rigid body.
- To move a force to a new point, keep the same force and add a couple equal to its original moment about that point.
- Two systems are equivalent when they share the same resultant force and the same resultant moment about any point.
- Always use the perpendicular distance, and always track the sign of the rotational sense.
