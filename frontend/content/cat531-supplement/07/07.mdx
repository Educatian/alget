# Professional Learning Network

![Professional Learning Network textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/07-07-professional-learning-network.png)

## Why This Section Matters

A professional learning network, the colleagues, online groups, and shared resources you draw on, is a source, not an authority. The distinction matters because networks circulate popularity, and popularity is not evidence. A tool recommended by 300 teachers in a Facebook group has 300 recommendations and possibly zero studies; the count measures reach, not effectiveness, and certainly not fit with your placement. Treating the network as a source means asking what backs each claim and whether the recommender's context resembles yours, before adopting anything.

This section treats the professional learning network as something you vet and contribute to, not something you follow. The skill is source evaluation under social pressure: separating signal from popularity, checking context fit, and contributing your own findings in a form colleagues can actually judge.

## Learning Targets

- Distinguish popularity (how many recommend a tool) from evidence (what backs the recommendation).
- Check whether a recommender's context (grade, subject, device ratio) resembles your placement before adopting.
- Verify AI-supplied citations and treat unlocatable sources as likely fabricated.
- Contribute findings back in a form colleagues can judge for fit, including where a strategy did not work.

## Opening Case

"300 teachers use it" is a social proof signal, and social proof is a heuristic that bypasses evaluation. It feels like evidence because agreement is comfortable, but the number tells you only that the tool spread, not that it worked, and not that it works in a room like yours. A tool can be popular because it is genuinely good, or because it is free, well-marketed, or fun regardless of learning outcomes.

The vetting move is to ask what the 300 recommendations are made of. Do the recommenders teach your grade and subject? What evidence backs their claims beyond enthusiasm? What does the tool do with student data? A recommendation from a one-to-one device school says little about your shared-cart placement. The network surfaced the tool; your job is to test it against your context before you trust it.

## Core Concept

Source evaluation in a network rests on three filters: evidence, context, and provenance. The **evidence filter** asks what supports a claim beyond the fact that someone made it; a strategy described with outcomes and conditions is stronger than one described with enthusiasm. The **context filter** asks whether the recommender's situation resembles yours; a glowing report from a 1-to-1 school is a different claim in a shared-cart placement, and the gap must be tested, not assumed away. The **provenance filter** asks where a cited fact actually comes from; in an age of AI-generated summaries, a citation that cannot be located is more likely fabricated than obscure.

These filters convert the network from an authority you defer to into a source you interrogate. The same skills make you a better contributor: when you post back, you supply the evidence, the context, and the locatable sources that let colleagues run the same three filters on your claim.

Three lenses sharpen the filters:

- **Critical edtech studies** asks whose interests a popular tool serves; virality often tracks marketing budget or novelty rather than learning, and the lens keeps you from mistaking reach for quality.
- **AI and education datafication** is the direct source of the provenance problem: AI summaries of "best practices" routinely cite studies that do not exist, so any AI-supplied citation must be located before it is trusted.

## Worked Example

Compare two responses to the same viral recommendation. The popularity-as-proof version skips evaluation:

> 300 teachers use it, so it must be effective for my placement.

This treats reach as evidence and ignores context entirely. The vetted version runs the filters:

> I checked what evidence backs the claims, whether the recommenders teach my grade and subject, and what the tool does with student data. A colleague's glowing strategy came from a 1-to-1 device school; my placement shares one cart, so I will test the strategy under my constraint before adopting it. An AI summary of best practices cited three studies; none appeared when I searched for them, so I treated the citations as likely fabricated and used only sources I could locate.

The second version surfaces a context gap (one-to-one versus shared cart) and a provenance failure (three uncited "studies") that the popularity version would have carried straight into the classroom. The contribution that follows posts what was tried, where it worked and where it did not, and a caution about the shared-device constraint, so colleagues can judge fit rather than just reshare.

## Decision Rule

Run evidence, context, and provenance before you adopt, and contribute back in a form that lets others run the same filters. The order is: ask what backs the claim, check whether the recommender's context matches yours, locate every cited source, then post your own findings with their conditions attached. Lena follows it: she tests evidence and context, refuses the unlocatable citations, and contributes a caution rather than a reshare.

Before you adopt from a network, three checks should pass:

1. **Evidence check:** Beyond how many people recommend it, what actually supports the claim that this tool helps learning?
2. **Context check:** Does the recommender's grade, subject, and device reality resemble yours, and have you tested the gap if it does not?
3. **Provenance check:** Did you locate every cited source, and did you discard the AI-supplied citations you could not find?

If you adopted on popularity alone, you followed the network rather than vetting it.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is treating a recommendation count as an effectiveness measure; reach and quality are different things, and virality often tracks marketing rather than learning. The second is assuming a strategy that worked elsewhere will work in your context; a one-to-one success can be a shared-cart failure, and the gap must be tested. The third is trusting AI-supplied citations without locating them; when a summary cites three studies that no search can find, the honest conclusion is fabrication, not obscurity, and only locatable sources should survive into your reasoning.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the three filters to a different network claim. A colleague in a teacher Slack posts that a particular AI flashcard app "raised our test scores 20 percent." Run the filters. Evidence: ask what the 20 percent is measured against, over what period, and whether anything else changed at the same time; a number with no comparison group is a story, not a finding. Context: this colleague teaches an honors section with one-to-one devices, while your placement is a mixed-level class sharing a cart, so the result may not transfer. Provenance: if the colleague attributes the claim to "research," ask for the study and try to locate it; if it traces back to the vendor's own marketing page, weight it accordingly.

The vetted conclusion is not "ignore it" but "test it small, under my conditions, and watch for the access barrier the shared cart creates." Then you contribute: you post your own result with its conditions, including if it underperformed, so the next teacher inherits a vettable claim rather than another unconditioned success story. The walkthrough is the same structure every time, applied to a new claim.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read a network recommendation, keep a two-column note: **what is claimed** on the left, **what backs it** on the right. A recommendation with a full left column and an empty right column is popularity dressed as evidence. The presence of locatable sources, matched context, and stated conditions on the right is the signal that a claim is worth acting on.

A strong reader of a network claim can answer four questions:

1. What evidence beyond popularity supports this recommendation?
2. Does the recommender's context resemble mine, and have I tested the gap?
3. Are the cited sources locatable, or are they likely AI-fabricated?
4. If I contributed this back, would a colleague have enough to judge fit, including where it failed?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A network contribution is strong when a colleague reading it could decide whether the strategy fits their own room without contacting you. That requires stating the context you tried it in, the evidence of what happened, the conditions under which it worked, and honestly where it did not. Polish is the last layer; a contribution that reads as an enthusiastic endorsement but omits its context and its failures has added to the noise the three filters exist to cut through.

A useful closing line sounds like this: "I am posting this with its conditions because a recommendation without context just spreads; it worked in my shared-cart room only after I pre-loaded the devices, and it did not help my multilingual learners. I dropped the AI-cited studies I could not locate, because an unverifiable source is not support."

## Transfer Case

Take the same vetting discipline to a higher-stakes channel: a district professional-development session where an outside vendor presents a tool as research-backed to a room of teachers who will be expected to adopt it. The social pressure is now institutional, not just a group thread. The three filters still apply, but running them means asking the vendor, in a room, for the studies, the test population, and the data-handling policy, which takes more standing than questioning a Facebook post. If you can run evidence, context, and provenance against an authoritative presenter, you understand the network skill as professional judgment rather than online skepticism.
