## Why This Section Matters

When a lesson underperforms, two unhelpful instincts compete: defend the plan ("the design is sound, the students just didn't do their part") or scrap it entirely ("flipped learning doesn't work"). Both skip the diagnostic step that distinguishes a teacher who learns from one who guesses. Field evidence rarely condemns a whole plan; it usually points at one component. The flipped lesson did not fail because flipping is wrong; it failed because the at-home prep step assumed home access that 17 of 26 students did not have. Revision is the work of isolating the broken component and changing it while keeping what worked.

This section treats revision as evidence-driven diagnosis. The skill is reading field data to locate the specific cause, then making the minimal change that fixes it, with follow-up data to confirm the fix.

## Learning Targets

- Read field evidence to isolate which component of a plan failed, rather than defending or scrapping the whole.
- Distinguish a design flaw from a context mismatch (laziness versus an access barrier).
- Document a bounded AI/software support move, including an AI reframing you rejected because it ignored the real cause.
- Show evidence-based revision: original plan, prompting data, targeted change, and follow-up data.

## Opening Case

A plan is not one decision; it is a chain of them. A flipped lesson chains an at-home video to an in-class activity. When the lesson disappoints, the question is which link broke, and the data usually answers it precisely. If the in-class AI-coached set worked for the nine students who watched the video but the other seventeen never watched, the broken link is the at-home prep, not the in-class design.

The mistake is reasoning about the whole when the data is about a part. "The flipped model failed" throws away a component that worked for everyone who reached it. The evidence points narrower than the conclusion you are tempted to draw, and revision means following the evidence to the actual link, not the whole chain.

## Core Concept

Evidence-based revision is component diagnosis. You decompose the plan into its sequential links, attach the field data to each link, and find the one where the data shows the failure. Then you ask a second question the diagnosis depends on: is this link broken by a design flaw (the activity itself is poorly built) or by a context mismatch (the activity assumes a resource the students do not have)? The answer determines the fix. A design flaw needs a redesigned activity; a context mismatch needs the same activity moved to where the resource exists.

In the flipped case, the failing link is at-home prep, and the cause is a context mismatch: an exit question shows most non-watchers cited unreliable home time, not unwillingness. That diagnosis points to a precise fix, move the video to a seven-minute in-class viewing, which keeps the AI-coached set that already worked and relocates only the link that broke. The follow-up data (24 of 26 reaching the set, up from 9) confirms the diagnosis was right.

Three lenses sharpen the diagnosis:

- **Critical edtech studies** (Selwyn, 2016) distinguishes "students didn't do it" from "students couldn't do it." Reading non-completion as laziness blames the learner; reading it as an access barrier locates the fixable cause in the design.
- **[AI and education datafication](https://doi.org/10.1080/17439884.2020.1686017)** warns that AI revision tools propose engagement gimmicks (a points system) that change the surface without touching the diagnosed cause. A fix that ignores the cause is not a fix.
- **[TPACK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_pedagogical_content_knowledge)** asks whether the technology component or the pedagogical sequence is the failing link, since the same low-completion symptom can come from either.

> **Recent evidence (the homework gap):** The access barrier in this case is a documented national pattern, not a one-classroom anomaly. Roughly 15% of U.S. households with school-age children lack a high-speed home connection; about 12% of teens report being unable to complete homework because they lack reliable computer or internet access, and 22% sometimes do homework on a cellphone. The gap is sharply income-stratified: about 44% of households earning under $30,000 lack high-speed home internet versus about 6% of households earning over $75,000 ([NCES, Children's Internet Access at Home](https://nces.ed.gov/programs/coe/indicator/cch/home-internet-access); [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2020/03/16/as-schools-close-due-to-the-coronavirus-some-u-s-students-face-a-digital-homework-gap/)). Non-completion is frequently "couldn't do it" (access), not "didn't do it" (effort), which is exactly the context mismatch this section teaches you to diagnose.

A lens has done its work when it changed which component you revised, not just how you described the failure.

## Worked Example

Compare two responses to the same disappointing result. The defend-on-principle version protects the whole plan:

> Keep the flipped design; reassign the video and warn students to watch it.

This treats the symptom as a discipline problem and changes nothing structural. The diagnostic version isolates the link:

> 17 of 26 students did not watch at home, and an exit question showed most cited unreliable home time, not unwillingness. The in-class AI coaching worked for the 9 who watched. So the failing link is at-home prep, not the design. Move the video to a 7-minute in-class viewing before the AI-coached set.

The second version reads the data to a component, names the cause (access, not laziness), and changes only the broken link. The follow-up confirms it: 24 of 26 reached the problem set, versus 9 before. The AI tool's suggestion to add a gamified points system was rejected, because points address motivation and the diagnosed cause was access.

Model the broken link directly. The reach to the in-class AI-coached set is the students who watched at home, plus, if you relocate the prep into class, the rest of the class who can then watch. Set the prep location to 0 (at home) or 1 (moved in-class) and watch reach climb as you lower home access.

### Annotated Judgment Call: Revising a Flipped Lesson After the Data

Aisha planned a flipped lesson: students watch a video at home, then use class time for an AI-coached problem set. After teaching it once, only 9 of 26 students had watched the video.

1. **Before (plan defended on principle):** "Keep the flipped design; reassign the video and warn students to watch it."
2. **Field evidence read:** 17 of 26 did not watch at home. An exit question showed most cited unreliable home time, an access barrier, not laziness.
3. **Cause isolated:** The in-class AI coaching worked for the 9 who had watched. The failing part was the at-home prep, so Aisha revises the cause rather than scrapping the whole plan.
4. **AI suggestion held to the evidence:** An AI tool proposed a gamified points system but never addressed home access. She rejected that reframing and kept her focus on the access barrier.
5. **After (evidence-based revision):** Move the video to a 7-minute in-class viewing before the AI-coached set. Follow-up data: 24 of 26 students reached the problem set, versus 9 before.

The record shows the original plan, the prompting data, the change, and the follow-up data, not a hunch that it "felt better."

Now run the diagnosis under pressure. Each turn either isolates the broken link or overcorrects.

## Decision Rule

Locate the failing component before you change anything, and confirm the cause is a flaw or a mismatch before you choose the fix. The order is: read the data to the link, classify the cause, change only that link, then gather follow-up data; this sequencing is [teacher professional judgment](https://doi.org/10.1177/002205741319300304) applied as diagnosis rather than defense. Aisha follows it exactly; she does not touch the in-class set that worked, classifies the prep failure as access rather than effort, relocates only the video, and measures the result.

Before you call a revision evidence-based, three checks should pass:

1. **Component check:** Does your data point to a specific link in the plan, or are you revising the whole because the result disappointed?
2. **Cause check:** Did you classify the failure as a design flaw or a context mismatch, and does your fix match that classification?
3. **Confirmation check:** What follow-up data shows the change worked, and what AI-suggested fix did you reject because it ignored the diagnosed cause?

A revision with no follow-up data is a hope, not an evidence-based change.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is defending the plan and blaming the students; "they should have watched" reads an access barrier as a discipline problem and fixes nothing. The second is overcorrecting by scrapping a whole plan when one link failed, which discards the components that worked. The third is accepting an AI revision that addresses the wrong cause; when a tool proposes gamification for a problem the data shows is about home access, adopting it changes the surface and leaves the failure intact.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply component diagnosis to a different disappointment. You ran a station rotation where one station used an AI research assistant, and the lesson felt chaotic. The temptation is "stations don't work for this class." Decompose instead. The data: the two non-tech stations ran smoothly, but the AI-assistant station had a line of confused students because the login process took four minutes per student and ate the rotation time.

The failing link is not the rotation structure; it is the login bottleneck at one station, and the cause is a design flaw (a slow setup), not a context mismatch. The fix is narrow: pre-log the devices before class or assign a station helper to manage logins, leaving the rotation intact. Follow-up data would be whether the AI station now completes within the rotation window. If an AI tool suggested abandoning stations for whole-class instruction, you reject it, because it discards two stations that worked to solve a problem confined to one. The walkthrough repeats: find the link, classify the cause, fix only that link, measure again.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read a revision, keep a two-column note: **what the data shows about each link** on the left, **what changed** on the right. A revision that changed more than the data implicated is overcorrecting; a revision that changed nothing structural despite clear data is defending. The match between the diagnosed link and the change made is the mark of evidence-based revision.

A strong reader of a revision can answer four questions:

1. Which specific component does the field data implicate, and which components does it exonerate?
2. Was the failure a design flaw or a context mismatch, and does the fix match?
3. What follow-up data confirms the change worked, with what before-and-after numbers?
4. What AI-suggested reframing was rejected because it addressed the wrong cause?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A revision artifact is strong when a reader can see the original plan, the data that prompted the change, the single component changed, and the follow-up data that confirmed it. That requires before-and-after numbers, not a claim that the lesson "felt better." Polished narration is the last layer; a revision that reads persuasively but cannot point to the implicated component or show follow-up data has documented a hunch, not a diagnosis.

A useful closing line sounds like this: "I moved the video into class because the data showed home access, not effort, was the failing link, and follow-up completion rose from 9 to 24 of 26. I rejected the gamified points suggestion, because it fixed a motivation problem the data never found."

## Transfer Case

Take the same diagnostic discipline to a success you want to scale. A lesson worked well in your fourth-period class, and you plan to run it identically in second period. Component diagnosis still applies, but now you are predicting which links depend on conditions that differ between the two classes (second period has three more multilingual learners and meets right after lunch). Rather than copying the plan whole, you identify which components rest on conditions that change and pre-adjust them. If you can diagnose a plan's components before they fail in a new context, not only after, you understand revision as forward-looking judgment rather than damage repair.
