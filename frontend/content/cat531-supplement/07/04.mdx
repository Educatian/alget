## Why This Section Matters

A generic risk statement, "this tool might not work for every student," protects no one, because it names no student, no harm, and no safeguard. An equity audit replaces that vagueness with a context-specific risk register: for this tool, in this placement, with these students, here is who is most likely to be harmed, how, and what we will do about it. The shift from generic to specific is the whole skill. "Might not be perfect" cannot be acted on; "the feedback model flags multilingual phrasing as errors, and 11 of my 27 students are multilingual" can.

This section treats the equity-oriented evaluation matrix as a tool for converting abstract caution into context-bound, monitorable risks. A risk you cannot tie to a named group, a mechanism, and a check is not yet an audit finding.

## Learning Targets

- Convert generic risk statements into context-specific risks tied to a named group, a mechanism, and your actual placement constraints.
- Treat vendor fairness claims as unverified hypotheses and write the questions they leave open.
- Document a bounded AI/software support move, replacing AI-generated generic risks with placement-specific ones.
- Produce an evaluation matrix that pairs each risk with an affected group, a safeguard, and a monitoring check.

## Opening Case

Vendors and AI assistants both produce risk language that sounds responsible and commits to nothing. "Bias-tested and fair for all learners" is a marketing claim, not evidence; it does not say which groups were tested, on what data, or whether your students resemble them. Accepting it ends the audit before it starts.

The audit begins when you treat that claim as a hypothesis and ask the questions it dodges. A fairness claim with no test population named is an open question, not a closed case. The matrix exists to hold those open questions next to your real roster, so the decision to adopt rests on what you can verify about your students rather than what the vendor asserts about everyone.

## Core Concept

An equity audit is disaggregation applied to risk. A whole-class average hides the students a tool fails; the audit's job is to break the class into the groups whose experience of the tool differs and ask what happens to each. The unit of analysis is never "the students"; it is "the 11 multilingual learners," "the 3 students using read-aloud," "the 9 without reliable home access."

Each finding has three required parts. The **affected group** names who, specifically and by count. The **mechanism** names how the tool produces the harm (a model trained on standard-English essays flags non-standard phrasing). The **safeguard** names what you will do (teacher review of flagged multilingual work) and the **monitoring check** names when you will confirm it worked (two weeks after rollout). A risk missing any part is incomplete.

Three lenses drive the disaggregation:

- **[Critical edtech studies](https://doi.org/10.4067/s0718-50492025000100248)** (Selwyn) supplies the core move: ask whose interests the tool was built around and which students sit outside that default. A model trained mostly on standard English has a built-in default that your multilingual students fall outside of.
- **[AI and education datafication](https://doi.org/10.1080/17439884.2020.1686017)** asks what the tool does with the data it gathers, and whether its training data represented your students at all. Underrepresentation in training is a mechanism, not a footnote.
- **[TPACK](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9620.2006.00684.x)** keeps the audit tethered to learning: a tool that fails a subgroup on the actual content goal is failing, regardless of how engaging it is for the majority.

A lens has done its work when it produced a specific risk row, not a general worry.

> **Recent evidence:** Both of this section's anchor risks are documented findings, not hypotheticals. On facial recognition, Buolamwini & Gebru's [Gender Shades](http://proceedings.mlr.press/v81/buolamwini18a/buolamwini18a.pdf) study (2018) found commercial face-analysis error rates up to 34.7% for darker-skinned women versus 0.8% for lighter-skinned men, and NIST's [FRVT Part 3: Demographic Effects](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2019/NIST.IR.8280.pdf) report (2019) found demographic differentials in the majority of algorithms tested, with elevated false positives for African American and Asian faces in one-to-many matching. On essay scoring, [Wilson & Huang (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.asw.2024.100815) found evidence of bias in automated essay scores for English language learners.

## Worked Example

Compare a generic risk to an audited one. The generic version is unactionable:

> The tool might not be perfect for every student.

It names no group, no mechanism, no safeguard. The audited version is a usable finding:

> The feedback model was trained mostly on standard-English essays. Of my 27 students, 11 are multilingual learners whose phrasing the tool may flag as errors. Safeguard: teacher review of all flagged multilingual phrasing before students see comments. Check: compare flag rates for multilingual and monolingual writers two weeks after rollout.

The second version can be acted on, assigned, and verified. It also handles the vendor's "fair for all learners" claim correctly by logging it as unverified and recording the question it leaves open: which groups were tested, on what data, and were multilingual learners represented.

Set side by side, the structure becomes clear:

| Element | Generic risk | Audited risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Affected group | "every student" | 11 multilingual learners (of 27) |
| Mechanism | none stated | model trained mostly on standard-English essays |
| Safeguard | none | teacher review of flagged multilingual phrasing |
| Monitoring check | none | compare flag rates by group at two weeks |

### Annotated Judgment Call: Auditing an AI Writing-Feedback Tool

Sofia audits an AI writing-feedback tool before her placement adopts it for 27 students.

1. **Before (generic risk):** "The tool might not be perfect for every student."
2. **Context-specific risk:** "The feedback model was trained mostly on standard-English essays. Of my 27 students, 11 are multilingual learners whose phrasing the tool may flag as errors."
3. **Vendor claim handled:** The vendor page says the tool is "bias-tested and fair for all learners." Sofia logs this as an unverified claim and writes the question it leaves open: which groups were tested, on what data, and were multilingual learners represented.
4. **AI output grounded:** An AI assistant generated a risk column that applied to any school. She replaced each generic line with a risk tied to her actual students, shared device cart, and district data policy.
5. **After (recommendation with safeguards):** Adopt with safeguards. Each risk is paired with the affected group, a safeguard (teacher review of flagged multilingual phrasing), and a monitoring check two weeks after rollout.

A colleague can now trust the recommendation because every risk names who is affected and how it will be watched.

For each risk, name the group before the harm and the safeguard before the recommendation. A recommendation to adopt is only as good as the safeguards attached to its risks. Sofia's audit follows the rule: she does not write "adopt" until each risk carries an affected group, a safeguard, and a check, which is why "adopt with safeguards" is a defensible recommendation rather than a hopeful one.

Before you submit an audit, three checks should pass:

1. **Specificity check:** Does every risk name an actual group by count, a mechanism, and a placement constraint, or do some rows apply to any school anywhere?
2. **Claim check:** Did you treat each vendor fairness claim as a hypothesis and record the question it leaves open, rather than accepting it as evidence?
3. **Monitoring check:** Does every risk you accepted carry a safeguard and a dated check, so adoption is conditional and watched, not final?

If any risk lacks a group, a mechanism, or a check, the audit is not yet actionable.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is treating a vendor's fairness claim as a finding; it is an advertisement until you know the test population. The second is leaving risks generic enough to apply to any school, which feels thorough but commits to nothing and protects no specific student. The third is accepting an AI-generated risk column wholesale; these read comprehensive but float free of your roster, and the audit only becomes real when each line is rewritten against your actual students, device cart, and data policy.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the matrix to a different tool. Your placement is considering an AI attendance system that uses facial recognition to log entry. The generic risk ("it might misidentify some students") is useless. Disaggregate.

Affected group: facial-recognition error rates are documented to be higher for darker-skinned students, and your placement's roster is majority Black and Latino. Mechanism: the model's training data underrepresented these groups, so misidentification is structurally more likely for them. Safeguard: a manual roster check whenever the system fails to match, and no consequence (such as a tardy mark) triggered by the system alone. Monitoring check: review misidentification logs by student after two weeks and compare rates across groups. The recommendation that follows is not a thumbs-up; it is "adopt only with the manual override and the disparity check, and reconsider if the two-week logs show a gap." The walkthrough repeats the structure: name the group, name the mechanism, attach a safeguard, schedule the check.

> **Legal floor:** Biometric attendance data on minors is not only a fairness question; it implicates FERPA (education records) and, for students under 13, COPPA. As of 2026, the FTC's [amended COPPA rule](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2025/05/childrens-online-privacy-in-2025-the-amended-coppa-rule) (finalized January 2025, with full compliance required by April 22, 2026) explicitly classifies biometric identifiers, including facial templates, as covered personal information requiring verifiable parental consent. The audit's monitoring check therefore also confirms legal basis and consent, not only error-rate disparity.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read an audit, keep a two-column note: **named group and mechanism** on the left, **safeguard and check** on the right. A row with the left side empty is a slogan; a row with the right side empty is an alarm with no response. A strong audit has both columns filled for every risk it raises.

A strong reader of an equity audit can answer four questions:

1. For each risk, which actual group is affected and through what mechanism?
2. Which vendor claims were accepted as evidence, and which were correctly logged as open questions?
3. What safeguard and dated check make the recommendation conditional rather than blind?
4. What would change in the matrix if this exact tool were adopted in a placement with a different student population?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

An equity audit is strong when a colleague could adopt the tool using only your matrix and know exactly who to watch and when. That requires every risk to carry its group, mechanism, safeguard, and check, and every vendor claim to be marked verified or open. Clean tables are the last layer; a matrix that looks rigorous but contains risks that could describe any school has skipped the specificity that makes it an audit rather than a checklist.

A useful closing line sounds like this: "I recommend adopting with safeguards because each risk names a real group and a check, not a general caution. I rejected the AI-generated risk column, because risks that fit any school protect none of mine."

## Transfer Case

Take the same audit to a placement with the opposite profile: a tool that works well for multilingual learners but was never tested on students with visual impairments, in a class that includes two students using screen readers. The disaggregation still drives the audit, but the affected group, the mechanism (incompatibility with screen-reader navigation), and the safeguard (an accessible alternate path) all change. If you can rebuild the matrix around a different vulnerable group rather than reusing the multilingual rows, you understand equity auditing as a method rather than a single finding.
