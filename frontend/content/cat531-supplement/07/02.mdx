# Mentor Teacher Conversation Guides

![Mentor Teacher Conversation Guides textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/07-02-mentor-teacher-conversation-guides.png)

## Why This Section Matters

A mentor teacher holds something you do not: standing, history with the students, and accountability if a tool goes wrong. When you want to try a technology the mentor has restricted, the conversation is not a debate to win; it is a negotiation between two people with different information and unequal authority. A conversation guide is the artifact that turns that negotiation from a complaint into a professional inquiry. It records what you asked, what concern the mentor actually held, what you agreed to, and what you ruled out together.

This section treats the guide as a map of a classroom value conflict and the decision you negotiated around it. The skill is not persuading the mentor; it is surfacing the real reason behind a policy so the eventual decision protects students rather than just satisfying you.

## Learning Targets

- Reframe a request to a mentor from a grievance into an inquiry that can surface the policy's actual rationale.
- Distinguish the stated rule (no phones) from the underlying concern it protects (uncontrolled student-data exposure).
- Document a bounded AI/software support move with the safeguard you accepted and the use you ruled out.
- Write a conversation guide a third party could read to see the concern, the agreed safeguard, and the planned check.

## Opening Case

When a mentor says "no phones in my room," the rule is a proxy for a concern, and you do not yet know which concern. It could be off-task behavior, an incident with parents, a district directive, or a specific app that mishandled student data last year. Arguing against the rule treats it as arbitrary. Asking about the rule treats the mentor as a professional with reasons you have not heard yet.

The conversation guide records that difference. A weak guide scripts your case for why you should get what other candidates get. A strong guide scripts the question that lets the mentor name the real risk, because only once the risk is named can the two of you design a use that respects it.

## Core Concept

Every classroom rule is a compressed judgment. "No phones" compresses a history of incidents, a read of these particular students, and a tolerance for risk into a single prohibition. The conversation guide decompresses it. The central technique is moving from positions to interests: a position is what someone demands (the ban, or your request to use a polling app), and an interest is the underlying concern the position protects (keeping student responses off an unvetted server, getting fast formative data).

When two positions collide, there is rarely a winner. When the interests beneath them are named, a third option often appears that serves both. In the phone case, the position "no phones" and the position "let me use a polling app" look irreconcilable until the mentor's interest (no student data on an outside server) surfaces; then a district-approved version that keeps responses on the school server satisfies both interests at once.

> **Standard of care.** The mentor's outside-server concern tracks specific law, not temperament. [FERPA](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/resources/responsibilities-third-party-service-providers-under-ferpa) governs when a school may disclose education records to a third-party vendor, and its school-official exception requires the district to keep direct control over how the vendor uses and maintains those records. [COPPA](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/22/2025-05904/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule) separately restricts online collection of personal information from students under 13, and the FTC's amended COPPA Rule (effective June 23, 2025, with full compliance required by April 2026) tightens consent requirements for third-party disclosures. As of 2026, most states also have student-data-privacy laws, many districts require a signed data-privacy agreement and vendor approval before student responses leave school-controlled systems, and more than 30 states have issued K-12 AI guidance that stresses vetting vendors on exactly these terms. So "whether the district has approved the vendor" is not mere caution; it is the compliance step these laws assume.

Three lenses keep the conversation defensible:

- **[TPACK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_pedagogical_content_knowledge)** keeps the request anchored to a learning purpose. If you cannot say what the polling app does for the content that pencil-and-paper cannot, the mentor is right to refuse it.
- **Critical edtech studies** names whose interests a tool serves beyond the classroom. The mentor's worry about an outside server is exactly this lens applied: the app's vendor has interests in the data that your lesson does not.
- **Teacher professional judgment** treats the mentor's accumulated reasons as data, not as obstacles. A policy you cannot yet explain is a gap in your information, not proof the policy is wrong.

A lens has done its work when it changes what you propose, not just how you argue for what you already wanted.

## Worked Example

Compare two openers for the same request. The grievance version frames the mentor as an obstacle:

> Your phone ban is outdated. Other candidates get to use phones, so I should too.

This guarantees a defensive answer and surfaces no information. The inquiry version frames the mentor as a source:

> Can you walk me through the reason behind the phone policy? I have one bounded use I would like to think through with you.

The second opener does two things the first cannot. It invites the mentor to name the interest beneath the rule, and it signals that your use is bounded (one use, not a general license). The negotiated decision that follows can then be specific: the district-approved app for one ungraded warm-up, responses on the school server, no named student data collected. That specificity is what makes the guide auditable later.

### Annotated Judgment Call: Negotiating a Phone Policy With a Mentor

Devon wants to try a free polling app for quick formative checks, but his mentor bans all student phone use. He drafts a conversation guide.

1. **Before (complaint draft):** "Your phone ban is outdated. Other candidates get to use phones, so I should too."
2. **Reframe to inquiry:** He rewrites the opener as a question: "Can you walk me through the reason behind the phone policy? I have one bounded use I would like to think through with you."
3. **Mentor's actual concern surfaced:** The mentor explains that last year a similar app stored student responses on an outside server with no district agreement. Devon records this as the real constraint, not an obstacle.
4. **Negotiated decision (after):** "We agreed I may use the polling app for one ungraded warm-up, but only the district-approved version that keeps responses on the school server. We ruled out collecting any named student data."
5. **Check during the lesson:** Devon will confirm no names are entered and report back whether the warm-up surfaced misconceptions worth reteaching.

The record shows the concern, the agreed safeguard, the use ruled out, and the check. That is professional judgment, not just getting a yes.

Now sit in the conversation yourself. Each turn either surfaces the mentor's real interest or hardens the standoff.

## Decision Rule

In a mentor conversation, surface the interest before you defend the position. The order is: ask for the rationale first, name the bounded use you want second, and propose a safeguard that addresses the rationale third. Devon's case follows it exactly: he asked first (the server concern surfaced), named one bounded use (a single ungraded warm-up), and proposed the safeguard (district-approved version, no named data) that resolved the mentor's actual interest.

Before you treat a negotiated decision as final, three checks should pass:

1. **Interest check:** Can you state the concern beneath the mentor's rule in the mentor's own terms, not as "they just don't like phones"?
2. **Safeguard check:** Does your proposed use address that specific concern, or does it only address your convenience?
3. **Boundary check:** What did you and the mentor explicitly rule out, and what check will you run during the lesson to confirm the boundary held?

A "yes" without these is permission, not agreement. The guide should record agreement.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is treating a "yes" as the goal. Getting permission while leaving the mentor's concern unaddressed sets up the next incident. The second is mistaking engagement for justification: "students love the app" is a position, not a reason that answers a data-safety interest. The third is reading the mentor's policy as ignorance to be corrected. Mentors usually hold reasons you have not heard; a guide that treats those reasons as obstacles has skipped the only step that makes the conversation professional.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the positions-to-interests move to a different conflict. A mentor refuses to let you use an AI essay-feedback tool. Your position is "let me use it to give faster feedback." The mentor's position is "no AI on student writing." If you argue the positions, you lose, because the mentor has authority and you have a grievance.

Instead, ask for the rationale. Suppose the mentor's interest is that AI feedback last year flattened the voice of multilingual writers and a parent complained. Now the conflict reframes: your interest (faster feedback) and the mentor's interest (protecting multilingual students' voice) can both be served by a bounded use where the AI flags only mechanics while you handle voice and content, with the multilingual students' flagged work reviewed by you before any AI comment is shown. The guide records the surfaced interest, the bounded use, and the review safeguard. The walkthrough is the same structure as the phone case with a different concern; the technique transfers, the specifics do not.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read a conversation guide, keep a two-column note: **position** on the left, **interest** on the right. For every demand on either side, write the concern it protects. A guide that has only positions and no interests is a script for an argument; a guide with interests is a map for a decision. The presence of named interests on both sides is the signal that the candidate negotiated rather than lobbied.

A strong reader of a conversation guide can answer four questions:

1. What concern does the mentor's rule actually protect, in the mentor's terms?
2. What bounded use did the candidate propose, and how narrow is the boundary?
3. What safeguard addresses the mentor's specific concern, not the candidate's convenience?
4. What would the candidate renegotiate if the mentor's concern turned out to be different than assumed?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A conversation guide is strong when a reader who was not in the room can see both the concern and the agreement. That requires the guide to record the mentor's interest in concrete terms (last year's outside-server incident, not "the mentor is cautious"), the specific bounded use agreed to, and the use explicitly ruled out. Smooth phrasing is the last layer; a guide that reads as a persuasive essay but never names the mentor's real concern has documented a debate, not a negotiation.

A useful closing line sounds like this: "We agreed to a single bounded use because it addressed the mentor's actual concern about an outside server, and we ruled out named-data collection entirely. I rejected the version that was faster for me, because it would have reintroduced exactly the risk the policy exists to prevent."

## Transfer Case

Take the same negotiation skill to a higher-stakes setting: a mentor who is not cautious but enthusiastic, who wants you to use a new AI grading tool you have doubts about. Now the authority gradient runs the other way; the mentor is pushing the tool and you hold the concern. The positions-to-interests move still applies, but you are the one who must surface an interest (consistency of grading across students, your own ability to defend a grade) without dismissing the mentor's enthusiasm. If you can run the same negotiation when the power dynamic reverses, you understand the technique rather than one comfortable script.
