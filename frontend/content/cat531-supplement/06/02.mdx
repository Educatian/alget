## Why This Section Matters

A teaching philosophy is the document a hiring committee, a mentor, or your future self reads to learn what you will actually do when a class is hard and a tool is tempting. Most candidate philosophies fail in the same way: they state warm beliefs ("technology should empower every learner") that no one could disagree with and that commit the writer to nothing. With AI now in nearly every classroom workflow, a philosophy that only emits values is worse than empty; it lets a teacher believe they have a stance while the actual practice (auto-grading, auto-feedback, data sharing) drifts wherever the default settings push it.

This section treats a teaching philosophy with AI as a set of operationalized commitments: for each value you claim, a specific practice that delivers it, a boundary you will refuse to cross, and a disclosure you owe students. The Design Tension Studio map is the proving ground, because a philosophy is only credible when its stated values survive contact with a real classroom conflict.

## Opening Case

Two candidates write the same opening line: "I believe AI should support, not replace, my professional judgment." Candidate A stops there. Candidate B continues: "Concretely, AI drafts feedback on student writing, but I read every draft and revise it before it reaches a student, and AI never enters or changes a grade without my sign-off." Only Candidate B has said anything a committee can test against her practice, disagree with, or hold her to. The first is a sentiment; the second is a commitment with a visible boundary.

The difference is not eloquence. It is whether the philosophy specifies a behavior under pressure. The pressure case for AI is always the same: the tool offers to do something faster than you can, and the question is what you will refuse to hand over even when handing it over would be convenient.

## Core Concept

A defensible AI teaching philosophy is built from value-to-practice triples, each of which makes an abstract belief falsifiable:

- **The value** names what you are committed to (student autonomy, equity, accountability for grades).
- **The enacted practice** names the observable behavior that delivers the value (students may opt for teacher-only feedback; you read AI feedback before it is sent).
- **The boundary** names what you will refuse even when it costs you time (no AI-assigned grades, no uploading student work to a tool that trains on it).

Two further commitments turn a private stance into a professional one:

- **Disclosure.** Students and families are told when AI has read or processed student work, because consent without notice is not consent.
- **Consistency.** The values section and the practice section cannot contradict each other. A philosophy that claims autonomy while the described workflow auto-processes every submission is internally incoherent, and a reader will catch it.

**Legal anchor.** The disclosure and data-protection boundaries rest on a statutory floor, not just professional courtesy. FERPA keeps education records under the school's direct control: the "school official" exception lets a vendor process student records only under contractual control, with data-use limits and no onward sharing or repurposing such as model training. COPPA requires verifiable parental consent before collecting data from children under 13, and the FTC's amended rule (finalized January 2025, with compliance required by April 2026) now requires separate consent before a child's data is disclosed to third parties and tightens retention and monetization limits. As of 2026, roughly half of US state education departments have issued generative-AI guidance for K-12. Treat these as the legal floor your commitments rest on; the ethical bar in your philosophy should sit above it.

The course anchors keep the triples honest. [TPACK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_pedagogical_content_knowledge) checks that the AI practice serves a real learning demand rather than novelty. Critical edtech studies (Selwyn) checks who carries the hidden cost of a convenient default. Datafication checks whether your disclosure actually matches what the tool does with the data. A value you cannot pair with a practice, a boundary, and a disclosure is a slogan, not a commitment.

### Worked Example: An Annotated Philosophy Revision

Watch a teaching-philosophy paragraph move from slogan to defensible stance.

1. **Before (slogan).** "I believe AI should empower every learner to reach their full potential." A reviewer annotates: "empower how, with what limit, and what happens when it does not?"
2. **Name a committed practice.** Revision adds: "I will use AI to generate first-draft feedback on student writing so I can spend conference time on each student's specific next step."
3. **State the boundary.** "I will not let AI assign or change a grade without my review, because grading is a judgment I am accountable for."
4. **Disclose to students.** "Students will be told when AI has read their work, and may opt for teacher-only feedback, because autonomy means they choose how their writing is processed."
5. **Check consistency (after).** The values section now matches the practice section: autonomy is stated and the opt-out practice delivers it. The earlier slogan claimed autonomy while the draft practice had AI auto-correct work before submission, which the revision removed.

The before could fit any candidate; the after states what this teacher will do, will refuse, and will disclose, so a hiring committee can question it.

## Decision Rule

Before you write any belief into a philosophy, run it through the triple test: state the practice that enacts it, the boundary that protects it, and the disclosure it requires. If a belief cannot generate all three, it is not yet a professional commitment and should be cut or rewritten.

A philosophy paragraph should survive three reader questions:

1. **Practice check:** What specific, observable thing will I do that enacts this value?
2. **Boundary check:** What will I refuse to let AI do here, even when refusing costs me time?
3. **Consistency check:** Does any practice I describe contradict a value I claim?

If any answer is missing or contradictory, the paragraph is a slogan and a committee will read it as one.

## Common Misreadings

A frequent mistake is mistaking caution for a philosophy. "I will be careful with AI" names a temperament, not a commitment; carefulness has no boundary a reader can check. The opposite error is enthusiasm as substitute for judgment: "I embrace AI to transform learning" tells a committee nothing about what you would refuse.

The deeper trap is letting the philosophy and the gradebook disagree. A teacher can sincerely claim accountability for grades while quietly letting an LMS auto-score and auto-release. The reader who notices the contradiction trusts the practice, not the prose, and concludes the stated value is decorative.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Take the value "I am accountable for every grade a student receives" and build it into a defensible commitment. The practice that enacts it: AI may suggest a score and a rationale, but you open every suggestion, check it against the rubric, and either confirm or override before any grade posts. The boundary that protects it: no automatic grade release, no AI changing a grade after a regrade request without your review. The disclosure it requires: students are told AI drafts the first-pass score and that you make the final call, so a student appealing a grade knows whom they are appealing to.

Now stress-test the commitment against [TPACK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_pedagogical_content_knowledge) and datafication. [TPACK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_pedagogical_content_knowledge) asks whether the AI score even measures the learning target; if the rubric rewards argument quality and the AI mostly scores grammar, the practice needs a guardrail (you weight the rubric dimensions yourself). Datafication asks whether the scoring tool retains the student essays; if it does, your accountability boundary now includes refusing tools that keep the work. The finished paragraph is no longer a belief about accountability; it is a procedure a mentor could audit on any given assignment.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read a philosophy (yours or a peer's), underline every value claim and write next to it the practice, boundary, and disclosure that would make it real. Empty claims will have blank margins; those are the lines to rewrite. By the end of CAT 531 06.02 you should be able to read any teaching-philosophy paragraph and say within seconds whether it commits the writer to a checkable behavior or merely signals good intentions.

A strong reader of an AI teaching philosophy can answer four questions:

1. For each stated value, what observable practice enacts it?
2. What is the boundary the writer will refuse to cross, even under time pressure?
3. Does any described practice contradict a claimed value?
4. What is disclosed to students, and does the disclosure match what the tools actually do?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A philosophy is strong when a skeptical reader can convert every paragraph into a behavior they could observe in your classroom next week. Eloquence is the last layer; the load-bearing layers are the value-practice-boundary triples and the consistency between what you claim and what you describe doing. If a paragraph has a value but no enacted practice, mark it and ask BigAL to compare a slogan version with a committed version of that exact belief rather than to "make it stronger."

A useful closing line would read: "I claim accountability for grades and enact it by reviewing every AI-suggested score against the rubric before release; the practice I refused is auto-posting, because a value I will not staff with a procedure is one I do not really hold."

**When the value meets a biased tool.** Suppose your disclosed value is "AI gives every student fast feedback," but the auto-feedback or detection tool performs measurably worse on multilingual or AAVE writers (Liang et al., 2023, documented exactly this disparate false-positive pattern for non-native English writers). The value as stated silently shifts burden onto those students, which forces a boundary: "I check tool error rates by subgroup before trusting AI feedback." A disclosed value without that boundary is a promise the tool cannot keep equitably.

## Transfer Case

Move the same philosophy from a writing classroom to a math classroom. The value (accountability for grades) is stable, but the enacting practice must change: AI in math may auto-check computation reliably, so the boundary shifts from "review every score" to "review every score where partial credit or reasoning is involved." If your philosophy only worked because you memorized the writing-class procedure, it will fail to transfer. If you understood the value-practice-boundary structure, you regenerate a new, defensible practice for the new subject without abandoning the value. That regeneration, not the original wording, is the evidence you actually hold the commitment.
