# Portfolio Artifact Selection

![Portfolio Artifact Selection textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/06-03-portfolio-artifact-selection.png)

## Why This Section Matters

A teaching portfolio is an argument, not a scrapbook. Every artifact you include is a piece of evidence offered in support of a specific claim about your competence, and a reviewer reads the portfolio the way a juror reads exhibits: asking what each one actually proves. The most common portfolio failure is selecting by pride (the assignment you got an A on, the lesson that felt good) instead of by warrant (the artifact that demonstrates the exact competency the claim names). A high grade proves you satisfied an instructor; it does not, by itself, prove you can use technology to support equitable learning.

Selection is therefore an evidentiary discipline. For each competency claim, you choose the smallest set of artifacts that, taken together, would convince a skeptic, and you tag each artifact with the reason it earns its place. The Design Tension Studio map is one such artifact, and choosing whether and how to include it is itself a selection decision you must be able to defend.

## Opening Case

A candidate must evidence the claim "uses technology to support equitable learning." Her instinct is to lead with her highest-scoring artifact, a polished biology slide deck. But the deck proves content mastery and design taste; it says nothing about equity of access. Meanwhile, sitting unused in her files is a one-page reflection in which she rejected a popular app because it excluded students without home devices. That reflection looks like a record of something she did not do, yet it is the single strongest piece of evidence for the equity claim, because it shows the judgment the claim names being exercised under real constraint.

The lesson is that evidentiary value and personal pride often point in opposite directions. The artifact you are proudest of may prove the wrong thing, and the artifact that looks like a near-miss may be the clearest demonstration of the competency.

## Core Concept

Portfolio artifact selection is the practice of matching evidence to claims by warrant, coverage, and economy:

- **Warrant** is the link between an artifact and the claim it supports. An artifact has warrant for a claim only if it shows the specific competency being asserted. A grade is warrant for "meets instructor expectations," not for "supports equitable learning."
- **Coverage** asks whether the chosen artifacts, together, address the full claim. A claim about equitable technology use is not covered by access alone; it may also need evidence of differentiation and of refusing a tool that excluded learners.
- **Economy** is the discipline of choosing the fewest artifacts that establish the claim. Adding a fourth artifact that proves nothing new dilutes the argument and signals you cannot tell strong evidence from filler.

A useful move is to write each artifact a one-line rationale tag stating exactly what it proves:

| Artifact | Claim it warrants | Why it earns inclusion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| UDL lesson plan with access plan | Equitable design | Names concrete access supports, not just intent |
| Screenshots of differentiated AI-feedback settings | Differentiation in practice | Shows the tool tuned for varied needs |
| Reflection rejecting a device-dependent tool | Equity judgment | Demonstrates the value exercised against convenience |

If you cannot write a rationale tag that names a distinct contribution, the artifact does not belong in this argument. The course anchors sharpen the tags: TPACK tests whether an artifact actually shows technology serving a learning target, critical edtech studies tests whether it shows attention to who is excluded, and datafication tests whether it shows responsible handling of student data.

## Worked Example

Start with a thin claim that many students write on a first pass:

> I made the Design Tension Studio map naming a classroom value conflict and negotiated decision clearer.

That claim is too thin because "clearer" does not identify the audience, the problem, or the evidence. A stronger claim would read:

> I revised the Design Tension Studio map naming a classroom value conflict and negotiated decision so that the intended audience can see the decision, the constraint, and the evidence source. The revision is justified by **critical edtech studies**, and I can point to the exact part of the artifact that changed.

The stronger version treats the Design Tension Studio map naming a classroom value conflict and negotiated decision as evidence. It names what changed, links the change to **critical edtech studies**, and leaves a reader able to audit the decision.

### Worked Example: An Annotated Selection Decision

Follow a candidate selecting 3 artifacts to evidence the claim "uses technology to support equitable learning."

1. **The claim to evidence.** "Uses technology to support equitable learning." Every selected artifact must defensibly support this exact claim.
2. **Candidate pool (before).** Five files: (a) an A-graded slide deck on cell biology, (b) a UDL lesson plan with an access plan, (c) screenshots of differentiated AI-feedback settings, (d) a reflection rejecting a tool that excluded device-less students, (e) a recent quiz with a high score.
3. **First instinct, then annotation.** First pick was (a) and (e) because of their grades. Reviewer note: "These show content mastery, not equity-of-access judgment." The grades are not the claim.
4. **Selection (after).** Keep (b), (c), (d). Each carries a rationale tag: (b) names the UDL access plan; (c) shows feedback tuned for varied needs; (d) shows a tool rejected on equity grounds.
5. **Defend a kept failure.** Artifact (d) documents a rejected tool, which looks like failure but evidences reflective equity judgment, so it stays.

The before selects by grade; the after selects by what each artifact proves about the claim, and tags the reason so a reviewer can audit it.

## Decision Rule

Select for warrant before you select for polish. Ask first "what claim am I making," then "which artifact most directly proves it to a skeptic," and only then "is the artifact presentable." A beautiful artifact that proves the wrong claim is a weaker exhibit than a plain one that proves the right claim.

Run each candidate artifact through three questions:

1. **Warrant check:** Does this artifact show the exact competency the claim names, not an adjacent one?
2. **Coverage check:** Does the set, taken together, address the whole claim rather than one slice of it?
3. **Economy check:** Would removing this artifact weaken the argument? If not, cut it.

If an artifact fails the warrant check, no amount of polish will save its place in the portfolio.

## Common Misreadings

The dominant misreading is treating grade as warrant. A grade certifies that an instructor was satisfied; it rarely maps onto the specific professional competency a portfolio claim asserts, and reviewers know this. A second misreading is hoarding: including everything strong so the portfolio looks abundant. Abundance without economy reads as an inability to judge evidence, which undercuts the very competency a portfolio is meant to demonstrate.

A subtler error is excluding artifacts that document a refusal or a near-miss. A reflection in which you rejected an unfit tool can be the most persuasive evidence of professional judgment precisely because it shows the value winning against convenience.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Suppose your claim is "designs assessments that AI cannot trivially complete for the student." A pride-first selection would feature your most elaborate project. A warrant-first selection asks what would prove this specific competency. Strong candidates: a revised assignment prompt that requires students to critique an AI draft rather than produce one (shows you redesigned for the AI era), a side-by-side of the old AI-vulnerable prompt and the new one (shows the reasoning), and a student work sample where the AI-resistant task surfaced genuine thinking (shows it worked).

Now apply coverage and economy. The three artifacts cover design intent, the reasoning behind it, and evidence of effect, which together establish the claim; a fourth artifact showing a generic rubric would add no new warrant and should be cut. Tag each: "redesigned prompt, shifts the cognitive demand from production to evaluation," "before/after, makes the design reasoning auditable," "student sample, evidence the redesign elicited thinking AI could not supply." A reviewer can now follow the argument from claim to proof without taking your word for any link.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read a portfolio (yours or a peer's), cover the artifacts and read only the claims; then predict what evidence each claim would need. Uncover the artifacts and check whether the actual selections match your predicted evidence or merely sit near it. The gaps you find are exactly where the portfolio substitutes pride for warrant. By the end of CAT 531 06.03 you should be able to look at any artifact and immediately name which claim, if any, it actually warrants.

A strong reader of a portfolio can answer four questions:

1. What specific claim does each artifact warrant, in one sentence?
2. Does the set cover the whole claim or only the easiest part of it?
3. Which artifact could be removed without weakening the argument?
4. Is any artifact included for pride or polish rather than warrant?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A selection is strong when every artifact carries a rationale tag a skeptic would accept, and when removing any one artifact would visibly weaken the case. Polish is the last layer; the load-bearing layers are warrant, coverage, and economy. If an artifact lacks a defensible tag, mark it and ask BigAL to compare it against a stronger candidate for the same claim rather than to "improve the portfolio."

A useful closing line would read: "I included the rejected-tool reflection over my A-graded deck because the claim is about equity judgment, not content mastery, and the reflection is the only artifact where that judgment is actually visible in action."

## Transfer Case

Move the same portfolio from a job application to a National Board certification review. The artifacts may stay, but the governing claims change: certification asks for evidence of impact on student learning over time, not a snapshot of competence. An artifact that warranted "designs equitable tasks" for a hiring panel may now need a companion piece showing the task's effect across a unit. If you understood selection as matching evidence to a claim, you re-tag and re-cover for the new claims without panic. If you only assembled a fixed set of favorites, you will submit evidence that proves the wrong proposition for the new audience.
