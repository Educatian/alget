# Evidence-Based Reflection

![Evidence-Based Reflection textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/06-06-evidence-based-reflection.png)

## Why This Section Matters

Most teaching reflection is a record of feelings: the lesson "went well," students "seemed engaged," the tool "really helped." Feelings are real, but they are not evidence, and a reflection built on them cannot tell you whether to keep doing the thing or stop. Evidence-based reflection replaces impression with a small, honest argument: here is what I claim happened, here is the data that supports it, here is the data that complicates it, here are the rival explanations I cannot rule out, and here is the check I will run next. It is the discipline that turns teaching from a series of vibes into a practice that learns.

This matters most for AI tools, where the experience of speed and novelty is especially seductive. A class can feel transformed by an AI feedback tool while the actual learning gain belongs to an easier quiz or a review day that happened the same week. The reflection that survives scrutiny is the one that went looking for the evidence against its own first impression. The Design Tension Studio map is the decision; the reflection is where you find out, with evidence, whether the decision was right.

## Opening Case

A teacher writes: "The AI feedback unit went really well and students were engaged." A mentor asks two questions that the reflection cannot answer. What did you observe that a skeptic would accept as evidence? And what did you notice that argued against your conclusion? The teacher has reported a conclusion without the case for it, and (more revealingly) without any sign that she looked for disconfirming evidence. A reflection that only confirms its author's hopes is indistinguishable from wishful thinking.

The repair turns the impression into an argument. Exit-ticket scores rose from 58% to 81%, which is evidence. But the four students on the printed fallback scored twelve points below peers, which is counter-evidence the honest reflection must report. And the same week had a review day and an easier quiz, which are rival explanations she cannot yet dismiss. The reflection becomes credible precisely when it stops being flattering.

## Core Concept

Evidence-based reflection is the construction of a defeasible claim about your own teaching, built from four moves:

- **Claim with data.** State what you think happened and attach the specific evidence: scores, work samples, observation counts, not "engagement" but the behaviors you would count as engagement.
- **Seek disconfirming evidence.** Actively report the data that complicates the claim. The subgroup that did not gain, the student whose work got worse, the metric that stayed flat. A reflection that contains no counter-evidence has not yet been honest, because real classrooms are never uniformly successful.
- **Weigh rival explanations.** Before crediting a gain to your tool, ask what else could have caused it. A review day, an easier assessment, regression to the mean, or your own increased attention to the topic are all rivals that must be considered before the tool gets the credit.
- **Commit to a check.** End not with a verdict but with the next test that would distinguish your explanation from the rivals (for example, holding quiz difficulty constant next time).

This structure mirrors how evidence works in any inquiry: a claim is only as strong as the disconfirming evidence it survived and the alternatives it ruled out. The course anchors keep the reflection grounded. TPACK keeps the claim tied to a real learning target rather than to engagement theater; critical edtech studies pushes the disconfirming move toward subgroup effects the average hides; datafication reminds you that the tool's own analytics are a vendor-shaped data source, not neutral truth.

### Worked Example: An Annotated Evidence-Based Reflection

Follow a teacher revising a reflection on a unit taught with an AI feedback tool.

1. **Before (impression).** "The lesson went really well and students were engaged." A reviewer annotates: "What did you observe, and what argued against your view?"
2. **Bring the data.** Revision: "Exit-ticket scores rose from 58% to 81% across the class."
3. **Report disconfirming evidence.** Add: "The 4 students on the printed fallback averaged 12 points lower than peers on the tool, so the gain may not be shared evenly."
4. **Check rival explanations.** The same week included a review day and an easier quiz, so the teacher notes she cannot yet credit the gain to the tool alone.
5. **Stated next step (after).** "Next week I will hold the quiz difficulty constant and compare the fallback group again before deciding whether to keep the tool."

The before reports a feeling; the after cites data, names counter-evidence, weighs rival causes, and commits to a check, which is what makes a reflection inspectable.

## Decision Rule

Look for the evidence against your conclusion before you write the conclusion. A reflection earns trust in proportion to the disconfirming evidence it reports and the rival explanations it rules out, not the confidence it projects. If your reflection contains no counter-evidence, assume you have not looked hard enough yet.

Run any reflection through three questions:

1. **Evidence check:** Is the claim attached to specific data a skeptic would accept, not to "engagement" or "felt good"?
2. **Disconfirmation check:** Does it report at least one finding that complicates the claim, such as a subgroup that did not gain?
3. **Rival check:** Have I considered what besides my tool could explain the result, and named the next test that would distinguish them?

If any answer is missing, the reflection is an impression dressed as a finding.

## Common Misreadings

The dominant error is confirmation: reporting only the evidence that supports the hoped-for conclusion and quietly ignoring the rest. This is rarely dishonesty; it is the natural pull of wanting a decision to have been right. The corrective is to make disconfirmation a required move rather than an optional virtue. A related error is the average that hides the subgroup: a class mean can rise while the students the tool was supposed to help fall further behind, and a reflection that reports only the mean misses the equity story entirely.

The subtlest misreading is the premature verdict. Crediting a gain to the tool before ruling out the review day and the easier quiz is a causal claim the evidence does not yet license. Honest reflection often ends in "I do not yet know, and here is how I will find out," which is a stronger professional position than a confident attribution the data cannot support.

## Textbook Walkthrough

A teacher reflects on whether an AI vocabulary app improved retention. The impression: "Students loved it and seemed to remember more." The evidence-based version starts with data: delayed-recall quiz scores rose from a class average of 62% to 74%. Then disconfirmation: she disaggregates and finds the gain concentrated in students who already scored high, while the lowest quartile barely moved, so the app may be widening rather than closing the gap the tension map cared about.

Then rivals: the app launched the same week she started a daily five-minute review routine, so the routine is a live alternative cause. And datafication: the app's own dashboard claims "90% mastery," but that figure counts in-app practice, not independent recall, so she treats the vendor metric as a claim to verify rather than evidence. Her reflection concludes not with "keep the app" but with a check: next unit she will run the review routine without the app for one section to isolate the effect, and she will track the lowest quartile specifically. That conclusion is modest, evidence-anchored, and actually informative, which is everything an impression-based reflection is not.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read a reflection, separate every sentence into impression (a feeling or unsupported conclusion) and evidence (a checkable observation). Then ask the two questions the author should have asked: what here argues against the conclusion, and what besides the intervention could explain the result. The missing answers reveal where the reflection stopped being honest. By the end of CAT 531 06.06 you should be able to read any teaching reflection and immediately tell whether its author went looking for the evidence against their own view.

A strong reader of an evidence-based reflection can answer four questions:

1. What specific data supports the claim, and would a skeptic accept it?
2. What disconfirming evidence, especially at the subgroup level, did the author report?
3. What rival explanations were considered before crediting the intervention?
4. What concrete check would settle the open question next time?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A reflection is strong when a reader who wanted the opposite conclusion would still find the argument fair, because the disconfirming evidence and the rival explanations are on the page, not omitted. Confident prose is the last layer; the load-bearing layers are the data, the counter-evidence, and the named next check. If a claim has no disconfirming evidence beside it, mark it and ask BigAL to compare an impression-only paragraph with an evidence-and-counterevidence paragraph rather than to "make it more reflective."

A useful closing line would read: "I cannot yet credit the gain to the tool because a review day confounds it and the lowest quartile did not move; next unit I will hold difficulty constant and track that subgroup before deciding to keep it."

## Transfer Case

Move the reflection from a single unit to an end-of-year review of a whole technology initiative. The four moves still govern, but the evidence base widens: a year of data invites trend analysis and the rivals multiply (curriculum changes, new staff, a different cohort). The temptation to credit the initiative for any improvement grows with the stakes. If you understood reflection as disciplined inquiry, you scale the disconfirmation and rival-explanation moves to the larger claim. If you only learned to attach a number to a feeling, the year-end reflection will become a confident endorsement that the messy multi-year evidence cannot actually support.
