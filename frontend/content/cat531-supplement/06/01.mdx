# Synthesis Across Modules

![Synthesis Across Modules textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/06-01-synthesis-across-modules.png)

## Why This Section Matters

Across the first five modules of CAT 531 you analyzed pedagogy, equity, classroom logistics, and data ethics as if they were separate lenses you could pick up one at a time. Real teaching decisions never arrive that cleanly. When a colleague proposes an AI essay-feedback tool in a department meeting, the pedagogical promise (faster revision cycles), the equity risk (uneven home access), and the data boundary (whether student writing is used for training) all press on the same choice at the same moment, and they frequently pull in opposite directions. Synthesis is the skill of holding those competing claims together and producing a single decision you can defend, not a list of considerations you noticed.

The artifact for this section, the Design Tension Studio map, exists to make that synthesis visible. A tension map does not ask you to resolve a conflict by pretending it disappeared; it asks you to name which value you let win, which you constrained, and what you gave up. That record is the difference between a teacher who says "I weighed the options" and one who can show, on paper, the weighing itself.

## Opening Case

A seventh-grade team is deciding whether to adopt an adaptive reading platform. The literacy coach loves the individualized pacing (a Module 2 pedagogy argument). The special-education teacher worries the platform's reading-level labels will follow students into other classes as a fixed identity (a Module 3 equity-and-labeling concern). The technology coordinator notes the vendor's contract permits resale of usage analytics (a Module 5 datafication boundary). Each colleague is right within their own lens. The question synthesis forces is not "who is correct" but "what decision honors the strongest claim from each lens at once, and which claim are we knowingly subordinating."

A teacher who can answer that, naming the subordinated value out loud, has done synthesis. A teacher who simply adopts because the pacing is good, or refuses because the contract is bad, has only applied one lens and called it a decision.

## Core Concept

Synthesis across modules is the disciplined act of resolving a multi-lens conflict into one accountable choice. It has three properties that distinguish it from a checklist:

- **It surfaces a genuine tension, not a list.** A checklist says "consider pedagogy, equity, and data." A synthesis names the specific place where pedagogy and equity collide for this tool, in this class, this term. If your map could describe any tool, it has not located a tension yet.
- **It assigns priority, not equality.** Treating every value as equally weighted is a refusal to decide. Synthesis states which value governs when two cannot both be fully satisfied, and gives the reason that ranking holds here (for example, "access governs efficiency because a revision the device-less student cannot reach is not a revision").
- **It records the cost.** Every real decision sacrifices something. A defensible map names what the chosen path gives up so a colleague can judge whether the trade was worth it.

The three course anchors do real work only when each one can change the decision:

| Anchor | What it forces you to ask | A change it can produce |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [TPACK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_pedagogical_content_knowledge) (Mishra & Koehler, 2006) | Does the tool serve a content-and-pedagogy demand, or just a generic "engagement" goal? | Drop a flashy tool whose AI feature does not touch the actual learning target. |
| Critical edtech studies (Selwyn) | Who carries new burden or risk that the marketing hides? | Add a fallback for students the default workflow would exclude. |
| [AI and education datafication](https://doi.org/10.1080/17439884.2020.1686017) (Perrotta & Selwyn, 2020; Williamson, 2023) | What happens to student data, and who profits from it? | Require an in-class, no-training-data configuration before adoption. |

If an anchor leaves your decision identical, you cited it; you did not use it.

## Worked Example

A first draft often hides the real decision behind a vague improvement claim:

> I made the Design Tension Studio map naming a classroom value conflict and negotiated decision clearer.

That claim is too thin because "clearer" does not identify the audience, the problem, or the evidence. A stronger claim would read:

> I revised the Design Tension Studio map naming a classroom value conflict and negotiated decision so that the intended audience can see the decision, the constraint, and the evidence source. The revision is justified by **critical edtech studies**, and I can point to the exact part of the artifact that changed.

Notice the shift: the artifact is no longer just polished. The revision points to **critical edtech studies**, names the evidence source, and preserves the reason a suggestion was accepted or rejected.

### Worked Example: An Annotated Synthesis Decision

Follow one teacher building a tension map for an AI writing-feedback tool across the module lenses.

1. **Scenario.** A 9th-grade teacher pilots an AI tool that gives instant feedback on student essays. Six of 28 students have no reliable internet at home; the tool's free tier caps revisions at three per week.
2. **Surface the conflict (before).** First draft of the map: "AI feedback helps students revise faster, so I will adopt it." This is a single-lens decision (pedagogy only) with no named tension.
3. **Apply each module lens.**
   - Pedagogy (Module 2): faster revision cycles can raise draft quality.
   - Equity (Module 3): the 6 students without home access get fewer revision cycles, widening a gap.
   - Ethics and datafication (Module 5): essays are student data; the free tier's terms allow training on submissions.
4. **Negotiate the collision (after).** Revised decision: "Adopt the tool for in-class use only, on school devices, with a no-training data setting, and a printed-feedback fallback for the 6 students. The equity lens overrides the pure-efficiency option."
5. **Record the rejected suggestion.** BigAL proposed assigning the tool as homework to save class time. Rejected: it reintroduces the home-access gap the equity lens flagged.

The before says what is convenient; the after shows a negotiated judgment a colleague can inspect, including the value that was allowed to win and why.

## Decision Rule

When two course lenses collide on the same tool, name the binding constraint before you reach for the affordance. A platform feature is only worth discussing once you have established that the feature does not violate the value you have decided governs the case.

Use the following check before you call a tension map finished:

1. **Tension check:** Have I named a specific collision between two values for this exact tool and class, or only listed concerns?
2. **Priority check:** Have I stated which value governs and given the reason that ranking holds here?
3. **Cost check:** Have I named what the chosen path sacrifices and what suggestion I rejected to protect the governing value?

If any answer is missing, the map is incomplete as evidence of synthesis even if it looks finished.

## Common Misreadings

The most frequent error is treating synthesis as compromise, splitting the difference so no value is fully honored and none is clearly subordinated. A map that says "we will use the tool a little, for some students, sometimes" reads as indecision dressed up as balance. Synthesis lets a value win; it does not dilute every value until the decision is unfalsifiable.

A second misreading is writing the map after the decision is already made, as a justification rather than a reasoning trace. A colleague should be able to see the collision being negotiated, not a tidy rationale for a choice that was never actually in doubt.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply this to a real adoption meeting. A middle-school team is choosing between two AI math tutors. Tutor A adapts faster but stores keystroke-level data the vendor may license; Tutor B is slower to adapt but stores nothing beyond scores. Begin by naming the tension precisely: adaptivity (a learning gain) versus data exposure (a student-protection cost), where the two tools force a choice rather than letting you have both.

Then let an anchor change the decision. Datafication analysis asks who benefits from the keystroke data; the answer is the vendor, not the students, and that reframes Tutor A's speed as a cost passed to families. [TPACK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_pedagogical_content_knowledge) then asks whether the extra adaptivity even serves the unit's target; if the unit needs procedural fluency that Tutor B handles adequately, the marginal adaptivity buys little. The synthesis names Tutor B as the choice, states that data protection governs because the adaptivity gain is small for this target, and records the cost (students wait slightly longer for the tutor to adjust). That last sentence, the named cost, is what makes the map honest rather than promotional.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: on the left write each value the case raises; on the right write what would have to be true for that value to govern the decision. The point is to practice ranking rather than listing. By the end of CAT 531 06.01 you should be able to take any new tool proposal and immediately ask "which two of my values does this pit against each other, and which one should win here."

A strong reader of synthesis can answer four questions:

1. Where exactly do two course values collide for this specific tool and class?
2. Which value should govern, and why does that ranking hold in this case rather than always?
3. What does the governing choice cost, and is the trade defensible?
4. What would change in the ranking if the class, the contract, or the tool changed?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A tension map is strong when a colleague who disagrees with your decision can still follow your reasoning and locate the exact point where they would have ranked the values differently. Polish (clean layout, tidy arrows) is the last layer; the load-bearing layers are the named collision, the stated priority with its reason, and the recorded cost. If any of those is missing, mark the gap with an annotation and ask BigAL for a targeted comparison of two rankings rather than a general explanation of "balancing values."

A useful closing line for the map would read: "I let equity of access govern over revision efficiency because a benefit students cannot reach is not a benefit; the cost is fewer total revisions for the class, which I judged acceptable because the floor matters more than the ceiling here."

## Transfer Case

Move the same map to a wealthy district where every student has a personal device and reliable home internet. The pedagogy-versus-equity collision that governed the original decision largely dissolves; the home-access gap that made efficiency dangerous no longer exists. But a new tension surfaces: with universal access, the datafication lens may now dominate, because the very ubiquity of the tool means more student data flowing to the vendor across more of the day. If you understand synthesis, your map changes its governing value when the context changes. If you only memorized "equity wins," you will defend the wrong value in the new setting.
