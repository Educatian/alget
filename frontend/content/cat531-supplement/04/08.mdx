# Module 4 Ethics Studio

![Module 4 Ethics Studio textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/04-08-module-4-ethics-studio.png)

## Why This Section Matters

The module taught the ethics tools one at a time: read a dialogue for the reasoning shift, weigh a value conflict on a tension map, find the silent stakeholder, write a consent clause that overrides the vendor default. Real adoption decisions do not arrive sorted into those boxes. A single tool drops all four problems on your desk at once, and the studio is where you prove you can run them together on one realistic case without losing any of them. Integration is its own competency; a teacher who can ace each tool in isolation can still produce an incoherent decision when the privacy answer and the equity answer pull in different directions.

The studio also exposes the trap that separates a checklist from judgment. It is tempting to run each tool, get four clean sub-answers, and staple them into a decision. But the answers interact: the silent stakeholder you found in the stakeholder pass is often the same student the consent rule and the negotiated decision must protect, and a strong capstone shows those threads converging on one coherent call, not four parallel paragraphs that happen to share a cover page.

## Learning Targets

- Sequence the module's tools (purpose, stakeholder check, privacy and consent check, negotiated tension map) into one integrated decision.
- Carry a finding from one tool into the next, so the silent stakeholder shapes both the consent rule and the final decision.
- Keep the learning purpose primary, so efficiency and convenience never silently override why the tool was considered.
- Record a rejected suggestion and the evidence behind the whole judgment, so a mentor teacher can audit the integration.

## Opening Case

A school proposes an AI reading-level assessment that records each student reading aloud and scores fluency automatically. Run only one tool and you get a partial, possibly dangerous answer: the efficiency case alone says "adopt it, it saves screening time." The studio asks for the integrated judgment. State the purpose (identify students who need support, not rank them), run the stakeholder check (the silent stakeholder is the student whose home language is not English, whose accent the model may misread), run the consent check (voice is biometric, so opt-in and deletion are required), and only then build the negotiated decision. The single-tool answer and the integrated answer point in different directions, which is exactly why integration is the skill being assessed.

## Core Concept

The ethics studio is an integration method: a fixed sequence in which each tool's output feeds the next, ending in one defensible decision rather than four separate verdicts. The sequence matters because the tools are not independent.

Start with purpose, because it governs everything after it. If the purpose is "identify students who need support," any feature that ranks or labels students is already in tension with the goal, and you will read the rest of the case through that frame.

Run the stakeholder check next, and carry its output forward. The silent stakeholder you surface (the non-native English speaker the fluency model may penalize) is not a finding you file away; it is the person the consent rule and the negotiated decision must now protect. An integrated studio threads that student through every later step.

Run the privacy and consent check on the specific data type. Voice is biometric, which raises the bar: parental opt-in and a deletion rule are not optional add-ons but conditions of adoption. Notice this finding compounds with the stakeholder finding, since the most-affected student is also the one whose biometric data is most consequential.

Build the negotiated decision last, because it must reconcile all the prior outputs at once: the purpose, the silent stakeholder, the biometric constraint, and the real efficiency benefit. The decision keeps a teacher confirmation step on every flag, requires opt-in and short retention, and is defended with evidence (the vendor's fluency model was trained on a single national accent).

The course anchors operate together here, which is the point. **Teacher professional judgment** sequences the tools and reconciles their outputs into one call. **Critical edtech studies** keeps the silent stakeholder visible from the stakeholder pass through the final decision. **AI and education datafication** raises the consent bar once the data is identified as biometric voice.

The capstone case: adopting an AI reading-level assessment that records each student's oral reading.

Step-by-step integrated decision:

- Step 1. State the learning purpose first. The tool should identify students who need reading support, not rank or label them.
- Step 2. Run the stakeholder check (from 04.03). Silent stakeholder = students whose home language is not English, whose accent the model may misread as low fluency.
- Step 3. Run the privacy and consent check (from 04.04). Voice recordings of minors require parental opt-in and a deletion rule; voice is biometric.
- Step 4. Build the negotiated decision map (from 04.02). Pole A = faster, consistent screening. Pole B = accent bias and biometric voice data. Negotiated decision: pilot with teacher confirmation of any low-fluency flag, parental opt-in, and 30-day deletion.

Before (weak studio submission): "The AI reading tool is efficient and I made the map clear." This skips purpose, the silent stakeholder, and consent.

After (strong): "Integrated decision: purpose is support not ranking; silent stakeholder is non-native-English speakers at risk of accent misreading; voice is biometric so parental opt-in and 30-day deletion are required; negotiated decision keeps a teacher confirmation step on every low-fluency flag. Evidence the accent risk is real = the vendor's fluency model was trained on a single national accent. I rejected the suggestion to auto-place students by score, because that removes the teacher judgment the purpose requires."

The strong submission integrates the module's three tools, cites evidence, and records a rejected suggestion, so a mentor teacher can audit the whole judgment.

## Decision Rule

A studio submission is integrated, not stapled, when:

1. **Purpose leads:** The stated learning purpose visibly constrains every later step.
2. **Threads carry:** The silent stakeholder from the stakeholder pass reappears in the consent rule and the final decision.
3. **Constraints compound:** The biometric or privacy finding raises the bar on the decision, not just on a side note.
4. **One coherent call:** The negotiated decision reconciles purpose, stakeholder, privacy, and benefit, and names a rejected suggestion.

If your four sub-answers never reference each other, you have a checklist; integration is when they converge.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is treating the studio as four separate assignments collated under one heading, so the privacy paragraph never mentions the student the stakeholder paragraph just identified. The second is letting the efficiency benefit, which is the easiest pole to measure, quietly become the deciding factor after the purpose said otherwise. A studio that ends with "it saves time, so adopt" has let the most measurable pole overrule the purpose it opened with.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Run the full sequence on a fresh capstone case: an AI essay-scoring tool a department wants for state-test practice. Purpose: give students fast formative feedback so they revise, not to assign a grade of record. Stakeholder check: the silent stakeholder is the student who writes in a dialect or developmental syntax the model scores as error, the same exposure pattern as the reading-fluency case. Privacy and consent check: essays are student records, so retention must be bounded and vendor training excluded; the bar is high but not biometric, which lets the consent rule be lighter than the voice case demanded. Negotiated decision: adopt for formative use only, with a teacher override on any score that would lower a grade, opt-in for any data leaving the building, and a rejected suggestion (auto-entering the AI score into the gradebook) named with its reason (it converts formative feedback into a high-stakes judgment the purpose forbids). The threads carry, the constraints compound, and the decision reconciles them, which is what distinguishes the studio from running four tools side by side.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

Keep a single-thread note that follows one student through every tool: who the silent stakeholder is, how the consent rule protects them, and how the final decision keeps them safe. A strong reader of a studio submission can trace that thread end to end and can state where the purpose constrained a tempting efficiency shortcut. If your reading treats the four passes as unrelated, the integration the studio assesses is not there.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A strong studio artifact leads with purpose, threads the silent stakeholder through consent and the final decision, compounds the privacy constraint into the call, and names a rejected suggestion with evidence. Visual neatness is the last layer. If the sub-answers do not reference each other, mark the gap and ask BigAL to test whether your final decision actually protects the stakeholder you identified rather than for a general explanation of the module.

A useful closing line for the artifact sounds like this: "Because the purpose was support not ranking and the silent stakeholder was the non-native English speaker the fluency model penalizes, the decision keeps a teacher confirmation on every flag and requires opt-in with 30-day deletion; I rejected auto-placement by score because it would override the very judgment the purpose protects."

## Transfer Case

Move the integration method to a case where the tools conflict more sharply: a safety tool (an AI weapons-detection scanner) where the privacy cost is severe but the stated purpose is student safety, not convenience. Here the purpose pulls toward adoption while the privacy and equity passes pull hard against it, and the negotiated decision cannot lean on "support not ranking" to resolve the tension. Working this version tests whether your integration method holds when the poles are genuinely close, or whether it only worked because the earlier cases had an obvious answer once the tools were run.
