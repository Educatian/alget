# Ethical Dialogue as Learning Activity

![Ethical Dialogue as Learning Activity textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/04-01-ethical-dialogue-as-learning-activity.png)

## Why This Section Matters

Most classroom ethics conversations end where they should begin. Students share opinions, the teacher affirms participation, and the bell rings. Nothing in that exchange tells you whether anyone's moral reasoning actually moved. This section treats ethical dialogue as a designed learning activity rather than a discussion that happens to take place, which means the teacher's job shifts from running a conversation to engineering the conditions under which reasoning can change and then capturing the evidence that it did.

The pivot here is from product to process. An essay tells you where a student landed; a dialogue, read carefully, tells you how they got there and where the turn happened. With a tool like Ethobot 3.2 generating transcripts, you now have a durable record of that reasoning, which is exactly what makes a transcript annotation a defensible artifact: it lets a colleague inspect the same evidence you used and check whether your reading of the shift holds up.

## Learning Targets

- Distinguish a discussion that produces talk from a dialogue designed to produce a shift in reasoning, and explain what each one yields as evidence.
- Identify the structural moves (a stakeholder reframe, a counterexample, a graded alternative to a binary) that mark genuine ethical reasoning rather than position-stating.
- Annotate an Ethobot transcript so the precise turn where reasoning changed is marked, quoted, and attributed to a cause.
- Defend the reuse decision: which exchange becomes the model, which gets cut, and why a colleague should trust that call.

## Opening Case

A teacher runs a 25-minute Ethobot session on whether the school should keep a homework-tracking app. The transcript fills four screens. Afterward she has a choice that looks small but is not: she can label the whole session "rich discussion, high engagement," or she can find the two or three turns where a student's reasoning visibly bent and build the activity around those. The first reading treats the dialogue as something that occurred; the second treats it as something that produced learning she can name, locate, and reuse. This section is about training your eye to do the second reliably.

## Core Concept

Ethical dialogue becomes a learning activity when it is built to create and then expose a change in reasoning. The unit of analysis is not the conversation but the turn, and the goal is to locate the moment a student stops defending a fixed position and starts reasoning toward a revised one.

It helps to distinguish three kinds of turns that look similar on the page but mean different things:

| Turn type | What it sounds like | Evidence value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Position-stating | "We should just ban the app." | Low: a starting stance, not yet reasoning |
| Reframing prompt | "Who benefits and who is burdened if we ban it?" | High as a cause: it can trigger a shift |
| Reasoning shift | "A ban hurts kids who need it for accessibility, so it isn't neutral either." | Highest: the turn worth annotating |

Three course anchors sharpen this reading. **[TPACK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_pedagogical_content_knowledge)** asks what the technology actually adds: an Ethobot transcript is worth using only if the durable record lets you teach the reasoning move better than live discussion would. **Critical edtech studies** asks who the dialogue serves and whether the tool's prompting nudges students toward a "right answer" rather than open reasoning. **Teacher professional judgment** is what turns an annotation from a highlight into a defensible claim: you, not the platform, decide which turn counts as a shift and you stand behind that with quoted evidence.

## Worked Example: Annotating an Ethical-Dialogue Transcript

Below is a classroom-discussion excerpt run with Ethobot 3.2. A teacher is deciding where ethical reasoning actually shifted, so the dialogue can be reused as a learning activity. Work the annotation in numbered steps.

Transcript excerpt (8th-grade ethics discussion):

1. Student A: "We should just ban the app. It tracks kids."
2. Ethobot: "Who benefits and who is burdened if the app is banned?"
3. Student A: "Banning hurts kids who use it for accessibility, so a ban is not neutral either."
4. Student B: "Maybe limit what data it keeps instead of banning it."

Step-by-step judgment call:

- Step 1. Mark the claim line. Turn 1 is a fixed position ("just ban it"), not yet reasoning.
- Step 2. Mark the move that opens reasoning. Turn 2 is the Ethobot prompt that introduces a stakeholder lens.
- Step 3. Mark where reasoning changed. Turn 3 is the shift: the student now weighs a competing burden (accessibility users) against the original position. This is the annotation anchor.
- Step 4. Mark the productive synthesis. Turn 4 reframes the binary into a graded data-retention decision.

Before annotation (weak): "Good discussion, students engaged." This records participation, not the reasoning move.

After annotation (strong): "Reasoning changed at Turn 3: Student A moved from a categorical ban to weighing a competing stakeholder burden. Evidence = the phrase 'a ban is not neutral either.' The Turn 2 Ethobot prompt caused the shift. I will reuse Turns 2 to 3 as the model exchange and cut Turn 1 as a non-reasoning opener."

The strong version names the exact turn, quotes the trigger phrase, attributes the cause, and states a reuse decision a colleague could inspect.

## Decision Rule

When you annotate a dialogue, locate the shift before you judge its quality. A transcript is teaching evidence only when three things are pinned down:

1. **The turn:** Which specific line marks the move from position to revised reasoning?
2. **The trigger:** What prompt or peer comment caused it, quoted verbatim?
3. **The reuse:** Which exchange becomes the model for the next class, and which lines do you cut as filler?

If you cannot point to a turn and quote its trigger, you have a record of talk, not yet evidence of learning.

## Common Misreadings

The most frequent error is counting volume as depth: a long, animated transcript reads as success even when every student simply repeated an opening stance more loudly. A second error is treating the Ethobot prompt as neutral. Its phrasing shapes which reasoning becomes possible, so a "good" shift may partly be an artifact of how the bot framed the question, which is exactly what you should note rather than hide.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the method to a fresh case. A teacher runs an Ethobot dialogue on whether a class novel with a slur should be assigned. One student opens with "Just remove it." Reading for the shift, the teacher waits for the bot's reframe ("What does a student lose if the book is cut, and what does a student carry if it stays?") and then watches Turn 5, where the same student says "Maybe we keep it but the teacher names the harm out loud first." That is the annotation anchor: the move from removal-or-nothing to a conditional that holds two values at once. The teacher quotes the Turn 5 phrase, attributes it to the Turn 4 reframe, and marks Turns 4 to 5 as the reusable model. The opening "just remove it" is cut, not because it is wrong, but because it carries no reasoning a future class can learn from.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

Keep a two-column note while you read a transcript: the left column names the reasoning move you think occurred, the right column holds the exact line that proves it. A strong reader of an ethical dialogue can say not only that reasoning improved but where, triggered by what, and at what cost (which value the student set aside to hold another). If your note cannot survive a colleague asking "show me the line," it is an impression, not an annotation.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A strong transcript annotation lets a mentor teacher reconstruct your judgment without you in the room. It marks the shift turn, quotes the trigger, names the cause, and states the reuse decision with a reason. Polish (color-coding, a tidy summary) comes last. If the shift turn is missing or unquoted, mark the gap and ask BigAL for a side-by-side of a weak and strong annotation of the same excerpt rather than a general explanation of "good discussion."

A useful closing line for the artifact sounds like this: "I anchored the annotation at Turn 3 because that is where the student first weighed a competing burden, quoted in the line itself; I cut Turn 1 because it stated a position without reasoning a future class could model."

## Transfer Case

Move the same dialogue method to a setting with no AI transcript: a live Socratic circle you can only record by hand. The purpose holds (find and reuse the reasoning shift), but the constraint changes hard. Without a verbatim record, you must annotate in real time, which means you can capture fewer turns and must decide on the spot which one to write down. That trade tells you whether you actually understand the method or were leaning on the transcript to do the noticing for you.
