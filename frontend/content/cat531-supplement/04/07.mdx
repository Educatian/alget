# Adapting Ethobot Prompts

![Adapting Ethobot Prompts textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/04-07-adapting-ethobot-prompts.png)

## Why This Section Matters

A default AI prompt is written for an imaginary average class that does not exist in any real room. "Conduct a Socratic dialogue probing the ethics of surveillance" assumes students who already know what Socratic means, who can engage an abstraction, and who feel a personal stake in a policy phrased in the third person. Drop that prompt into a 9th-grade class of English learners and you get one-word answers, not because the students lack ideas but because the prompt blocked access to them. Adapting the prompt is how a teacher closes the gap between a tool's defaults and the actual learners in front of her, and doing it well is a precise instructional skill, not a matter of "simplifying."

The danger that runs alongside adaptation is steering. The easiest way to get richer-looking output is to bake your own opinion into the prompt, at which point the bot stops drawing out student reasoning and starts echoing yours. A defensible adaptation lowers the barrier to participation without tilting the conclusion, and the way you prove you did that is by annotating a before-and-after transcript: the same students, the same topic, with evidence that the change produced their reasoning rather than your answer in their mouths.

## Learning Targets

- Diagnose why a default prompt failed: jargon, abstraction, or missing personal stakes, each with a different fix.
- Replace opaque framing with a concrete scenario that gives every student an entry point.
- Constrain the bot's move (one probing follow-up) and add a language scaffold without scaffolding the verdict.
- Show the effect with a before-and-after transcript, and name the change you rejected because it would steer the conclusion.

## Opening Case

A teacher runs the default Ethobot prompt, "Conduct a Socratic dialogue probing the ethics of surveillance in schools," with a 9th-grade class where most students are English learners. The transcript is a wall of one-word answers. It would be easy to read that as disengagement or low ability. Reading it as a prompt failure points to the actual problem: the words "Socratic" and "probing" are opaque, and "surveillance in schools" is an abstraction with no personal hook. The fix is not to lower expectations but to rewrite the prompt so the same students can reach the same ideas, then to prove with a re-run transcript that the rewrite, not luck, made the difference.

## Core Concept

Adapting a prompt is targeted redesign, and the target depends on why the default failed. There are three common failure modes, each with its own remedy:

| Failure mode | Symptom | Remedy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Jargon | Students stall on the prompt's own words | Replace academic terms with plain ones |
| Abstraction | Correct but flat, impersonal answers | Anchor the question in a concrete scenario |
| No stakes | Polite participation, no investment | Make the prompt name a person who is affected |

Two design moves do most of the work. The first is to swap abstraction for a concrete scenario: not "the ethics of surveillance" but "your school is thinking about cameras in the hallway, who would feel safer and who would feel watched." The second is to constrain the bot's behavior, not the student's answer: instruct the bot to ask one follow-up that makes the student name a person affected, then stop, so it deepens reasoning without flooding or leading.

The line you must not cross is scaffolding the verdict. Adding a language support ("if the student gives a one-word answer, ask for an example from their own school day") helps students reason; adding your opinion ("guide the student to see that surveillance harms trust") tells them what to conclude. The first is adaptation; the second is steering, and a re-run transcript is how you check which one you did.

The course anchors apply sharply. **Critical edtech studies** is the lens that flags the steering risk: a prompt engineered toward a "correct" ethical answer trains compliance, not reasoning. **TPACK** asks whether your adaptation still serves the content goal (ethical reasoning) rather than just producing longer transcripts. **Teacher professional judgment** is what decides which failure mode you are seeing and what to change, since the bot cannot diagnose its own prompt.

## Worked Example: Adapting a Bot Prompt for a Specific Class

The default Ethobot prompt may not fit every class. Here a teacher adapts a prompt for a 9th-grade class where most students are English learners, then annotates the effect. Work it as numbered before/after steps.

Default Ethobot prompt: "Conduct a Socratic dialogue probing the ethics of surveillance in schools."

What went wrong in the first run:

1. Students gave one-word answers; the prompt's vocabulary (Socratic, probing) was opaque.
2. The abstract framing ("surveillance in schools") drew no personal stakes.

Step-by-step prompt adaptation:

- Step 1. Replace jargon with a concrete scenario. New opener: "Your school is thinking about cameras in the hallway. Who would feel safer, and who would feel watched?"
- Step 2. Constrain the move, not the answer. Add: "Ask one follow-up that makes the student name a person affected, then stop."
- Step 3. Add a scaffold for language, not for the verdict. "If the student gives a one-word answer, ask: can you give an example from your own school day?"
- Step 4. Annotate the change in a re-run transcript. Mark where richer reasoning appeared compared to the default run.

Before (weak adaptation note): "I simplified the prompt and it worked better."

After (strong): "Adapted the prompt by swapping 'Socratic dialogue probing surveillance' for a concrete hallway-camera scenario and adding a one-follow-up scaffold for one-word answers. Evidence it helped: in the re-run, Turn 3 moved from 'cameras bad' to 'my little brother would feel watched but the front office would feel safer.' The change I rejected was adding the teacher's own opinion to the prompt, because that would steer the verdict."

The strong note states the exact prompt edit, shows transcript evidence of the effect, and records a rejected change, which a colleague can audit.

## Decision Rule

Before you keep an adapted prompt, run three checks:

1. **Diagnosis:** Did you name the failure mode (jargon, abstraction, stakes) before changing the prompt?
2. **Open, not steered:** Does the change lower the barrier to participation without telling students what to conclude?
3. **Evidence:** Does a before-and-after transcript show the change, in a student's own words, produced the richer reasoning?

If your evidence is "it felt better," you have a hunch; the re-run transcript is what turns it into a defensible adaptation.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading equates adaptation with simplification, as if shorter words alone fix a flat dialogue; often the real failure is missing stakes, and a simpler abstraction is still an abstraction. The second, more serious error is treating a more agreeable transcript as proof of success when the agreement was manufactured by a prompt that told students what to say. Richer output that all converges on your opinion is a warning sign, not a win.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the diagnosis to a different misfire. A teacher's default prompt, "Debate the trolley problem and justify your utilitarian reasoning," produces blank stares in a 7th-grade class. Diagnose: the failure is jargon ("utilitarian") plus an abstraction with no personal hook. Adapt: "Your bus can only pick up one of two groups before the bell, the soccer team or the band, and both will be late if you choose wrong. Who do you pick, and what makes it fair?" Constrain the bot to one follow-up that asks the student to name who loses. Add a scaffold for one-word answers. Then re-run and annotate: if Turn 4 moves from "the soccer team" to "the band, because they have a concert tonight and the team just has practice, so the band loses more," the adaptation worked, and the warrant is the student's own comparative reasoning, not a verdict you supplied. The same diagnose-adapt-prove cycle handles any prompt that lands flat.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

Keep a two-column note: the prompt change on the left, the transcript evidence of its effect on the right, quoted from a student. A strong reader of an adaptation can name the failure mode it addressed and point to the line in the re-run that proves the fix, and can also name the tempting change they rejected to avoid steering. If your note claims improvement without a quoted before-and-after, you have not yet shown the adaptation did anything.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A strong adaptation note states the diagnosed failure mode, the exact prompt edit, transcript evidence of the effect in a student's words, and the steering change you rejected and why. Polish is the last layer. If the note lacks before-and-after evidence, mark the gap and ask BigAL to compare your adapted prompt against one that subtly steers, so you can see the line you are protecting.

A useful closing line for the artifact sounds like this: "I replaced the abstract surveillance framing with a hallway-camera scenario to fix a missing-stakes failure; the re-run shows a student moving from 'cameras bad' to naming who feels watched and who feels safer, and I rejected adding my own opinion to the prompt because it would have produced agreement instead of reasoning."

## Transfer Case

Move the adaptation skill to a class on the opposite end: gifted seniors who find the concrete hallway scenario too easy and disengage from its simplicity. Here the failure mode flips from "no access" to "no challenge," and the fix is to add complexity (a budget constraint, a competing legal requirement) rather than to simplify. Working this version tests whether you adapt by diagnosing the specific learners in front of you, or whether "adapt" has quietly come to mean only "make it simpler."
