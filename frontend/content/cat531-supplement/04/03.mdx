## Why This Section Matters

Every school AI tool is purchased by someone and used on someone, and those are rarely the same person. A district administrator signs the contract, a teacher runs the tool, and a student, often the one with the least voice in the room, absorbs whatever the tool gets wrong. Stakeholder analysis is the discipline of naming everyone with something at stake before the decision is made, especially the parties whose interests are structurally easy to overlook. Skip it and you will design for the people who are present in the meeting, which is exactly the wrong set.

The move that separates real analysis from a polite list is finding the silent stakeholder: the person most affected by the tool who is least likely to be consulted about it. In school AI this is almost always a student in a marginalized position, an English learner, a student with a disability, a student whose data is most sensitive. A stakeholder map that surfaces that person and asks whose interest the design currently centers is a defensible artifact; a map that lists "students, teachers, parents" and stops is decoration.

## Learning Targets

- List the full stakeholder set for a school AI proposal, distinguishing buyers, operators, subjects, and gatekeepers (the data officer, the vendor).
- Name each stakeholder's actual interest, including conflicts (the vendor wants adoption and data; parents want privacy).
- Identify the silent stakeholder: the most-affected party least likely to be in the decision.
- State whose interest the current design centers, and propose a move that re-centers it toward the most-affected student.

## Opening Case

A school plans to adopt an AI writing-feedback tutor that grades and comments on essays automatically. In the adoption meeting are the principal, two department heads, and the vendor's sales engineer. Everyone in the room benefits from "yes": the principal gets a modernization win, the teachers get time back, the vendor gets a contract and a stream of student writing to train on. The students whose essays the model will misread, particularly English learners whose phrasing the grammar engine flags as error rather than dialect, are not in the room and will not be asked. The whole point of a stakeholder map is to put that absent student on the page before the decision hardens.

## Core Concept

A stakeholder analysis for school AI maps every party with an interest in the tool, makes their conflicting interests explicit, and then asks the uncomfortable question of whose interest the design actually serves. Three distinctions keep the map honest.

First, separate roles. A single tool has buyers (who decide), operators (who run it), subjects (whom it acts on), and gatekeepers (who must approve, like a district data officer). These roles have different power and different exposure, and conflating them hides the imbalance.

Second, name interests as they actually are, including the ones nobody says aloud. The vendor's interest is adoption and access to student data; that is not sinister, but it is not neutral, and a map that lists the vendor as "provides the tool" has erased the part that matters.

**Legal anchor.** The vendor's data interest and the parent-consent move both have statutory hooks, not just ethical ones: FERPA governs disclosure of student education records and the parental-consent requirement behind any consent step, and COPPA governs data collection from students under 13. The FTC's 2025 COPPA amendments tighten this further, limiting data retention and requiring separate verifiable parental consent before children's data is disclosed to third parties, including for model training, as of 2026.

Third, find the silent stakeholder by asking: who is most affected and least consulted? In school AI the answer correlates almost perfectly with who has the least institutional power. The English learner whose syntax the model scores as error, the student in special education whose accommodations the tool ignores, the family without the literacy to read a consent form, these are the people the design will fail quietly.

The course anchors do pointed work. **Critical edtech studies** is the lens that insists you ask who benefits and who is burdened, rather than assuming a tool that helps "students" helps all students equally. **[AI and education datafication](https://doi.org/10.1080/17439884.2020.1686017)** names the vendor's data interest as a stake rather than a service. **Teacher professional judgment** is what lets you, the operator who actually sees the students, speak for the silent stakeholder in a room where no one else will.

**Recent evidence.** The English-learner concern is documented, not speculative: peer-reviewed work has directly investigated bias in automated essay scores for elementary-age English learners (Wilson & Huang, 2024), and a systematic review of automated essay scoring reports recurring construct-irrelevant variance and fairness limitations across systems (Ramesh & Sanampudi, 2022), supporting the claim that grammar models can score non-standard phrasing as error rather than dialect or developmental form.

## Worked Example: Mapping Stakeholders Around an AI Tutoring Pilot

A school plans to adopt an AI writing-feedback tutor. Before deciding, a teacher maps who has a stake and what each one stands to gain or lose. Work it as numbered judgment calls.

The proposed pilot: a vendor AI grades and comments on student essays automatically.

Step-by-step stakeholder map:

- Step 1. List the stakeholders, not just the buyers. Students, parents, teachers, the vendor, the district data officer, and English-learner support staff.
- Step 2. Name each one's interest. Students want faster feedback; teachers want time back; the vendor wants adoption and data; parents want privacy; the data officer wants compliance; EL staff want feedback that does not penalize non-standard syntax.
- Step 3. Find the silent stakeholder. English learners are affected most yet are rarely consulted; the AI may score their phrasing as error rather than dialect or developmental form.
- Step 4. Decide whose interest the design currently centers. Right now the design centers teacher time and vendor adoption, not the most-affected students.

Before (weak): "Teachers and students are the stakeholders, and the tool helps both." This lists only the visible, aligned parties.

After (strong): "Six stakeholders, with conflicting interests. The silent stakeholder is English learners, whose phrasing the AI may misread as error. The current design centers teacher time and vendor adoption. Negotiated move: pilot only with a teacher override on EL essays and a parent consent step. Evidence for the EL concern = the vendor's grammar model has no dialect setting."

The strong map names interests, surfaces the silent stakeholder, and states whose interest the design centers, which a colleague can audit. (The EL concern cited as evidence is itself evidence-backed: see Wilson & Huang, 2024, on automated essay-score bias for English learners.)

## Decision Rule

Before approving any school AI tool, work the map in this order:

1. **Roster everyone:** buyers, operators, subjects, gatekeepers, and the vendor, each as a distinct line.
2. **Name interests honestly,** including the data and adoption interests no one volunteers.
3. **Find the silent stakeholder** by asking who is most affected and least consulted.
4. **Re-center:** state whose interest the design currently serves and one concrete move that shifts it toward the most-affected student.

If your map has no silent stakeholder, you have not finished; you have listed the people who were already in the room.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is treating "stakeholders" as a synonym for "people who like the tool," which produces a map of aligned interests and zero tension. The second is writing the analysis after the decision, as a justification, rather than before it, as a check. A map built to defend a choice already made will conveniently never find a silent stakeholder, because finding one would reopen the question.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply it to an attendance-prediction tool that flags students "at risk of dropping out." Roster the parties: district (buyer), counselors (operators), students (subjects), the data officer (gatekeeper), the vendor. Name interests: the district wants a measurable intervention metric; counselors want early warning; the vendor wants the predictive contract and the historical data to refine its model. Now find the silent stakeholder: the flagged student, who never consented to being scored and may be tracked into lowered expectations by a label they cannot see or contest. Whose interest does the design center? The district's metric and the vendor's model, not the student's right to be treated as more than a risk score. The re-centering move: make every flag generate a private counselor conversation, never an automated placement, and give families the right to know and contest the label. The mechanics are identical to the writing-tutor case; only the silent stakeholder's specific harm changes. This harm is documented, not hypothetical: Wisconsin's Dropout Early Warning System was found to raise false alarms about Black and Hispanic students at significantly higher rates than white students, to be wrong about three-quarters of the time when predicting non-graduation, and to attach color-coded "high risk" labels students could not see or contest (The Markup, 2023; see also Yu et al., 2021, on fairness audits of dropout-prediction models).

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

Keep a two-column note: each stakeholder on the left, their actual (not stated) interest on the right, and circle the row for the party who is most affected and least present. A strong reader can answer "whose interest does this design center right now" with a name, not a hope, and can propose one move that shifts the center. If your note lists only parties whose interests align, you have not found the conflict that makes the decision a judgment.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A strong stakeholder map names roles distinctly, states interests including the unspoken ones, surfaces a silent stakeholder, and proposes a concrete re-centering move with evidence. Tidy formatting is the last layer. If the map has no conflicting interests, mark the gap and ask BigAL to help you locate the party your list is protecting by omission rather than for a general explanation of stakeholders.

A useful closing line for the artifact sounds like this: "The design currently centers teacher time and vendor adoption; I re-centered it on English learners with a required teacher override on flagged EL essays, because the vendor's model has no dialect setting and would otherwise penalize developmental syntax as error."

## Transfer Case

Move the analysis to a tool you personally like and want to adopt: a discussion-prompt generator you find genuinely useful. The challenge is that wanting the "yes" makes you a biased mapper, prone to not looking too hard for a silent stakeholder. Working this version honestly tests whether stakeholder analysis is a method you run even against your own preference, or a ritual you perform only on tools you already distrust.
