# Facial Recognition as Classroom Case

![Facial Recognition as Classroom Case textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/04-02-facial-recognition-as-classroom-case.png)

## Why This Section Matters

Facial recognition is the cleanest case study in the module because the trade it forces is so concrete. A vendor promises measurable time savings; independent testing shows the same system fails more often on darker-skinned faces; and the data it collects (a face template) is biometric and durable. A teacher who can reason through this one case can reason through almost any classroom AI proposal, because it contains all three pressures (efficiency, equity, privacy) in a single decision and refuses to let you satisfy all three at once.

What makes this a judgment problem rather than a slogan is that "facial recognition is biased, so no" is not yet a defensible decision. It names one pole and skips the negotiation a colleague or principal will demand. The artifact you build, a tension map with a negotiated decision, exists precisely to show that you weighed the real benefit against the documented harm and the hidden data cost, and arrived somewhere you can defend with evidence.

## Learning Targets

- Separate the three distinct pressures in an automated-attendance proposal: instructional time, demographic accuracy, and biometric data retention.
- Read a vendor accuracy claim critically by asking for error rates broken out by subgroup, not a single aggregate number.
- Build a tension map that attaches specific evidence to each pole rather than asserting a verdict.
- State a negotiated decision with a re-entry condition, so the door is not simply closed but conditioned on measurable improvement.

## Opening Case

A district pilots facial-recognition check-in to cut hallway tardies, and a teacher is asked to weigh in before the building-wide rollout. The pitch is appealing: ten minutes per period back for instruction. But the teacher knows two facts the pitch omits. The vendor's own documentation shows higher false-non-match rates for darker-skinned students, which means some students would be marked absent while standing in the doorway. And the face templates sit on a vendor server for two years. The decision is not "is this cool" but "which value do I protect, and can I name a condition under which the trade would change."

## Core Concept

A defensible facial-recognition decision treats the proposal as a set of competing values with evidence attached, not as a single feature to approve or reject. The mistake to avoid is collapsing the case to its most salient pole. "It saves time" hides the equity cost; "it's biased" hides the privacy cost and the real instructional benefit. A tension map keeps all the poles visible at once.

There are three distinct pressures, and they do not trade against each other on the same scale:

- **Efficiency** is measured in recovered instructional minutes. Real, but bounded and substitutable (a manual roster also works).
- **Equity** is measured in differential error rates across groups. A system that is 99 percent accurate overall can still be 90 percent accurate for one subgroup, and that subgroup absorbs the entire cost.
- **Privacy** is measured in what data is collected, where it lives, and for how long. A face template is biometric: unlike a password, a student cannot change their face if the data leaks.

The course anchors do specific work here. **Critical edtech studies** is what surfaces the equity pole, because aggregate accuracy claims are designed to make subgroup failure invisible; you have to ask for the breakdown. **AI and education datafication** is what surfaces the privacy pole, naming the 24-month retention as a standing risk rather than a footnote. **Teacher professional judgment** is what produces the negotiated decision, the part no policy template can write for you: which value you protect now, and what evidence would justify revisiting.

## Worked Example: Mapping the Facial-Recognition Tension

A district piloted facial-recognition check-in to cut hallway tardies. A teacher must build a Design Tension Studio map naming the value conflict and a negotiated decision. Work it as numbered judgment calls.

The case facts:

1. Stated benefit: attendance logging is automated, saving roughly 10 minutes per class period.
2. Reported error: the vendor's accuracy drops for darker-skinned students, producing more false non-matches.
3. Data fact: face templates are retained on a vendor server for 24 months.

Step-by-step tension map:

- Step 1. Name the poles. Pole A = instructional time recovered. Pole B = equitable, accurate treatment of every student.
- Step 2. Attach evidence to each pole. Pole A evidence = the 10-minute saving. Pole B evidence = the documented accuracy gap by skin tone.
- Step 3. Find the hidden third pole. Data retention (24 months on a vendor server) is a privacy tension the time-versus-equity framing hides.
- Step 4. State the negotiated decision, not a winner. "Use manual check-in for attendance; do not deploy facial recognition until the vendor shows parity across skin tones and 24-month retention is reduced."

Before (weak): "Facial recognition is biased so we should not use it." This is a verdict with one pole and no negotiation.

After (strong): "The map weighs 10 minutes saved against a measured accuracy gap that falls hardest on darker-skinned students, plus 24-month vendor retention. Negotiated decision: keep manual check-in now; revisit only if the vendor demonstrates parity and shorter retention. Evidence for the equity pole = the vendor's own error-rate breakdown."

The strong map names both poles, attaches evidence, surfaces the privacy pole, and records a negotiated decision a reviewer can audit.

## Decision Rule

For any biometric classroom tool, run the poles in this order before you decide:

1. **Equity first:** Ask for error rates by subgroup. If the vendor gives only an aggregate, treat the gap as unknown and therefore unacceptable until shown.
2. **Privacy second:** Name what is collected, where it is stored, and the retention period. Biometric data raises the bar because it cannot be reissued.
3. **Efficiency last:** Quantify the benefit honestly, then ask whether a non-biometric method captures most of it.

A negotiated decision must include a re-entry condition: the specific, measurable thing that would have to change for the answer to flip.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is trusting a single accuracy number. "99 percent accurate" is a marketing average that can conceal a steep subgroup failure, and the students who absorb that failure are usually the ones with the least power to complain. The second is treating efficiency as a value on the same footing as equity. Saved minutes are recoverable by other means; a misidentified student or a leaked face template is not. Ranking them flat is itself the error.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the reasoning to a different biometric pitch. A vendor offers fingerprint scanning for lunch-line payment, sold as faster lines and no lost cards. Run the poles. Equity: fingerprint readers have known higher failure rates for young children and for worn or damaged prints, so some kids get held up daily, in front of peers, at the lunch line. Privacy: fingerprints are biometric and the contract stores them with a third-party payment processor. Efficiency: real, but a PIN pad captures most of the speed gain without biometric data. The negotiated decision writes itself once the poles are visible: adopt the PIN pad, decline the fingerprint reader, and name the re-entry condition (an on-device, no-third-party storage model with documented parity for young children). The same three-pole move that handled facial recognition handles this with no new theory.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

Keep a two-column note: each pole on the left, the exact evidence on the right (the subgroup error figure, the retention clause, the measured time saving). A strong reader of a facial-recognition case can state which pole the current design centers and which one absorbs the cost, and can name the one fact that would change the decision. If your note has a verdict but only one pole with evidence, you have an opinion, not a map.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A strong tension map shows both poles with evidence attached, surfaces the hidden privacy pole, and ends in a negotiated decision with a re-entry condition, not a winner-take-all verdict. Visual neatness is the last layer. If a pole has no evidence beside it, mark the gap and ask BigAL for the vendor-documentation question you should be asking rather than a general explanation of bias.

A useful closing line for the artifact sounds like this: "I centered the equity pole because the vendor's own breakdown shows the cost falls on darker-skinned students; I held the door open with a parity-and-retention condition rather than a permanent ban, so the decision stays evidence-driven."

## Transfer Case

Move the case to a small rural school where the principal argues the demographic homogeneity makes the accuracy gap "not really a problem here." That setting changes the equity calculus but does not erase it: the privacy pole is unchanged (templates still leave the building), and a single misidentified student in a small school is more, not less, visible. Working that version tests whether you treat equity as a checklist item that small numbers can wave away, or as a structural question about who bears the cost when a system fails.
