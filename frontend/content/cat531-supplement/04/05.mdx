## Why This Section Matters

Section 01 asked you to find where reasoning shifted. This section asks the harder, more transferable question: would another teacher, reading the same transcript with your method, mark the same place? That is the difference between a hunch and a method. Highlighting a turn in yellow and writing "good thinking" records your reaction; it cannot be checked, reproduced, or defended. A coding scheme, a small set of defined labels you apply consistently turn by turn, converts a private intuition into evidence two readers can agree on. This is the move that lets transcript annotation function as assessment rather than impression.

The reason this matters for a teacher specifically is fairness. If your annotation method is subjective, then the students whose phrasing happens to match your taste get credited with "reasoning" while others do not, and you would never know. A defined scheme with written warrants forces you to state, for each coded turn, the observable feature that earned the code, which is the same discipline that makes any rubric defensible to a parent, a colleague, or yourself a week later when the memory of the lesson has faded.

**Recent evidence.** The bias mechanism is documented, not assumed: language models and the judgment systems built on them have been shown to penalize non-standard English dialects, including African American English, judging the same reasoning less favorably when its phrasing diverges from a "standard" (Fleisig et al., 2024; Hofmann et al., 2024). A defined, warrant-based scheme is the safeguard, because it ties credit to an observable reasoning move rather than to discourse style.

## Learning Targets

- Define a small, mutually exclusive code scheme (for example, claim, reframing question, reasoning revision) with criteria, not vibes.
- Apply the codes turn by turn and write a one-line warrant beside each high-value code.
- Estimate inter-rater agreement by asking whether a colleague using your definitions would code the same turn the same way, and tighten definitions where they would not.
- Distinguish reproducible coding from subjective highlighting, and explain why reproducibility is an equity safeguard.

## Opening Case

Two teachers annotate the same Ethobot transcript of a high-school debate on AI and cheating. The first highlights three turns and writes "strong reasoning." The second applies a three-code scheme and marks Turn 3 as a reasoning revision with the warrant "now conditions cheating on teacher permission." A department head asks both to justify their marks. The first can only say "it felt insightful." The second can hand over the scheme and the warrant and invite anyone to apply it and check. Only the second has produced something that survives scrutiny, and only the second has a method she can hand to a student teacher and trust them to use the same way.

## Core Concept

Transcript annotation becomes a method when it rests on a code scheme: a short list of labels with definitions clear enough that a second rater would assign them the same way. The aim is not elaborate taxonomy but reproducibility. Three design principles keep a scheme usable.

First, keep the codes few and mutually exclusive. A scheme of three or four labels (a fixed claim, a question that reframes, a turn that revises a prior position) is applied consistently; a scheme of fifteen overlapping labels is applied differently every time, which destroys agreement.

Second, attach a warrant to every high-value code. The warrant is the one-line observable reason the turn earned the label: not "good reasoning" but "revises the categorical claim by adding a condition." The warrant is what a second rater checks against, and what makes your call inspectable.

Third, test for agreement and tighten where it fails. If you imagine a colleague applying your definitions and suspect they would code a turn differently, the definition is too loose. The fix is to sharpen the criterion until the disagreement disappears, which is the practical meaning of reliability.

The course anchors apply directly. **Teacher professional judgment** is exercised in defining the scheme and writing warrants; the platform cannot decide for you what counts as a reasoning revision. **Critical edtech studies** (Selwyn; see Pangrazio & Selwyn, 2023) warns that a scheme can smuggle in bias, rewarding the discourse style of dominant-culture students, so the criteria must describe reasoning moves, not eloquence. **[AI and education datafication](https://doi.org/10.1080/17439884.2020.1686017)** reminds you that an Ethobot transcript is data about minors; a reproducible scheme makes your use of that data accountable rather than arbitrary.

## Worked Example: Building and Applying an Annotation Code Scheme

This section is about the annotation method itself: a small, consistent code scheme a teacher can apply turn by turn so two readers would agree. Work it as numbered steps on the excerpt below.

Transcript excerpt (Ethobot 3.2, high-school AI-cheating debate):

1. Student: "Using AI to write is always cheating."
2. Ethobot: "Does it matter whether the teacher allowed it?"
3. Student: "If the teacher allowed it then it is not cheating, so the rule depends on the assignment."
4. Student: "But what about a student who cannot afford a tutor and uses AI instead?"

Step-by-step coding:

- Step 1. Define a 3-code scheme. CL = fixed claim, QU = a question that reframes, RE = reasoning that revises the prior position.
- Step 2. Apply codes per turn. Turn 1 = CL. Turn 2 = QU. Turn 3 = RE (revises the categorical claim). Turn 4 = RE (introduces an equity dimension).
- Step 3. Write the warrant beside each RE code. Turn 3 warrant = "now conditions cheating on teacher permission." Turn 4 warrant = "now weighs access and fairness."
- Step 4. Check inter-rater agreement. Ask whether a colleague applying the same scheme would code Turn 3 as RE. If the codes are subjective, tighten the definitions.

Before (weak): highlighting Turns 3 and 4 in yellow with the note "good thinking here."

After (strong): "Codes: T1 CL, T2 QU, T3 RE (warrant: cheating now conditioned on permission), T4 RE (warrant: equity of access introduced). Scheme is the CL/QU/RE rubric above so another rater can reproduce the coding. The QU at T2 is what triggered both RE turns."

The strong annotation uses a reusable code scheme with warrants, not subjective highlighting, so the judgment is reproducible.

Match each code in the scheme to the observable feature that earns it. A scheme is only reproducible when the definitions are this precise.

## Decision Rule

Before an annotation counts as evidence, it must pass three checks:

1. **Defined scheme:** Are the codes few, mutually exclusive, and defined by observable criteria?
2. **Warranted codes:** Does each high-value code carry a one-line reason a second reader could verify?
3. **Agreement:** Would a colleague using your definitions code the contested turns the same way?

If a code rests on "it felt like reasoning," sharpen the criterion until the feeling becomes a feature.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is mistaking highlighting for coding: color marks where you looked, not why, and carries no criterion anyone can check. The second is over-engineering the scheme. A teacher who builds a twelve-code taxonomy will apply it inconsistently and lose the very agreement the scheme was meant to create; three or four well-defined codes beat twelve fuzzy ones every time.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the method to a middle-school transcript on whether classmates should report a friend who plagiarized. Define the scheme: CL (fixed stance), QU (reframing question), RE (revision of prior stance). Code the turns: a student opens "You always report cheating" (CL); Ethobot asks "What do you owe the friend, and what do you owe the class?" (QU); the student says "Maybe I talk to the friend first, then report only if it keeps happening" (RE, warrant: "introduces a graded loyalty-versus-fairness response"). Now run the agreement check: would a colleague also code that last turn RE? Yes, because the warrant points to an observable feature (the addition of a condition), not to tone. Had the warrant read "sounded thoughtful," a second rater could not confirm it, and the code would fail. The same three-code scheme that handled the cheating debate handles this with no modification, which is the point of building a method rather than reacting to each transcript fresh.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

Keep a two-column note: the code on the left, the verbatim warrant on the right, and flag any code whose warrant you could not defend to a skeptical colleague. A strong reader of a transcript can hand over the scheme and predict, with confidence, how someone else would code the same turns. If your warrants describe how a turn sounded rather than what it did, the scheme is not yet reliable.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A strong annotation includes the code scheme with definitions, codes every turn, warrants the high-value codes with observable reasons, and notes where agreement is shaky. Formatting is the last layer. If a code has no checkable warrant, mark the gap and ask TeachGen@i to compare your definition against a tighter one rather than for a general explanation of coding.

A useful closing line for the artifact sounds like this: "I coded Turn 3 as a reasoning revision with the warrant 'conditions cheating on teacher permission,' an observable feature a second rater can confirm; I rejected color-highlighting because it would have recorded where I looked without a criterion anyone could reproduce."

## Transfer Case

Move the scheme from an ethics debate to a math-reasoning transcript, where the "reasoning revision" you are looking for is a student abandoning a wrong procedure after a probing question. The codes' names hold but their criteria must be rewritten for the new domain: a revision in math looks like a corrected step, not a reframed value. Working this version tests whether you grasp the underlying idea (define, warrant, check agreement) or only memorized the CL/QU/RE labels from the ethics case.
