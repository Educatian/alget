# Moving From Debate to Policy

![Moving From Debate to Policy textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/04-06-moving-from-debate-to-policy.png)

## Why This Section Matters

A good classroom debate generates heat, conviction, and a dozen competing positions. None of that helps you on Monday morning when a student actually turns in an AI-assisted essay and asks whether it counts. Debate clarifies values; it does not produce a rule you can apply consistently to thirty students. This section is about the translation step, converting the energy and the disagreements of a debate into a policy that is observable, enforceable, and fair, which is a genuinely different skill from arguing well. Many teachers are strong at the first and surprisingly weak at the second.

The translation is hard because it forces choices a debate lets you dodge. A debate can hold "AI helps stuck students" and "AI is unfair if only some use it" in tension forever; a policy has to do something about both at once. Worse, a policy has to confront enforcement reality (you usually cannot reliably detect AI use), which means the rule you can actually run may differ from the rule you wish you could. Naming that gap honestly, and designing around it, is what makes a policy defensible rather than aspirational.

## Learning Targets

- Extract the shared value beneath competing debate positions rather than picking the loudest claim.
- Rewrite a vague principle ("use AI responsibly") as an observable rule a colleague could apply the same way.
- Match the enforcement mechanism to reality: base a rule on disclosure when detection is unreliable.
- Build in an equity exception so the rule does not silently penalize students with less access.

## Opening Case

A class debates "Should students be allowed to use AI on take-home essays?" and produces three sharp claims: AI helps students who get stuck, it is unfair if only some students use it, and you cannot tell who used it anyway. The teacher could write "Our policy is to use AI ethically and responsibly," which sounds finished and decides nothing. Or she could treat the three claims as raw material: a shared value (fairness of access and transparency), an enforcement fact (detection is unreliable), and a constraint (some students lack home internet). The second path produces a rule she can apply to an actual essay; the first produces a sentence she will reinterpret differently for every student who tests it.

## Core Concept

Moving from debate to policy is a four-move translation, and skipping any move leaves you with a slogan instead of a rule.

The first move is to extract the shared value, not the dominant voice. Beneath "AI helps stuck students" and "it's unfair if only some use it" sits a common concern with fairness of access. A policy built on the shared value resolves the disagreement; a policy built on the loudest claim merely takes a side.

The second move is to make the rule observable. "Use AI responsibly" cannot be applied consistently because two teachers will read "responsibly" differently. "You may use AI to brainstorm and to check grammar, but not to generate submitted paragraphs, and you must cite any AI use in a footnote" can be checked against an actual essay.

The third move is to match enforcement to reality. If detection is unreliable, a rule that depends on catching violators is theater. The honest design bases compliance on disclosure rather than surveillance, which is both more enforceable and more respectful.

The fourth move is to write an equity exception. A rule that assumes home internet quietly punishes students who lack it; providing class-time access keeps the rule from converting a resource gap into a discipline problem.

The course anchors steer the translation. **Teacher professional judgment** is what extracts the shared value and decides what "observable" means in your room. **Critical edtech studies** is what catches the hidden equity penalty before it becomes policy. **AI and education datafication** informs the enforcement-reality move, since "AI detectors" are themselves unreliable, often biased tools whose error rates fall hardest on non-native writers.

## Worked Example: Turning a Class Debate Into an Enforceable Rule

A class debated "Should students be allowed to use AI on take-home essays?" The debate produced strong opinions but no rule a teacher could apply on Monday. This section converts debate into policy. Work it as numbered steps.

What the debate produced:

1. "AI helps students who get stuck."
2. "It is unfair if some students use it and some do not."
3. "You cannot tell who used it anyway."

Step-by-step debate-to-policy conversion:

- Step 1. Extract the shared value, not the loudest claim. The points converge on fairness of access and transparency.
- Step 2. Write a rule that is observable. Vague: "Use AI responsibly." Observable: "You may use AI to brainstorm and check grammar, but not to generate full paragraphs; cite any AI use in a footnote."
- Step 3. Name the enforcement reality. Since detection is unreliable (point 3), base the rule on disclosure, not surveillance.
- Step 4. State the exception path. A student without home internet may use class time, so the rule does not punish lack of access.

Before (weak): "Our policy is to use AI ethically and responsibly." This is unenforceable and hides the disagreements.

After (strong): "Policy: AI permitted for brainstorming and grammar, not for generating submitted text; all AI use disclosed in a footnote; enforced by disclosure not detection because detection is unreliable; class-time access provided so the rule does not penalize students without home internet. This resolves the fairness and transparency tensions the debate raised."

The strong policy is observable, names how it is enforced, and includes an equity exception, which a colleague could apply consistently.

## Decision Rule

Before a policy leaves your desk, run the four-move test:

1. **Shared value:** Does the rule rest on the value beneath the debate, not on whoever argued hardest?
2. **Observable:** Could two teachers apply this rule to the same essay and reach the same call?
3. **Enforceable as written:** Does the mechanism match reality, leaning on disclosure where detection fails?
4. **Equity exception:** Does the rule avoid punishing students for a resource gap they did not choose?

A policy that fails the observable test is a slogan; one that fails the equity test will quietly fall hardest on the students with the least.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is writing a rule that sounds principled but cannot be applied: "act with integrity" passes no observable test and so means whatever each teacher decides in the moment. The second is reaching for surveillance to enforce what disclosure should handle, trusting an AI detector whose false positives land disproportionately on English learners and produce exactly the unfairness the policy was meant to prevent.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the four moves to a different debate: whether students may use AI image generators for a history poster project. The debate splits over creativity ("it lets weak artists shine") and authenticity ("it isn't their work"). Extract the shared value: the assignment is about historical understanding, not drawing skill. Make it observable: "AI-generated images are allowed if every image is captioned with the prompt used and a one-sentence historical justification." Match enforcement to reality: you cannot reliably tell AI art from stock art, so require the prompt disclosure rather than policing the image source. Write the equity exception: students without reliable home devices may generate images on classroom machines during a set period. The result is a rule a substitute teacher could apply to any poster in the stack, which a debate, however lively, never produces on its own.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

Keep a two-column note: each debate claim on the left, what it contributes to the policy on the right (a shared value, an enforcement fact, a constraint). A strong reader can show the path from three conflicting opinions to one applicable rule, and can point to the line that handles the enforcement gap and the line that protects access. If your policy reads like a value statement rather than a procedure, you have summarized the debate instead of resolving it.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A strong policy memo names the shared value, states an observable rule, declares the enforcement mechanism honestly, and includes an equity exception. Tone and formatting are the last layer. If the rule is not observable or has no equity exception, mark the gap and ask BigAL to test your rule against two borderline student cases rather than for a general explanation of policy.

A useful closing line for the artifact sounds like this: "I based enforcement on footnoted disclosure rather than AI detection because detectors misfire on English learners, and I added class-time access so the rule does not turn a home-internet gap into an academic-integrity charge."

## Transfer Case

Move the policy from a single classroom to a whole department, where you no longer control how each colleague interprets "observable." A rule clear enough for you may still drift across five teachers' judgment. Working this version tests whether your rule is truly observable or only observable-to-you, and it usually forces a worked example or two pasted right into the policy so colleagues converge on the same calls.
