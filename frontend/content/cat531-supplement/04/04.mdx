# Privacy and Consent With Minors

![Privacy and Consent With Minors textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/04-04-privacy-and-consent-with-minors.png)

## Why This Section Matters

Consent is the one place where good intentions are not enough, because minors cannot legally give it. A teacher who says "I told the students the chatbot would save their questions" has not obtained consent; she has issued a notice to people who lack the legal capacity to agree. The gap between notifying, assenting, and consenting is where most well-meaning classroom AI runs aground, and closing that gap is the core competency of this section. Privacy law treats children differently for a reason, and a defensible policy memo has to honor that difference in its design, not just its tone.

The stakes are higher with AI tools specifically because their default business model often runs against the student. Many vendors retain student inputs to train their models, sometimes for years, by default. A teacher who adopts the tool without changing that default has, in effect, enrolled minors in a data pipeline their families never agreed to. The consent clause you learn to write here is the mechanism that flips the default back toward the student.

## Learning Targets

- Distinguish notification, assent, and consent, and assign each to the correct party (student assents; parent or guardian consents).
- Specify what data an AI tool collects, the single purpose it is collected for, and what it is explicitly not used for.
- Choose opt-in with parental consent over opt-out, and explain why a minor family's silence is not agreement.
- Write a retention and deletion rule that overrides the vendor's default, and cite the vendor setting that made the clause necessary.

## Opening Case

A teacher wants to use an AI homework chatbot with her 6th-grade class. The tool stores each student's questions to "personalize" help, and the vendor's default retains those logs for 24 months and uses them to train the model. The teacher's instinct is to send a friendly note home: "Your child will be using a helpful AI tutor this term." That note feels responsible but does almost nothing legally or ethically. It does not name what data is collected, it does not ask for agreement, and it treats a non-response as a yes. This section is about turning that note into a consent clause that would survive a privacy officer's review.

## Core Concept

Privacy and consent with minors rest on three terms that are routinely blurred and must be kept separate:

| Term | Who acts | What it means | Legally sufficient alone? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Notification | Teacher tells | "Here is what will happen." | No |
| Assent | Minor agrees | The student's age-appropriate "yes." | No |
| Consent | Parent or guardian agrees | Legally binding permission for a minor. | Yes, when informed |

A defensible consent design does four things in order. It names both parties (the student assents, the guardian consents). It states the data and a single bounded purpose ("question text and timestamps, used only to alert the teacher to a struggling student, never to train the vendor model"). It sets the default to opt-in, because a family that does not return a form has not agreed to anything. And it sets a retention and deletion rule that overrides the vendor's, with a specific end date.

The course anchors are concrete here. **AI and education datafication** is the lens that exposes the vendor default as the real adversary: the 24-month retention and training-on-student-data setting is the thing your clause exists to override. **Critical edtech studies** reminds you that the families least able to parse a dense consent form are often the ones whose children are most exposed, so plain language is an equity requirement, not a courtesy. **Teacher professional judgment** is what bounds the purpose: you decide the data serves a teacher alert and nothing more, and you write that limit in.

## Worked Example: A Consent Clause for a Minors' AI Tool

A teacher drafts the consent section of a policy memo for a 6th-grade class chatbot. Minors cannot give legal consent, so the design of the consent step is a judgment call. Work it in numbered steps.

The tool: an AI homework chatbot that stores each student's questions to "personalize" help.

Step-by-step consent decision:

- Step 1. Separate assent from consent. Students (minors) can assent; the parent or guardian gives legal consent. The memo must name both.
- Step 2. State what data is collected and why. Question text and timestamps, used only to surface a struggling-student alert to the teacher, not to train the vendor model.
- Step 3. Set the default. Default to opt-in with parental consent, not opt-out, because silence from a minor's family is not consent.
- Step 4. Name the retention and deletion rule. Delete chat logs at the end of the grading term; do not let the vendor retain them.

Before (weak): "Parents will be notified that students use the chatbot." Notification is not consent, and it omits data use and retention.

After (strong): "Consent clause: parental opt-in required before any student account is created. Collected data = question text and timestamps, used only for a teacher struggling-student alert, never for vendor model training. Logs deleted at term end. Students give assent on a plain-language screen. Evidence the clause is needed = the vendor's default setting retains logs for 24 months and trains on them."

The strong clause separates assent from consent, names data use and retention, defaults to opt-in, and points to the vendor setting that made the clause necessary.

## Decision Rule

Before any minor uses an AI tool that records them, the consent design must satisfy four tests:

1. **Capacity:** Is legal consent sought from a guardian, with student assent as a separate, age-appropriate step?
2. **Purpose limitation:** Is the data tied to one named use and explicitly walled off from model training?
3. **Default:** Is the tool opt-in, so that no form returned means no account created?
4. **Retention:** Is there a deletion date that overrides the vendor default, with the vendor setting cited?

If any test fails, the tool is not ready, regardless of how useful it is.

## Common Misreadings

The most common error is mistaking notification for consent: telling families is not asking them, and assuming a yes from silence inverts the protection minors are owed. A subtler error is naming a vague purpose ("to improve learning") that quietly licenses almost any data use. Purpose limitation only protects students if the purpose is narrow enough to exclude the vendor's training pipeline by name.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the four tests to a different tool: an AI behavior-tracking app that logs which students are "off task" via webcam. Capacity: webcam observation of minors plainly requires guardian consent, not just a posted notice. Purpose limitation: the data must be tied to one use (a teacher's own classroom-management reflection) and explicitly barred from any disciplinary record or vendor analytics. Default: opt-in, with a non-camera alternative for families who decline, so opting out carries no penalty. Retention: footage deleted same-day, since standing video of children is a severe risk and the vendor's default cloud storage runs 90 days. Once you walk the tests, the honest conclusion may be that no consent design rescues this tool, because continuous webcam monitoring of minors fails the purpose-and-proportion test no matter how the clause is worded. Recognizing when the answer is "decline" is itself the competency.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

Keep a two-column note: each of the four tests on the left, the exact clause language that satisfies it on the right, with the vendor default it overrides in the margin. A strong reader can point to the line that turns the vendor's 24-month training default off, not just assert that privacy is protected. If your note says "we got consent" without naming who consented, for what bounded purpose, and against which vendor setting, the consent is decorative.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A strong policy memo separates assent from consent, states a bounded purpose, defaults to opt-in, sets a deletion date, and cites the vendor setting each clause overrides. Polish is the last layer. If the memo names a vague purpose or omits the retention override, mark the gap and ask BigAL to show the vendor default your clause needs to counter rather than a general explanation of privacy.

A useful closing line for the artifact sounds like this: "I required parental opt-in and walled the data off from vendor training because the tool's default retains and trains on student questions for 24 months; the clause deletes logs at term end, which the vendor setting otherwise would not."

## Transfer Case

Move the consent design to an 18-year-old dual-enrollment class, where students are legal adults. The capacity test changes: students now consent for themselves, and a separate guardian step is no longer required. But the purpose-limitation, opt-in, and retention tests do not relax at all, and treating adult students as if consent is automatic is its own error. Working this version tests whether you understand consent as a structured set of protections or merely as "ask the parents."
