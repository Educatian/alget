# Portfolio Evidence Setup

![Portfolio Evidence Setup textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/01-07-portfolio-evidence-setup.png)

## Why This Section Matters

A portfolio is not a scrapbook of things you made; it is an argument that you can do something, addressed to a reader who was not there. The difference is whether each entry is verifiable. "I learned a lot about equity" is a feeling, and a reviewer cannot check a feeling. "Here is the memo where I declined a tool after a six-essay equity check" is evidence, and a reviewer can examine it, agree or disagree, and form a judgment. The whole value of a portfolio rises or falls on that distinction.

This section matters because the most common portfolio failure is sincere: the author genuinely grew, says so, and attaches nothing inspectable. Setting up portfolio evidence well means treating each competency claim as a hypothesis a skeptic could test, anchoring it to a specific artifact, and showing growth as a documented change between two artifacts rather than as a report of feeling more confident.

## Opening Case

A teacher sets up her module-1 portfolio entry with the line: "I learned a lot about balancing efficiency and equity, and I feel more confident making tool decisions now." It is honest and it may even be true, but it attaches nothing a reviewer can verify. The claim of learning floats free of any artifact, so a skeptical reader has no way to test it. The entry documents a feeling, not a competency.

What counts as evidence a reviewer can verify is the question this section answers. The fix is not more words about confidence; it is a named artifact the reviewer can open and judge.

## Core Concept

Portfolio evidence setup is the practice of making each competency claim verifiable by anchoring it to an inspectable artifact and demonstrating growth as a documented trajectory. It rests on three moves:

- **Claim as a hypothesis.** State the competency as something a skeptic could test ("I can balance efficiency against equity in a tool decision"), not as a feeling ("I feel ready").
- **Anchor to a specific artifact.** Point to the actual thing you produced (the adoption memo from 1.4), and label exactly which part of it demonstrates the competency. An anchor a reviewer cannot locate is not an anchor.
- **Show growth as a delta between two artifacts.** Place an early artifact next to a later one and annotate the difference. Growth is the change a reader can see, not the change you assert. "Week 1: justified a tool by 'students find it engaging.' Week 4: declined a tool after a quantified equity check." The contrast is the evidence.

The hard distinction here is between reflection and evidence. Reflection narrates an internal change; evidence exhibits an external trace that supports the narration. A strong entry uses reflection to point at evidence, never as a replacement for it. TPACK and critical edtech studies are useful as labels for what a chosen artifact demonstrates, but only if the artifact actually shows the reasoning, not if the anchor names a theory the artifact never used.

## Worked Example

Scenario: the teacher from the opening case rebuilds her module-1 portfolio entry. The question is what counts as evidence a reviewer can verify. Walk it in four numbered moves.

**Before (claim-only setup).** Her first entry reads:

> Module 1 takeaway: I learned a lot about balancing efficiency and equity, and I feel more confident making tool decisions now.

1. **Diagnose the gap.** This asserts learning and confidence but attaches nothing inspectable. A reviewer cannot verify the claim. It is reflection, not evidence.
2. **Select an artifact to anchor it.** Choose the actual adoption memo she wrote in 1.4 where she declined an AI grader for multilingual students.
3. **Tie the artifact to the competency.** Label it: "Evidence of competency: balancing efficiency and equity. This memo shows me quantifying a 170-minute efficiency gain against a 6-essay equity check and choosing a bounded trade-off."
4. **Show growth, not just a product.** Pair it with her week-1 memo that justified a tool by 'students find it engaging,' and annotate the shift: early reasoning rested on preference; later reasoning rested on quantified trade-offs.

**After (evidence-anchored setup).** The revised entry reads:

> Competency: balancing efficiency and equity. Early artifact (week 1): tool justified by 'students find it engaging.' Later artifact (1.4): tool declined after a 6-essay equity check showed it under-scored 4 of 6 multilingual writers, with the efficiency cost named. The change in my reasoning, from preference to quantified trade-off, is the evidence of growth a reviewer can inspect.

The setup is defensible because each claim is anchored to a named, inspectable artifact and growth is shown as a documented trajectory rather than a self-report of feeling more confident.

## Decision Rule

When setting up an entry, lead with the competency claim, then the artifact that proves it, then the boundary or limit the artifact respected. A portfolio claim is justified only when it points to an inspectable artifact that improves a specific, namable competency, not when it reports a feeling.

A defensible entry survives three quick tests:

1. **Purpose check:** Is the claim stated as a testable competency rather than a feeling?
2. **Evidence check:** Can a reviewer open the exact artifact you named and find the part you say demonstrates the competency?
3. **Revision check:** Is growth shown as a labeled difference between an earlier and a later artifact, not as a confidence report?

If a reviewer would have to take your word for it, the entry is reflection, not evidence.

## Common Misreadings

The dominant error is confidence-as-evidence: "I feel more prepared" describes an internal state no reader can audit. A second is the unlocatable anchor, citing "my work this module" without pointing to a specific file the reviewer can open. A third is the polished single artifact mistaken for growth; one strong product shows a present capability but not a trajectory, and growth claims require two artifacts with the difference made explicit. Polish is not evidence; an inspectable delta is.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the setup to a different competency: "I can match a tool to my classroom context rather than to a vendor's claim" (the skill from 1.5). A teacher wants to evidence it.

State the claim as a hypothesis a skeptic could test, not "I think about context now." Then choose the anchor: the actual note where she compared a discussion-bot study's context (12 students, 1:1 devices, no multilingual learners) to her own (34 students, shared tablets, 9 multilingual learners) and decided to pilot locally.

Tie the artifact to the competency precisely: "This note demonstrates context-matching because it lists the study's variables beside mine and suspends the borrowed result where they diverge." Then show growth by pairing it with an earlier note where she had written "the study shows 30 percent, so I'll roll it out," annotating the shift from importing a number to interrogating its conditions. The walkthrough produces a verifiable entry for a different competency using the same three moves, demonstrating that the setup method, not the topic, is what transfers.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: each **competency claim** on the left, the **specific artifact a reviewer could open to verify it** on the right. If the right column says "my growth" or "this module," the claim is unanchored. For this topic, the tell that you are reading well is that you can convert any feeling-statement into a testable claim plus a named artifact.

A strong reader of portfolio setup can answer four questions:

1. Is the claim a testable competency or a feeling?
2. Which exact artifact, locatable by a reviewer, demonstrates it?
3. Does the entry pair two artifacts to show growth as a visible delta?
4. Would a skeptic be able to disagree with the evidence, which is the sign it is evidence at all?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The entry is strong when every competency claim names an inspectable artifact and growth appears as a labeled difference between an earlier and a later artifact. An entry that reports confidence or cites "my work" without a locatable file has supplied reflection in place of evidence; mark that gap and ask BigAL to compare a confidence-report entry against an artifact-anchored one rather than for a general explanation.

A useful closing line sounds like: "The change in my reasoning, from justifying a tool by 'students find it engaging' to declining one after a quantified equity check, is the evidence of growth a reviewer can inspect."

## Transfer Case

Move the portfolio setup into a high-stakes context: a National Board or licensure portfolio where an external assessor scores entries blind, with no access to you. The reliance on inspectable, locatable artifacts intensifies, because there is no conversation to fill the gaps your reflection leaves. If your setup method depends on being there to explain, it fails here; if it produces entries a stranger can verify cold, you understood evidence.
