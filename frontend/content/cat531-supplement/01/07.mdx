## Core Concept

The core idea in this section is that **Portfolio Evidence Setup** should be treated as a relationship among purpose, constraint, evidence, and revision. Purpose names what the learner or audience needs to accomplish. Constraint names what limits the decision. Evidence names the source that makes the decision defensible. Revision shows what changed after the evidence was considered.

Three course anchors shape the reasoning here:

- **TPACK** helps you decide what counts as a meaningful learning or performance demand.
- **critical edtech studies** helps you notice when a design or technology choice creates risk, burden, or unsupported assumptions.
- **AI and education datafication** helps you check whether the artifact works for varied learners, audiences, or use contexts.

Read the anchors as competing checks on quality. One may highlight the learning demand, another may expose a risk, and another may help you test transfer.

## Worked Example

Scenario: A teacher is setting up her module-1 portfolio entry to demonstrate competency in technology decision-making. The question is what counts as evidence a reviewer can verify. Walk it in four numbered moves.

**Before (claim-only setup).** Her first entry reads:

> Module 1 takeaway: I learned a lot about balancing efficiency and equity, and I feel more confident making tool decisions now.

1. **Diagnose the gap.** This asserts learning and confidence but attaches nothing inspectable. A reviewer cannot verify the claim. It is reflection, not evidence.
2. **Select an artifact to anchor it.** Choose the actual adoption memo she wrote in 1.4 where she declined an AI grader for multilingual students.
3. **Tie the artifact to the competency.** Label it: "Evidence of competency: balancing efficiency and equity. This memo shows me quantifying a 170-minute efficiency gain against a 6-essay equity check and choosing a bounded trade-off."
4. **Show growth, not just a product.** Pair it with her week-1 memo that justified a tool by 'students find it engaging,' and annotate the shift: early reasoning rested on preference; later reasoning rested on quantified trade-offs.

**After (evidence-anchored setup).** The revised entry reads:

> Competency: balancing efficiency and equity. Early artifact (week 1): tool justified by 'students find it engaging.' Later artifact (1.4): tool declined after a 6-essay equity check showed it under-scored 4 of 6 multilingual writers, with the efficiency cost named. The change in my reasoning, from preference to quantified trade-off, is the evidence of growth a reviewer can inspect.

The setup is defensible because each claim is anchored to a named, inspectable artifact and growth is shown as a documented trajectory rather than a self-report of feeling more confident.

## Decision Rule

Use the classroom value conflict first, the technology affordance second, and the policy boundary third. A tool choice is justified only when it protects students while improving a specific learning activity.

A strong submission should survive three quick tests:

1. **Purpose check:** What should the audience be able to do after reading or using the artifact?
2. **Evidence check:** Which exact source, observation, annotation, rubric line, data pattern, or policy boundary justifies the revision?
3. **Revision check:** What changed in the artifact, and what suggestion did you reject or modify?

If you cannot answer all three questions, the artifact may be complete, but it is not yet ready as evidence of learning.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Read this section as a small professional decision cycle, not as a list of terms. First, locate the work product: **Design Tension Studio map naming a classroom value conflict and negotiated decision**. Then ask what problem the artifact is supposed to solve inside pedagogical judgment before tool choice. A strong reader can explain the artifact in plain language before naming any theory: who will use it, what decision it supports, and what would count as evidence that it improved.

Next, connect the artifact to the course anchors: **TPACK**, **critical edtech studies**, **teacher professional judgment**. The point is not to cite every anchor. The point is to let one anchor change the artifact. If the anchor does not change wording, layout, evidence, timing, or feedback, it has not yet become design reasoning.

Finally, inspect the revision trail. A weak submission says the artifact was improved. A strong submission shows the earlier version, the issue noticed, the support requested, the decision accepted or rejected, and the reason the final version is better for the intended learner or audience.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: **claim** on the left, **evidence** on the right. The claim is the decision you think the section is teaching. The evidence is the exact phrase, example, annotation, support move, or artifact revision that makes the claim inspectable. By the end of CAT 531 01.07, your notes should make it possible to reconstruct your reasoning without asking you to explain it again.

1. What is the real decision hidden inside this topic?
2. Which constraint makes the decision difficult?
3. What evidence would persuade a skeptical reviewer?
4. What would you revise if the audience, learner, classroom, or tool changed?
