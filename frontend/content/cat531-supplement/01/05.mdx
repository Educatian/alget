## Why This Section Matters

A research finding or a vendor case study always comes attached to a context, but the context is usually printed in small type while the headline number is in bold. "Raised participation 30 percent" travels; "in classes of twelve with one-to-one laptops and no multilingual learners" gets left behind. When a teacher imports the number without the conditions, she is not borrowing evidence; she is borrowing a result that may not exist in her room.

This section matters because the difference between a finding that transfers and one that does not is a finite list of context variables you can actually write down and compare. Class size, device ratio, language profile, accommodations, home connectivity, the platform's interaction model. The professional move is not to dismiss outside evidence and not to swallow it whole, but to lay your context next to the study's context, variable by variable, and decide where the gap is large enough to break the claim.

## Learning Targets

- Perform a graded, variable-by-variable comparison of a study's or vendor pilot's reported context against your own classroom to decide whether a borrowed result transfers, is suspended, or must be locally piloted.
- Distinguish artifact polish from evidence-based revision.
- Document a bounded AI/software support move with accepted and rejected suggestions.
- Use annotations, support requests, and revision notes to make a classroom technology decision more defensible.

## Opening Case

A teacher reads that a discussion-bot "raised participation by 30 percent in a published study" and wants to roll it out to all her sections next week. The number is real, but it was produced under conditions she has not examined. Her own room has 34 students, a cart of 10 shared tablets, 9 multilingual learners, and several students without reliable home internet. The question is not whether the 30 percent happened, but whether the conditions that produced it exist in her classroom.

The record that matters here is not "a study supports this tool." It is a side-by-side comparison of the study's context variables and her own, with a decision that follows from where they diverge.

## Core Concept

A context variable is any classroom condition that can change whether an instructional result reproduces. The core skill is treating a borrowed finding as conditional, not universal: the claim is "this tool produced this effect under these conditions," and your job is to check whether the conditions hold for you. This is the standard posture of evidence review, not a classroom-only heuristic: the What Works Clearinghouse handbook treats generalizability as bounded by the people, settings, and outcomes a study actually sampled (What Works Clearinghouse, 2022), and field-experiment results are conditional on the population, situation, and implementation that produced them (List, 2020).

The variables that most often break transfer fall into four families:

- **Scale.** Class size and the student-to-device ratio. A discussion tool that thrives at 12 students with 1:1 devices behaves differently at 34 students sharing 10 tablets.
- **Learner composition.** Number of multilingual learners, students with IEP or 504 accommodations, reading-level spread. A text-only, fast-typing interaction model advantages some compositions and disadvantages others; research on multilingual learners frames such interface and language demands as an access-and-equity variable, not a footnote (Grapin et al., 2023).
- **Access conditions.** Home connectivity, device ownership, whether the activity assumes work outside class.
- **Interaction model fit.** Whether the tool's mode of engagement (typing speed, screen time, real-time response) matches how this group can actually participate.

The disciplined comparison is variable by variable, not impression by impression. Where a variable matches, the borrowed evidence carries weight; where it diverges sharply, the evidence is suspended until you test it locally. Critical edtech studies is the lens for who a context mismatch excludes; datafication is the lens for whether the tool's model was built for learners like yours; [TPACK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_pedagogical_content_knowledge) keeps the comparison tied to the actual content goal rather than novelty.

## Worked Example

Scenario: the teacher from the opening case decides whether the discussion-bot's 30 percent result applies to her. The judgment turns on whether her classroom context matches the study's. Walk it in four numbered moves.

**Before (context-blind framing).** Her note reads:

> The study shows the discussion bot raises participation 30 percent, so I will roll it out to all my sections next week.

1. **Pull the study's context variables.** Reported setting: 12 students per class, 1:1 laptops, 0 multilingual learners flagged, all students with home broadband.
2. **Pull her own context variables.** Her setting: 34 students, 10 shared tablets on a cart, 9 multilingual learners, 7 students with IEP/504 accommodations, about a third without reliable home internet.
3. **Compare variable by variable.** Class size 12 vs. 34. Device ratio 1:1 vs. roughly 1:3.4. Language profile 0 vs. 9 multilingual. The bot's text-only, fast-typing interaction model fits the study's room but not hers.
4. **Decide using the mismatch.** The 30 percent figure does not transfer. She will pilot the bot with one 34-student section, on a rotation so the device ratio is manageable, with a voice-input fallback for multilingual learners, and measure participation in HER room before any wider rollout.

**After (context-aware framing).** The revised note reads:

> The 30 percent study ran with 12 students, 1:1 devices, and no multilingual learners. My section has 34 students, a 1:3.4 device ratio, and 9 multilingual learners. I am not assuming the result transfers. I will pilot one section with device rotation and a voice-input fallback, then check my own participation data before scaling.

The decision is defensible because the context variables that govern transfer were listed and compared, so the borrowed evidence is tested against this room rather than assumed to apply.

## Decision Rule

Before a borrowed result can justify a tool, the value conflict in your room comes first, the affordance second, the policy boundary third, and the context comparison underwrites all three. A tool choice is justified only when it protects students while improving a specific learning activity in the conditions you actually teach in.

A defensible context judgment survives three quick tests:

1. **Purpose check:** Does the study's outcome actually match the goal you have for your students, or just sound impressive?
2. **Evidence check:** Did you pull the study's context variables and lay them next to yours, variable by variable?
3. **Revision check:** Where the contexts diverge, did you suspend the claim and plan a local pilot rather than scale on faith?

If you cannot name the study's class size, device ratio, and learner profile, you cannot yet say the result transfers.

## Common Misreadings

The most frequent error is treating "engaging in the study" as "engaging for my students," which skips the composition and access variables entirely. A second is reading a large effect size as strong transfer evidence; a large effect under conditions you do not share is a large effect you may never see. A third is the opposite overcorrection, dismissing all outside evidence because contexts always differ; the skill is graded comparison, keeping the evidence where variables match and suspending it where they break.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the comparison to a new claim: a vendor says its math-fluency game "improved scores in a pilot of 200 students" (an illustrative composite of typical vendor pilot claims). A teacher is tempted to adopt it for her two sections. Setting-dependence of this kind is well documented at national scale: the growth-mindset experiment of Yeager et al. (2019) found the intervention worked only where peer norms supported it, the same context-moderates-effect logic this section teaches.

Pull the pilot's context. The fine print: suburban district, 1:1 Chromebooks, daily 20-minute home practice assigned and completed, students screened to grade level. Pull her context: an urban section with shared devices, no reliable way to assign home practice, and a reading-level spread of three grades, several below the game's text demands.

Compare variable by variable. The 20-minute daily home-practice assumption is load-bearing for the pilot's result, and it simply does not hold for her students without home devices; the reading-level mismatch means her struggling readers will spend the game decoding instructions rather than practicing math. Two large variables diverge.

Decide from the mismatch. She does not adopt on the vendor's number. She runs a two-week in-class-only pilot with audio instructions enabled, measures fluency in her own sections, and compares before deciding. The walkthrough shows the same variable-by-variable discipline applied to a vendor pilot rather than a journal study, with the divergent variables driving a local test instead of a leap.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: each **context variable** on the left, **study value versus my value** on the right. The variables where the two columns differ most are your transfer risks; circle them. For this topic, the tell that you are reading well is that you can name at least one variable in the study's fine print that the headline omitted.

A strong reader of context variables can answer four questions:

1. What scale, composition, and access conditions produced the original result?
2. Which of those conditions does my classroom not share?
3. Is the diverging variable load-bearing for the effect, or incidental?
4. Where the contexts break, what local pilot would test the claim in my room?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The artifact is strong when the study's context variables and your own are listed side by side and the decision follows from the specific divergences, not from the headline number. An artifact that cites the 30 percent without the conditions has imported a result it cannot support; mark that gap and ask BigAL to compare a context-blind adoption against a context-matched one rather than for a general explanation.

A useful closing line sounds like: "The study ran at 12 students with 1:1 devices and no multilingual learners; my section diverges on all three, so I am piloting locally with a voice fallback before scaling."

## Transfer Case

Move the discussion-bot decision into a small honors seminar of 11 students, all native speakers, each with a laptop. The teacher's context now nearly matches the study's. The same variable-by-variable comparison that blocked adoption in the 34-student room may now support it, because the load-bearing variables align. If your method only ever produces "do not adopt," you learned a reflex; if it produces "adopt here, suspend there" depending on the variables, you understood context.
