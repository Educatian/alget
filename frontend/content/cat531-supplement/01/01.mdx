# CBI as Pedagogical Judgment

![CBI as Pedagogical Judgment textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/01-01-cbi-as-pedagogical-judgment.png)

## Why This Section Matters

Computer-based instruction (CBI) is often sold as a question of features: which platform grades faster, adapts more, or engages students longer. That framing hides the part that actually belongs to you. The pedagogical question is not "is this tool good?" but "does putting a computer between me and this learning goal change what my students can do, and at what cost to whom?" That question has no vendor-supplied answer. It is a judgment, and judgment is the thing this course holds you accountable for.

Treating CBI as judgment matters because the alternative, treating it as procurement, quietly transfers authority over learning to whoever designed the software. When you cannot articulate why a tool serves a specific goal for specific students, you have not selected a tool; you have deferred a decision. This section establishes the habit that runs through the whole module: every technology choice you defend should be reconstructable by a colleague who was not in the room.

## Opening Case

A 9th-grade biology teacher has 28 students, a free AI quiz-generator that promises to cut three hours of grading a week, and a unit test in nine days. The tool stores student responses on a vendor server with no published retention period, and two families have already filed third-party-processor opt-outs. The exciting question ("will instant feedback motivate them?") is the wrong one to lead with. The professional question is whether she can name the instructional purpose, the equity-and-privacy boundary, and the evidence that would let a colleague call her decision sound rather than convenient.

This is the shape of nearly every CBI decision a teacher faces: a real benefit, a real risk, and a deadline that pressures you to skip the reasoning. The section exists to slow that moment down.

## Core Concept

Pedagogical judgment about CBI is the disciplined act of deciding, with reasons a peer can audit, whether and how a computer should mediate a particular piece of learning. It is not intuition and it is not compliance with a recommendation. It is a structured weighing of four elements that must all be named explicitly:

- **Purpose**: the specific thing a learner should be able to do that the tool is supposed to serve. "Surface misconceptions before the summative" is a purpose; "save time" and "boost engagement" are benefits, not purposes.
- **Constraint**: the condition that limits or forbids the obvious choice. Class size, a privacy opt-out, an unstated retention clause, a device shortage.
- **Evidence**: the specific source that makes the call defensible. A clause in a privacy policy, a district vetted-tools list, a spot-check of outputs.
- **Revision**: what changed once the evidence was considered, including what you decided NOT to do.

Three scholarly anchors keep this from collapsing into opinion, and they are deliberately in tension:

| Anchor | What it makes you ask | Where it pushes the decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| TPACK | Does the technology actually fit THIS content and THIS pedagogy together, not just generically? | Toward purpose-fit |
| Critical edtech studies | Whose burden, surveillance, or exclusion does this tool create that the sales pitch omits? | Toward naming hidden costs |
| AI and education datafication | What happens to student data, and does the model's training match my learners? | Toward boundaries and privacy |

A choice that satisfies one anchor while ignoring the others is not yet a judgment. The friction between them is the work.

## Worked Example

Scenario: the 9th-grade biology teacher from the opening case decides whether to adopt the free AI quiz-generator. Walk the judgment in four numbered moves.

**Before (preference framing).** The teacher's first draft of the adoption note reads:

> I want to use QuizGenAI because it saves me about three hours a week and students said the instant feedback feels motivating.

1. **Locate the decision.** This is not "is the tool good?" It is "does adopting this tool serve a specific learning purpose without creating an unacceptable student-data risk?"
2. **Name the instructional purpose.** Faster formative feedback on unit checks so misconceptions surface before the summative test. (Engagement and the 3-hour saving are benefits, not the purpose.)
3. **Name the binding constraint.** Two of 28 students are on data-privacy flags from families who opted out of third-party processors; the retention period is unstated.
4. **Weigh evidence and an alternative.** Evidence: the privacy policy has no retention clause; the district list of vetted tools does not include this one. Alternative weighed and rejected: a vetted but slower district tool that keeps data in-house.

**After (judgment framing).** The revised note reads:

> I am declining QuizGenAI for whole-class use and routing formative quizzes through the district-vetted tool instead. Purpose: surface misconceptions before the summative. Constraint: two students carry data-opt-out flags and QuizGenAI has no stated retention period. I rejected the faster QuizGenAI option because the time saving does not justify processing flagged students' responses on an unvetted server. I can revisit if the vendor publishes a retention clause.

The improvement is not a longer note. It is an inspectable trace: a colleague can now see the purpose, the constraint, the evidence, and the alternative that was set aside, and can challenge the call on its merits.

## Decision Rule

When a CBI choice forces a value against an affordance, order your reasoning so students are protected first. Name the classroom value conflict before you discuss what the tool can do, weigh the technology affordance second, and confirm the policy boundary third. A tool choice is justified only when it protects students while improving a specific learning activity.

A defensible CBI decision should survive three quick tests:

1. **Purpose check:** What, specifically, should students be able to do that they could not before, and is the computer load-bearing for that?
2. **Evidence check:** Which exact source (a policy clause, a vetted-tools list, an output spot-check) makes the call inspectable rather than asserted?
3. **Revision check:** What did you decide against, and why did you reject it?

If you cannot answer all three, you have a preference dressed as a decision.

## Common Misreadings

The most common error is treating a vendor recommendation or a popular adoption as evidence. "Other schools use it" describes diffusion, not fit; it tells you nothing about your purpose or your students' privacy posture. A second error is collapsing benefit into purpose: time saved is real, but it cannot justify a tool unless the learning goal is also met. A third is believing that a decision documented after the fact is the same as a decision reasoned through; in this course, judgment is the visible trace, not the eventual outcome.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the concept to a fresh case: a teacher considering an AI discussion-summarizer that condenses each class's online forum into three bullet points the next morning. Walk the judgment as a decision, not a list of terms.

First, state the purpose precisely. Is the computer load-bearing here? The goal is "help me notice which threads stalled so I can re-open them." A summarizer can serve that; a faster human skim might too. Naming the purpose this tightly already tells you the tool is convenience, not necessity, which lowers how much risk you should accept for it.

Second, run the anchors against it. TPACK: does a summary actually support discussion pedagogy, or does it let the teacher skip reading student voice? Critical edtech studies: a summarizer trained on majority-register prose may flatten or misread multilingual students' contributions, quietly making their threads "the ones that did not matter." Datafication: forum posts are now leaving the building. Each anchor surfaces a cost the feature list hid.

Third, decide and show the trade. A defensible call: use the summarizer as a triage aid the teacher still reads behind, never as a substitute for reading flagged or short threads, and disable it if posts cannot stay inside district storage. The judgment is visible because the rejected option (trusting the summary outright) is named alongside the reason.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: the **decision** on the left, the **evidence that makes it inspectable** on the right. For CBI judgment specifically, watch for the moment a benefit is being smuggled in as a purpose, and write down what the real purpose would have to be for the tool to be load-bearing.

A strong reader of CBI as judgment can answer four questions:

1. What learning goal is the computer actually serving, and is it necessary or merely convenient?
2. Which constraint (privacy, equity, access) would forbid the obvious choice?
3. What source would persuade a skeptical colleague that the call protects students?
4. What would change if the vendor published a retention clause, or if the tool became mandatory?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The artifact is strong when a mentor teacher can read your decision trace and reconstruct the call without you in the room: purpose stated as a learning goal, the binding constraint named, the evidence cited, and at least one rejected alternative with its reason. Visual polish is the last layer and counts for nothing if the rejected alternative is missing, because a decision with no visible road-not-taken is indistinguishable from a preference.

A useful closing line for the artifact sounds like: "I declined the faster option because the time saving did not justify processing flagged students' data on an unvetted server; I will revisit if the vendor publishes a retention clause." That sentence shows the trade, the threshold, and the conditions for changing the call.

## Transfer Case

Move the same biology decision into a district that has just signed an enterprise data-processing agreement covering all major vendors. The purpose (surface misconceptions early) is unchanged, but the binding constraint dissolves: the two opt-out flags are now covered by the agreement, and retention is contractually defined. The defensible call may flip from "decline" to "adopt with the leaderboard off." If your reasoning only produces "decline," you memorized an outcome; if it produces a different but still-justified call, you understand CBI as judgment.
