# Adapting Plans for Learner Variability

![Adapting Plans for Learner Variability textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/02-05-adapting-plans-for-learner-variability.png)

## Why This Section Matters

Learner variability is the ordinary fact that a single class contains students who decode at different rates, hold different prior knowledge, and reach the work through different languages and supports. Adapting a plan for that variability is not the same as making the work easier. The professional distinction, drawn from Universal Design for Learning, is that you change the route to a learning goal while holding the goal constant for everyone. A weak adaptation lowers the goal for some students (a simpler text, fewer questions) and, in doing so, teaches them something less than their peers learned. A strong adaptation keeps the goal fixed and removes the specific barrier that stood between a student and that goal. This section trains the diagnostic move at the center of that distinction: locating the actual barrier rather than reacting to a label.

The artifact, a Design Tension Studio map, records the live tension between equal access and equal expectations, and the negotiated adaptation that protects both. A reviewer should see the same goal surviving for every student while only the access path changed.

## Opening Case

The objective is fixed for the whole class: "Students analyze a primary-source letter from 1863." Two students read well below grade level, and three multilingual learners are new to dense historical English. The fast, tempting fix is to hand those five a shorter, plainer letter. It feels kind and it is wrong: now five students analyze an easier source while their peers analyze the real one, and the gap the teacher meant to close has quietly widened. The barrier in this room is not analysis ability; it is decoding archaic vocabulary and long sentences. An adaptation that keeps the 1863 letter but removes the decoding barrier serves the goal. An adaptation that swaps the letter abandons it.

## Core Concept

Adapting for variability means holding the goal constant and changing the access path, which requires you to separate three things students often conflate: the goal, the barrier, and the label.

| Element | What it is | The trap |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Goal | The cognitive task everyone must reach (analyze a source) | Lowering it for some students and calling it differentiation |
| Barrier | The specific obstacle between this student and the goal (decoding archaic text) | Treating the barrier as if it were a deficit in the goal itself |
| Label | A category attached to a student (ELL, IEP, below grade level) | Designing from the label instead of the task barrier |

The decisive habit is to build adaptations from the barrier, not the label. "ELL" does not tell you what to change; "new to dense historical English" tells you to supply a context preview and a glossary. The same student labeled ELL needs nothing extra for a hands-on lab. Three access levers usually suffice once the barrier is named: change the input (audio, a sentence-by-sentence version, a glossary), change the support (a model, a preview, a partner), or change the output mode (speak instead of write) while holding the cognitive demand identical. [TPACK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_pedagogical_content_knowledge) and critical edtech studies add a caution: an AI adaptation tool will often default to lowering the goal because that is the easiest text transformation, so you must check that what it changed was the access, not the analysis.

## Worked Example

Walk through one annotated judgment call on adapting for learner variability. The objective is fixed: "Students analyze a primary-source letter from 1863." The class includes two students reading well below grade level and three multilingual learners new to dense historical English.

**Step 1. Keep the goal, find the barrier.** The objective stays analyze a primary source. The barrier is not analysis ability; it is decoding archaic vocabulary and long sentences in the letter.

**Step 2. Reject the easy but wrong moves.**

> **Tempting move A (rejected):** Give struggling readers a shorter, simpler letter. Annotated: changes the goal, since they now analyze a different, easier source.

> **Tempting move B (rejected):** Give them four questions instead of twelve. Annotated: changes only quantity; the decoding barrier is untouched.

**Step 3. Make the access-preserving decision.** Recorded on the map: "Keep the same 1863 letter and the same analysis questions for everyone. Provide a glossary of six archaic terms, an audio reading of the letter, and a sentence-by-sentence version for the two below-level readers. Multilingual learners get the glossary plus a first-language preview of the historical context."

**Step 4. Tie it to the label trap.** The teacher notes: the adaptation was built from the task barrier (archaic decoding), not from the labels ELL or below grade level. Every student still performs the same analysis, so a mentor can see the goal held while access changed.

Match each named barrier to the access lever that removes it while holding the cognitive demand identical. Notice that the lever follows the barrier, never the label.

## Decision Rule

Hold the goal constant for every student; change only the access path, and build that path from the named barrier rather than from the student's label. If an adaptation changes what students learn rather than how they reach it, it is a lowered goal in disguise.

Test any adaptation against four questions:

1. **Goal:** Is the cognitive demand identical to what every other student faces?
2. **Barrier:** Have I named the specific obstacle, separate from the label?
3. **Lever:** Does my change adjust input, support, or output mode, not the task itself?
4. **Label trap:** Did I design from the barrier, or did I default to a generic "easier" version because of a category?

If the goal moved, the adaptation has failed even if the student is happier.

The first misreading is to equate adaptation with reduction: fewer questions, simpler text, lighter rubric. Reduction usually leaves the real barrier intact while teaching less. The second is to design from the label, producing a one-size "ELL packet" that ignores which specific demand of today's task is hard for which student. The third is to assume an AI rewrite preserved the goal. When a tool "simplifies" a primary source, it frequently strips the very complexity the objective asked students to analyze, so the simplified version trains a different, lower skill. Always compare the adapted task's demand to the original's before trusting it.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the goal-barrier-lever method to a math word-problem lesson whose objective is "Students model a real situation with a linear equation." A student with dyscalculia and a student new to English both struggle, but for different barriers. The dyscalculia barrier is holding multi-digit arithmetic in working memory; the lever is allowing a calculator so the modeling cognition is not blocked by computation. The language barrier is parsing the problem's English; the lever is a visual diagram of the situation and key vocabulary pre-taught. Both students still write and justify the same linear equation, so the goal is untouched. The map records two different barriers, two different levers, and one shared goal, which is exactly what distinguishes adaptation from giving some students a smaller math problem.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: in the left column write the goal and the barrier; in the right column write the access lever you chose and confirm the goal did not move. For the 1863 letter, the left column says "analyze the source; barrier is decoding archaic text" and the right column says "audio plus glossary; same letter, same questions." A strong reader can audit any adaptation by asking, "did the cognitive demand survive?" If a student ended up doing easier thinking, not just easier reading, the adaptation crossed the line. Your map is strong when the goal is visibly identical across students.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A strong adaptation map states the single goal that every student must reach and shows it unchanged across adaptations. It names the specific barrier for each adaptation, separate from any label, so a reviewer can see you diagnosed rather than categorized. It uses access levers (input, support, output mode) and documents that the cognitive demand stayed constant. And it records at least one reduction move you rejected (the shorter text, the fewer questions) with the reason it would have lowered the goal. Polish is a neat differentiation chart; evidence is the demonstrated constancy of the goal beneath the varied access.

## Transfer Case

Move the method to a kindergarten setting where the objective is "Students retell a story in sequence." There are no reading levels yet, but variability is real: one child is selectively mute, one is a wheelchair user, one is still building English. The goal-barrier-lever logic transfers, but the levers change shape: the selectively mute child retells by ordering picture cards (output mode), the wheelchair user needs the sequencing materials at an accessible height (physical access), the English learner gets the key sequence words pre-taught (input support). The goal, retell in sequence, holds for all three. If you can design these without reaching for "easier story," you understand adaptation as barrier removal rather than as making the work smaller.
