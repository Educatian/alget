## Why This Section Matters

A generative planning tool produces output that is only as grounded as the situation you hand it. "Scenario setup" is the act of writing that situation down: the grade, the time block, the prior lesson, the room's constraints, and the students who must be served. It is the single highest-leverage move in AI-assisted planning, because an AI cannot see your room. It fills every gap you leave with a statistical default, usually an affluent, well-resourced, on-grade-level classroom that does not exist. A teacher who skips scenario setup is not collaborating with the tool; they are accepting the average classroom baked into its training data.

This section treats scenario setup as the foundation on which every later decision rests. The artifact is a prompt transcript with teacher edits and rejected suggestions, and the transcript begins with the scenario you supplied. A reviewer reading it should be able to tell, from the first prompt alone, that the plan that follows was built for a specific room rather than a generic one.

## Opening Case

A first-year teacher opens TeachGen@i and types "Plan a fractions lesson." The result is fluent and confident: a 60-minute block, students working in pairs on laptops, an opening that assumes everyone already understands equivalent fractions. None of it fits her room. Her period is 38 minutes, the laptop cart is checked out that day, a third of her class reads below grade level, and yesterday's lesson only introduced the words numerator and denominator. The plan was not wrong because the tool is bad; it was wrong because she gave it an empty scenario and it filled the emptiness with a default classroom. The fix is not a better tool. It is a better scenario.

## Core Concept

Scenario setup means converting the facts of your room into constraints the tool must honor, and distinguishing the facts that bind from the facts that are merely flavor. A complete scenario carries four kinds of information, and a thin prompt is usually missing three of them.

| Scenario element | The binding fact | What the AI defaults to if you omit it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time and structure | "38-minute single period, no block" | A roomy 60-minute lesson with transitions you do not have |
| Resources present today | "No student devices; one projector" | One-to-one laptops and reliable bandwidth |
| Learner profile (de-identified) | "About a third read below grade level" | An on-grade-level, monolingual class |
| Prior knowledge | "Yesterday we named numerator and denominator only" | Mastery of prerequisites the class has not met |

Two disciplines govern how you write these down. [TPACK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_pedagogical_content_knowledge) (Mishra & Koehler, 2006; extended to AI-rich planning by Ning et al., 2024) reminds you that the content prerequisite (what students already know) is a scenario fact, not a nicety; an AI that assumes mastery will plan the wrong lesson. Critical edtech studies and datafication scholarship (Selwyn, 2016; Williamson et al., 2023) remind you to de-identify: you encode "about a third read below grade level" rather than naming students, because the transcript may be stored by a vendor and a real classroom profile should never leave the room attached to names. This rule is legal, not just prudent: FERPA protects personally identifiable information in education records (34 CFR Part 99), PII disclosed to a third-party vendor remains FERPA-governed and may be used only for school-authorized purposes, and COPPA (16 CFR Part 312) adds a stricter verifiable-parental-consent regime where students are under 13. A scenario is complete when omitting any one element would let the tool substitute a default that does not match your room.

## Worked Example

Walk through one annotated judgment call on setting up a scenario. A fourth-grade teacher wants TeachGen@i to draft a small-group fractions lesson and records the prompt transcript as evidence.

**Step 1. Write the before scenario.** The first prompt reads: "Plan a fractions lesson." Annotated in the margin: too thin. No grade, no prior lesson, no time, no constraint. The plan that came back assumed a 60-minute block, one-to-one laptops, and students already comfortable with equivalent fractions.

**Step 2. Name what the scenario must carry.** The teacher lists the binding facts: grade 4, 38-minute period, no devices that day, about a third of the class reading below grade level, yesterday's lesson introduced numerators and denominators only.

**Step 3. Make the before/after edit on the transcript.**

> **Before:** "Plan a fractions lesson."

> **After:** "Plan a 38-minute grade-4 lesson on comparing fractions with the same denominator. No student devices today. About one third of the class reads below grade level, so keep written directions short and add a visual model. Yesterday we only named numerators and denominators; do not assume equivalence."

**Step 4. Record one rejected suggestion.** TeachGen@i proposed a paired laptop sorting game. The teacher rejected it on the transcript: "Cut, violates the no-device constraint and would exclude the whole class today." The de-identified phrasing ("about one third read below grade level") protects students while still steering the plan. A mentor reading the transcript can see the scenario, the added constraints, and the reasoned rejection.

Match each scenario fact the teacher supplied to the specific default it blocks. Naming the blocked default is what proves the fact was load-bearing.

## Decision Rule

State the scenario before you state the request, and write every binding fact as a hard limit the tool may not relax. The order matters: a request placed before its constraints invites the tool to optimize for an ideal room and then bolt your room on afterward. Put the room first.

When the transcript looks finished, audit the opening scenario against four questions:

1. **Time:** Did I give the real minutes, not a generous estimate?
2. **Resources:** Did I name what is missing today (devices, bandwidth), not just what exists in theory?
3. **Learners:** Did I encode the profile in de-identified, actionable terms the tool can plan around?
4. **Prerequisites:** Did I tell the tool what students do NOT yet know, so it cannot assume mastery?

If any answer is no, the plan that follows is built for a room other than yours.

The first misreading is to mistake adjectives for constraints. "Plan an engaging, rigorous, differentiated lesson" tells the tool nothing it can act on; engaging is not a number of minutes and differentiated is not a learner profile. The second is to treat a fluent output as a sign the scenario was adequate. Fluency only proves the model wrote confidently; it says nothing about whether the plan fits your 38 minutes or your missing laptops. A polished plan built on a thin scenario is the most dangerous case, because its confidence hides the gap it filled with defaults.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Take a thin prompt apart and rebuild it. Start with "Make a reading lesson for my class." A reviewer cannot evaluate this because the scenario is invisible. Rebuild it element by element. Time: "in a 45-minute period." Resources: "I have a class set of the novel but no devices." Learners: "five multilingual learners new to academic English, and two students with IEPs allowing extended response time." Prerequisites: "students have read chapters one through three but have not analyzed theme before." The rebuilt prompt is longer, but every clause is a constraint the tool must satisfy and a reviewer can check. The transcript should show this rebuild, not just the final clean prompt, because the rebuild is the evidence of judgment.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: in the left column list each scenario fact you supplied; in the right column write the default the AI would have used if you had left that fact out. For the fractions case, the left column says "no devices today" and the right column says "the tool would have assumed one-to-one laptops." A strong reader can look at any AI plan and reverse-engineer the missing scenario by spotting the defaults: a 60-minute structure betrays an unstated time, a laptop activity betrays an unstated resource assumption. Your transcript is strong when a reader can see exactly which defaults you blocked.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A strong scenario transcript shows the thin first prompt, not just the polished final one, so the reviewer can see what you fixed. It encodes the learner profile in de-identified terms (counts and needs, never names), proving you protected students while still informing the plan. And it names at least one default the tool reached for and you blocked (the assumed devices, the assumed prerequisite mastery), with the binding fact that ruled it out. Polish is the clean final prompt; evidence is the visible repair from thin to grounded.

## Transfer Case

Move the same setup task from a fourth-grade math lesson to a high-school chemistry lab. The four scenario elements still apply, but their content shifts and a fifth pressure appears: safety constraints (no open flame near the new student who has not completed lab-safety training) become a binding fact the tool will never infer on its own. The skill transfers (state the room before the request, encode what is missing, block the defaults), but the specific facts do not. If you can list the chemistry scenario's binding facts without copying the math example's, you have learned scenario setup as a method rather than as a template.
