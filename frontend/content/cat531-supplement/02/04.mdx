## Core Concept

The core idea in this section is that **Comparing AI Suggestions to Pedagogy** should be treated as a relationship among purpose, constraint, evidence, and revision. Purpose names what the learner or audience needs to accomplish. Constraint names what limits the decision. Evidence names the source that makes the decision defensible. Revision shows what changed after the evidence was considered.

Three course anchors shape the reasoning here:

- **TPACK** helps you decide what counts as a meaningful learning or performance demand.
- **critical edtech studies** helps you notice when a design or technology choice creates risk, burden, or unsupported assumptions.
- **AI and education datafication** helps you check whether the artifact works for varied learners, audiences, or use contexts.

Use these anchors as lenses, not as names to drop into a reflection. A useful lens changes what you notice, what you question, and what you revise.

## Worked Example

Walk through one annotated judgment call on comparing an AI suggestion to pedagogy. A teacher's objective is: "Students will explain why two fractions are equivalent." TeachGen@i offers a confident suggestion.

**Step 1. State the objective as the test.** The verb is explain, not match or recall. Any suggestion must move students toward producing an explanation.

**Step 2. Hold the suggestion against the objective.**

> **AI suggestion:** "Open with a fast-paced escape room: students race to match cards showing equivalent fractions; the first team out wins." Reads polished and engaging.

> **Pedagogy check (annotated):** Matching cards quickly is recognition under time pressure. It never asks a student to say why one half equals two fourths. Fluent and fun, but off the objective.

**Step 3. Make the negotiated decision on the map.** The teacher does not discard the engagement, and does not accept the activity as-is. Decision recorded: "Keep the card sort, but after each match students write one sentence using the stem 'These are equivalent because...' and a partner checks it. Rejected the timed-race framing because the clock pushes guessing over reasoning."

**Step 4. Check the margins.** A third of the class reads below the card text, so the teacher adds an icon model alongside the words. The map now shows the tension (appeal vs. fit to the explain objective), the negotiated revision, the rejected element with its reason, and the learner-variability adjustment, all auditable by a mentor.

## Decision Rule

Use the classroom value conflict first, the technology affordance second, and the policy boundary third. A tool choice is justified only when it protects students while improving a specific learning activity.

Before submitting the Design Tension Studio map naming a classroom value conflict and negotiated decision, slow down and check whether the trace can answer three questions:

1. **Purpose check:** What should the audience be able to do after reading or using the artifact?
2. **Evidence check:** Which exact source, observation, annotation, rubric line, data pattern, or policy boundary justifies the revision?
3. **Revision check:** What changed in the artifact, and what suggestion did you reject or modify?

If you cannot answer all three questions, the artifact may be complete, but it is not yet ready as evidence of learning.

## Common Misreadings

A common mistake is to defend a technology because it is engaging. Engagement matters, but it does not replace evidence about learning, access, or risk.

The artifact should show how student need, evidence, and policy boundaries shaped the revision.
