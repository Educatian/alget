# Comparing AI Suggestions to Pedagogy

![Comparing AI Suggestions to Pedagogy textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/02-04-comparing-ai-suggestions-to-pedagogy.png)

## Why This Section Matters

An AI suggestion arrives polished, confident, and often genuinely appealing. None of those qualities tell you whether it teaches the thing you intended to teach. The danger is alignment drift: a suggestion that is engaging and well-written but pointed at a cognitive demand one or two levels below your objective. A "match the equivalent fractions" race feels like a fractions lesson, yet matching is recognition while your objective asked students to explain. The tool optimized for fluency and fun; it did not optimize for your verb. This section gives you a test that holds any AI suggestion against the actual cognitive demand of your learning objective, so that appeal never substitutes for alignment.

The artifact, a Design Tension Studio map, records the live tension between a suggestion's appeal and its fit, and the negotiated decision that keeps what is useful while correcting what is misaligned. A reviewer should see that you measured the suggestion against the objective rather than against your gut.

## Opening Case

A teacher's objective for the day is precise: "Students will explain why two fractions are equivalent." TeachGen@i offers a sleek opener, a timed escape room where teams race to match cards showing equivalent fractions, first team out wins. It is the kind of activity students cheer for. But the teacher reads the objective's verb again. Explain is a reasoning task; the escape room asks students to recognize matches quickly. A child can win the race without ever saying why one half equals two fourths. The suggestion is not bad; it is aimed at the wrong cognitive level. The decision is what to do with an appealing activity that misses the target by one rung.

## Core Concept

Comparing a suggestion to pedagogy means treating the objective's verb as a fixed target and asking what cognitive demand the suggestion actually requires of a student. The two often diverge, and the gap is usually downward: appealing activities tend to live at recognition and recall because those are easy to gamify.

| Objective verb | Cognitive demand it sets | A suggestion that only reaches recognition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Explain / justify | Produce reasoning a listener can follow | Matching cards, multiple choice, a sorting race |
| Compare / contrast | Articulate a relationship between cases | Highlighting differences without stating why they matter |
| Apply / solve | Use a procedure in a new situation | Re-watching a worked example, filling a template |

The test is a single question asked of every suggestion: what is the smallest thing a student could do to "succeed" at this activity, and does that smallest thing match my verb? If a student can win the escape room by pattern-matching without reasoning, the activity's true demand is recognition, whatever the packaging suggests. TPACK frames this as the content-pedagogy fit: the technological activity must serve the specific content reasoning, not just animate the room. The negotiated decision is rarely "reject"; more often it is "keep the appeal, add the missing rung," because a small modification can lift a recognition task to the reasoning the objective requires.

## Worked Example

Walk through one annotated judgment call on comparing an AI suggestion to pedagogy. A teacher's objective is: "Students will explain why two fractions are equivalent." TeachGen@i offers a confident suggestion.

**Step 1. State the objective as the test.** The verb is explain, not match or recall. Any suggestion must move students toward producing an explanation.

**Step 2. Hold the suggestion against the objective.**

> **AI suggestion:** "Open with a fast-paced escape room: students race to match cards showing equivalent fractions; the first team out wins." Reads polished and engaging.

> **Pedagogy check (annotated):** Matching cards quickly is recognition under time pressure. It never asks a student to say why one half equals two fourths. Fluent and fun, but off the objective.

**Step 3. Make the negotiated decision on the map.** The teacher does not discard the engagement, and does not accept the activity as-is. Decision recorded: "Keep the card sort, but after each match students write one sentence using the stem 'These are equivalent because...' and a partner checks it. Rejected the timed-race framing because the clock pushes guessing over reasoning."

**Step 4. Check the margins.** A third of the class reads below the card text, so the teacher adds an icon model alongside the words. The map now shows the tension (appeal vs. fit to the explain objective), the negotiated revision, the rejected element with its reason, and the learner-variability adjustment, all auditable by a mentor.

## Decision Rule

Measure every suggestion against the objective's verb, not against how engaging it feels. When the demand falls short of the verb, prefer modifying the suggestion up to the target over rejecting it outright, and prefer rejecting it over lowering your objective to match it.

Run a suggestion through the alignment test:

1. **Verb:** What cognitive demand does my objective's verb set?
2. **Floor:** What is the least a student could do to succeed at this suggestion?
3. **Gap:** Does that floor reach the verb, or fall short of it?
4. **Move:** Can I add the missing rung (a reasoning stem, a justification step) while keeping the appeal?

Never quietly rewrite the objective to fit an attractive activity; that is alignment drift disguised as flexibility.

The first misreading is to accept engagement as evidence of alignment. A loud, happy room can be working at a level far below the objective; volume is not depth. The second is to treat the AI's confidence as authority. A suggestion phrased as "this proven activity will deepen understanding" is still just text; the claim of depth is not the presence of depth. The third, subtler error is silent objective-lowering: when an activity is too fun to give up, teachers sometimes downgrade explain to match without noticing, and the lesson then succeeds at a goal no one set on purpose. The map exists to catch that drift in writing.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the alignment test in a different subject. A history objective reads: "Students will evaluate whether a primary source is reliable." TeachGen@i suggests a polished interactive timeline where students drag events into chronological order. The activity is handsome and students enjoy it. Run the test. The verb is evaluate, a judgment about reliability; the floor of the timeline activity is sequencing dates, which is recall of order. The gap is two rungs. The negotiated move keeps the timeline as a context-builder but adds the real task on top: for two of the timed events, students rate the source's reliability and write the reason. The map records the timeline as kept-but-demoted (now a warm-up, not the assessment) and the reliability rating as the added rung that finally reaches evaluate.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: in the left column write the objective's verb; in the right column write the cognitive demand the suggestion actually requires. For the fractions case, the left column says "explain (reasoning)" and the right column says "match (recognition)." A strong reader sees the gap immediately and can name the smallest modification that closes it. Your map is strong when it shows the verb, the suggestion's true floor, the gap between them, and the specific rung you added, rather than a vague note that you "made it more rigorous."

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A strong comparison map states the objective verb as the fixed test, not as background. It records the suggestion's actual cognitive floor (the least a student could do to pass), so the reviewer can see the gap you found. It shows a negotiated decision that preserves the appeal while adding the missing rung, rather than a blunt accept or reject. And it never shows the objective being quietly lowered to fit the activity. Polish is the attractive activity; evidence is the demonstrated alignment between what students will do and what you said they should learn.

## Transfer Case

Move the test to a writing class where the objective is "Students will revise a draft for clarity." TeachGen@i suggests an AI tool that rewrites student paragraphs to be clearer automatically. The activity is impressive and instant. Run the test: the verb is revise, which requires the student to do the revising; an automatic rewrite reduces the student's floor to accept, which reaches nothing on the objective. Here the gap cannot be closed by a small add-on, because the tool performs the very cognition the objective assigns to the student. The defensible decision is to repurpose the tool as a model the student critiques and then revises by hand. If you can reach that conclusion without copying the fractions logic, you understand alignment as a property of the student's cognitive work, not of the tool's output.
