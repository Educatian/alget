# Evidence for Portfolio Entry

![Evidence for Portfolio Entry textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/02-07-evidence-for-portfolio-entry.png)

## Why This Section Matters

A teaching portfolio is read by someone who was not in your room (a licensure reviewer, a hiring committee, a program assessor) and who must judge a competency from documents alone. The recurring failure is to confuse an artifact with evidence. A clean lesson plan is an artifact; it proves you can produce a document. Evidence is the artifact plus a claim it supports, the warrant linking the two, and an outcome that shows the claim held. "Uses formative assessment to revise instruction" is not demonstrated by a polished plan; it is demonstrated by data you collected, the revision you made because of it, and what changed afterward. The generative-AI era makes this concrete: empirical work finds markers cannot reliably distinguish GenAI-assisted from unassisted student work (Kofinas et al., 2025, BJET), which is exactly why product polish fails as competency evidence and the inspectable warrant and measured outcome must carry the weight. This section teaches the claim-warrant-outcome structure that converts artifacts into reviewable evidence, and the discipline of not overclaiming.

The artifact here is a Design Tension Studio map that lays out one competency claim, the warrant that supports it, the outcome that confirms it, and a deliberate non-claim. A reviewer should be able to verify each link rather than take a tidy document on faith.

## Opening Case

A candidate needs to evidence the competency "uses formative assessment data to revise instruction." She uploads her best-looking lesson plan, AI-polished and labeled "evidence of data-driven teaching." A reviewer reads it and cannot verify the claim: the plan is handsome, but nothing in it shows data being collected, a decision being made because of that data, or a result. It is an assertion wearing the costume of evidence. The fix is not a nicer plan. It is to attach the claim to a warrant a reviewer can inspect (the annotated exit tickets, the revision they prompted) and an outcome that shows the revision worked. Polish persuades no one who is trained to ask "how do I know?"

## Core Concept

A portfolio entry is an argument, and a valid argument has three parts plus a boundary. Borrowing the structure of evidence-based reasoning:

| Part | Question it answers | Weak version | Strong version |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Claim | What competency am I demonstrating? | "I am a good teacher" | "I use formative data to revise instruction" |
| Warrant | What links my artifact to the claim? | "Here is a lesson plan" | The annotated exit tickets plus the revised next-day opener |
| Outcome | How do I know it worked? | (omitted) | "The same error appeared on one ticket, not six" |
| Boundary | What does this entry NOT claim? | (overclaimed to five competencies) | "This evidences formative data only, not family communication" |

The competency the portfolio is really demonstrating is judgment, so the entry must show a decision, not a product. The boundary matters as much as the claim: a single lesson honestly evidences one or two competencies, and tagging it to five weakens the entry that is genuine. Critical edtech studies (Selwyn, 2014) adds a warning specific to AI-assisted portfolios: a tool can generate a flawless artifact in seconds, which makes the artifact worthless as evidence of your skill, and assessors cannot reliably distinguish generative-AI-assisted work from unaided work (Kofinas et al., 2025). The reviewer trusts the warrant and outcome, the things a tool cannot fabricate for you, not the polish a tool can.

## Worked Example

Walk through one annotated judgment call on building a portfolio entry. A candidate wants to evidence the competency "uses formative assessment data to revise instruction."

**Step 1. Resist artifact-as-evidence.**

> **Before (rejected entry):** Uploads a clean, AI-polished lesson plan labeled "evidence of data-driven teaching." Annotated: this is an artifact, not evidence. Nothing in it shows data being used or a revision happening.

**Step 2. Attach the claim, the warrant, and the outcome.**

> **After (strong entry):** Claim: "I use formative data to revise instruction." Warrant: the annotated set of twenty exit tickets showing six area-versus-perimeter errors, plus the revised next-day opener that sorts tasks by what is being measured. Outcome: "The following day, the same error appeared on one ticket instead of six." A reviewer can now check the claim against a located moment and a result.

**Step 3. Refuse to overclaim.** The candidate is tempted to tag the same artifact to five competencies. Decision recorded on the map: "Claim only the formative-data competency here. This single lesson does not genuinely evidence family communication or technology integration, and stretching it would weaken the entry that is real."

**Step 4. Leave it auditable.** The map shows one claim, one warrant, one outcome, and one deliberate non-claim. An external reviewer can verify each link rather than taking a polished plan on faith.

## Decision Rule

Pair every claim with a warrant a reviewer can inspect and an outcome that shows the claim held, and claim only what the evidence honestly supports. An artifact without a warrant and an outcome is a document, not evidence; an entry stretched across competencies it does not prove weakens itself.

Audit each portfolio entry against four questions:

1. **Claim:** Is the competency stated precisely enough to be checked?
2. **Warrant:** Can a reviewer inspect the link between my artifact and the claim?
3. **Outcome:** Is there a result showing the claim held, not just an intention?
4. **Boundary:** Am I claiming only what this evidence supports, and naming what it does not?

An entry missing the warrant or the outcome is an assertion, however polished the artifact.

The first misreading is artifact-as-evidence: believing that a beautiful product proves a competency. It proves you can make a product. The second is overclaiming, the temptation to tag one strong artifact to every competency on the rubric; reviewers discount an entry the moment one of its claims is visibly a stretch, and the inflation taints the honest claims beside it. The third, growing with generative tools, is mistaking AI polish for evidence of skill. When a reviewer suspects a tool wrote the artifact, the artifact's quality counts for nothing; only the warrant and outcome, which require your actual classroom, carry weight.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Build an entry for a different competency: "establishes a respectful, inclusive classroom environment." The weak entry uploads a printed list of class norms. Apply the structure. Claim: "I build inclusive norms with student input and enforce them consistently." Warrant: a photo of the norms students drafted in week one, plus two dated incident notes showing how a norm was applied when a conflict arose. Outcome: a mid-semester anonymous climate survey where the share of students reporting they felt safe to speak rose from 62 percent to 84 percent. Boundary: "This evidences inclusive climate; it does not by itself evidence differentiated instruction." The entry now lets a reviewer verify the claim through a survey result and dated practice rather than through a poster of good intentions.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: in the left column write each claim; in the right column write the warrant and outcome that would let a stranger verify it. For the formative-data case, the left column says "uses data to revise" and the right column says "annotated tickets plus revised opener; error dropped from six to one." A strong reader can scan any portfolio entry and immediately ask, for each claim, "where is the warrant and where is the outcome?" If either is missing, the entry is an assertion. Your map is strong when every claim has a verifiable companion in the right column and the boundary line is honest.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A strong portfolio entry states a precise claim, supplies a warrant a reviewer can inspect, and reports an outcome showing the claim held. It includes an explicit boundary, naming a competency this entry does NOT prove, which signals calibrated judgment rather than inflation. And it does not lean on the artifact's polish, because the entry's strength lives in the warrant and outcome that a tool could not have produced for the candidate. Polish is a clean document; evidence is the inspectable argument from claim to result, plus the honesty about its limits.

## Transfer Case

Move from a licensure portfolio to a grant report, where the audience is a funder asking whether their money produced the promised effect. The claim-warrant-outcome-boundary structure transfers directly: the claim is the funded objective, the warrant is the implementation data, the outcome is the measured change, and the boundary is the candid statement of what the grant did not move. The temptation to overclaim is even stronger here because funding depends on it, which makes the boundary line more, not less, important to credibility. If you can write a defensible grant report using this structure without copying the portfolio example, you understand evidence as an inspectable argument rather than as a format for one document type.
