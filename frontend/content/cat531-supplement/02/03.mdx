# Prompting for Classroom Constraints

![Prompting for Classroom Constraints textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/02-03-prompting-for-classroom-constraints.png)

## Why This Section Matters

Scenario setup tells the tool what room it is planning for; prompting for constraints tells it what it is not allowed to do. The two are different skills. A constraint is a boundary the plan must respect even when respecting it makes the plan worse on some other dimension: a hard time cap, a legally required accommodation, a no-homework week, a privacy rule. Generative tools are optimizers; left to their own devices they will quietly trade a constraint they were told to keep for an outcome they were trained to favor (more engagement, more coverage, a tidier flow). Prompting for constraints, and then verifying that the constraint actually held, is how a teacher keeps the tool inside the lines that law and equity draw.

This section teaches the difference between hard constraints (non-negotiable, often legal or ethical) and soft preferences (nice to have), and the discipline of checking compliance line by line rather than trusting that a stated constraint was honored. The transcript artifact records both the constraint you set and the verification you performed.

## Opening Case

A middle-school teacher has a student whose IEP guarantees extended time on written responses, a hard 38-minute period, and a school directive: no homework this week. She prompts TeachGen@i for "an engaging, rigorous civics lesson." The plan runs 55 minutes and ends with a take-home reading. She re-prompts, this time naming the time cap, the no-homework rule, and the extended-time accommodation. The new plan fits 38 minutes but drops the extended-time support to squeeze in a movement break. The tool treated a legal accommodation and a nice-to-have break as interchangeable constraints to juggle. The teacher's job is to mark which boundaries are immovable and verify they survived the optimization.

## Core Concept

Every constraint you give a tool falls into one of two classes, and conflating them is the central error of constraint prompting.

| | Hard constraint | Soft preference |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Definition | A boundary that must hold no matter the cost | A goal the plan should pursue if possible |
| Typical source | Law, IEP/504, policy, ethics, the bell schedule | Pedagogical taste, pacing, variety |
| Example | "Extended response time for one student" | "Include a movement break" |
| If violated | The plan is unusable or unlawful | The plan is merely less ideal |
| Correct instruction to the tool | "Non-negotiable; never drop" | "Optional; drop this first if something must give" |

The method has three moves. First, sort: label each constraint hard or soft before prompting, so you can tell the tool what it may sacrifice. Second, rank the soft preferences, so the tool knows the order to cut them in. Third, and most important, verify: read the returned plan against each hard constraint individually, because stating a constraint and the tool satisfying it are two separate events. TPACK keeps you honest about which content demands are real; critical edtech studies warns that an optimizer will always find a way to trade a quiet accommodation for a visible feature unless you forbid it explicitly and then check.

## Worked Example

Walk through one annotated judgment call on prompting for constraints. A middle-school teacher needs a 38-minute civics lesson, has a student with an IEP for extended response time, and may not assign homework this week.

**Step 1. Sort hard from soft.** Hard constraints (must hold): 38-minute cap, extended-time accommodation, no homework. Soft preferences (nice to have): a short movement break, a current-events hook.

**Step 2. Prompt and check, iteration by iteration.**

> **Prompt 1:** "Plan an engaging, rigorous civics lesson." Result ran 55 minutes and ended with a take-home reading. Annotated: adjectives are not constraints, and two hard limits were violated.

> **Prompt 2:** "Plan a 38-minute civics lesson, no homework, and build in extended response time for one student. A movement break is optional." Result dropped the extended-time support to fit a movement break. Annotated: the tool traded a hard accommodation for a soft preference.

> **Prompt 3:** "Protect extended response time as non-negotiable. Drop the movement break if needed. Keep the lesson at 38 minutes with no homework." Result honored all three hard constraints in 37 minutes.

**Step 3. Verify, do not assume.** The teacher re-read prompt 3's plan line by line against each hard constraint and confirmed compliance, then logged the rejected movement-break suggestion with the reason. The transcript now shows that stating a constraint and satisfying it are two different events, and the teacher checked both.

## Decision Rule

Mark every hard constraint as non-negotiable, tell the tool which soft preference to cut first, and verify each hard constraint by reading the plan rather than trusting the prompt. A constraint you stated but did not check is a constraint you do not know you have.

Before accepting any plan, run the compliance pass:

1. **Sort:** Is each constraint explicitly labeled hard or soft for the tool?
2. **Rank:** Have you told the tool the order to drop soft preferences in?
3. **Verify:** Have you read the plan against each hard constraint one at a time?
4. **Log:** Did you record any hard constraint the tool violated and the re-prompt that fixed it?

A plan that "feels" compliant has not been verified; only a line-by-line read counts.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is to assume that naming a constraint guarantees the tool kept it. Optimizers regress toward their training favorites; a stated accommodation can silently vanish in the next revision. The second is to over-trust the platform's own "this plan meets accessibility standards" badge and skip your own read. A vendor's compliance claim is a marketing surface, not a verified fit to your specific student's IEP. The under-documented constraint, the one you held in your head but never wrote into the prompt or the transcript, is the one most likely to be traded away and the hardest to defend later.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the sort-rank-verify method to a science lab. Hard constraints: a 50-minute period, a safety rule that the student who missed safety training does no open-flame work, and a budget line that allows no consumables over \$40 this unit. Soft preferences, ranked: (1) a real-world phenomenon hook, (2) lab partners chosen by students, (3) a digital data-collection sheet. Prompt the tool with the hard constraints marked non-negotiable and the soft list in cut-order. When the plan returns, verify each hard line: search the plan for any open-flame step assigned to that student, total the consumable cost against \$40, and confirm the timing. The transcript should show the cost arithmetic and the safety check explicitly, because "I told it the budget" is not evidence that the plan stayed under \$40.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: in the left column list each constraint and whether it is hard or soft; in the right column write the verification result (held, violated, or fixed in re-prompt N). For the civics case, the left column says "extended time, hard" and the right column says "violated in prompt 2, restored in prompt 3 after marking non-negotiable." A strong reader can look at any AI plan and ask, for each requirement, "what is my evidence this held?" If the only answer is "I asked for it," the reading is not strong yet.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A strong constraint transcript labels each constraint hard or soft so a reviewer can see what was protected and what was tradeable. It shows at least one iteration where the tool violated a hard constraint and the re-prompt that forced compliance, because the violation-and-fix is the clearest evidence of verification. And it includes a concrete verification trace (the cost total, the line-by-line accommodation check), not just the assertion that the plan complied. Polish is a clean final prompt; evidence is the visible check that the boundaries held.

## Transfer Case

Move the method from a single lesson to a multi-day field trip plan. The hard constraints multiply and harden: a fixed bus return time, a 1:8 chaperone ratio mandated by district policy, a peanut-free lunch for one student, and a strict spending cap. The optimizer's temptation also grows, because a richer itinerary is easy to favor over a boring ratio rule. The sort-rank-verify discipline transfers exactly, but verification now means counting chaperones against students per group and checking every food vendor against the allergy, not just reading for time. If you can build the field-trip compliance pass without reusing the civics example, you have learned constraint prompting as a verification habit rather than a phrasing trick.
