# Teacher Noticing and Revision

![Teacher Noticing and Revision textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/02-06-teacher-noticing-and-revision.png)

## Why This Section Matters

Teacher noticing is the professional skill of attending to what students actually do (in their work, their questions, their errors) and interpreting it before deciding what to change. It has two halves that novices tend to collapse: describing what happened and judging what it means. Strong noticing keeps them separate, because a description can be checked by a colleague while a verdict cannot. "The class does not get geometry" is a verdict with no foothold for action; "six of twenty tickets gave perimeter when the question asked for area" is a description that points to a specific revision. This section trains the discipline of grounding every instructional change in a described observation and a distinction between a one-off moment and a real pattern.

The artifact, a Design Tension Studio map, records the chain from observation to interpretation to revision, and the alternative revision you rejected. A reviewer should be able to trace why the change was made, not merely that something changed after a hard day.

## Opening Case

A teacher finishes a geometry lesson feeling that it went badly and reaches for the verdict "they just do not get it." That sentence ends inquiry; there is nothing in it to act on, and tomorrow's revision would be a guess. Then she actually reads the twenty exit tickets. Six students gave the perimeter when the question asked for area, and four of those six wrote the correct area formula before adding the side lengths instead of multiplying. The verdict said the class failed at geometry. The description says the class knows the formula and is confusing two concepts. Those point to opposite revisions. Noticing is the difference between reteaching everything and fixing the one confusion the evidence reveals.

## Core Concept

Noticing has a structure: observe, then interpret, then decide, and a revision is only defensible when each step is visible and linked to the next. Two distinctions do most of the work.

The first is description versus verdict. A description reports what was on the page or in the room in terms another teacher could verify ("six tickets swapped area and perimeter"). A verdict reports a conclusion that skips the evidence ("they do not get geometry"). Verdicts feel like noticing but cannot be audited, and they tend to trigger overbroad revisions.

The second is moment versus pattern. One confused student is a moment, handled with a quick check-in. The same error across a third of the class is a pattern that justifies changing the next lesson. Conflating them leads to two failures: overreacting to a single off comment with a whole-unit overhaul, or ignoring a class-wide pattern because it surfaced quietly. The defensible revision names the pattern, links it to a described observation, and chooses a change that targets the diagnosed cause rather than a generic "do it again." Teacher professional judgment is exactly this disciplined chain; AI tools can surface aggregate patterns from exit data, but the interpretation of what an error means stays with the teacher who knows the lesson.

## Worked Example

Walk through one annotated judgment call that moves from noticing to revision. A teacher finishes a geometry lesson and reviews twenty exit tickets.

**Step 1. Record the observable, not the verdict.**

> **Weak note (rejected):** "The class just does not get geometry." That is a verdict with nothing to act on.

> **Noticing note (kept):** "Six of twenty exit tickets gave the perimeter when the question asked for area; four of those six wrote the correct area formula but then added the side lengths instead of multiplying." The teacher described what was on the page before deciding what it meant.

**Step 2. Weigh the moment against the pattern.** One confused student would warrant a quick check. Six tickets showing the same area-versus-perimeter swap is a pattern worth a revision, and the teacher notes that distinction so a single reaction does not trigger an overhaul.

**Step 3. Make the cause-linked revision.** Recorded on the map: "Because six tickets confused area with perimeter, tomorrow opens with a two-minute sort: students place six tasks under 'measures the inside' or 'measures the border' before any formula appears. Rejected option: reteach the area formula again, because the tickets show the formula is known; the two ideas are being confused, not the procedure."

**Step 4. Leave the link visible.** The map now ties the described observation directly to the revision and explains the rejected alternative. A mentor can trace why the change was made, not just that a change happened.

## Decision Rule

Describe before you judge, distinguish a moment from a pattern, and link every revision to the specific observation that motivated it. A revision that cannot name the observed evidence behind it is a reaction, not a professional decision.

Before committing a revision, check the chain:

1. **Describe:** Is my note a verifiable observation or an unfalsifiable verdict?
2. **Scale:** Is this a single moment or a pattern across enough students to justify changing the plan?
3. **Cause:** Does my revision target the diagnosed cause (a conceptual confusion) rather than a generic redo?
4. **Reject:** What plausible revision did I rule out, and why does the evidence rule it out?

If the revision floats free of a described observation, the chain is broken.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is to treat a strong feeling about a lesson as data. The sense that "that flopped" is a starting prompt to look, not a finding to act on. The second is to overcorrect from a single salient moment: one student's loud confusion can trigger a needless overhaul while a quiet class-wide gap goes unaddressed. The third is to reach for the most familiar revision (reteach the formula) regardless of what the evidence shows; if students already wrote the formula correctly, reteaching it wastes a day and leaves the actual confusion untouched. The map's "rejected option" line exists to catch this reflex.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the observe-interpret-decide chain to a writing class. The teacher's impression is that "the essays were disorganized." That is a verdict. Reading the drafts produces a description: eleven of twenty-four essays state a claim in the first paragraph but never return to it, while the body paragraphs each introduce a new, unconnected point. That is a pattern, and it is specific: the issue is not organization in general but the missing link between thesis and evidence. The cause-linked revision is a five-minute mini-lesson where students draw an arrow from each body paragraph back to the thesis and rewrite any paragraph whose arrow does not connect. The rejected revision is "review essay structure," which is too broad to touch the specific thesis-to-body break the drafts revealed.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: in the left column write the described observation; in the right column write the interpretation and the revision it justifies. For the geometry case, the left column says "six tickets swapped area and perimeter; four had the right formula" and the right column says "concept confusion, not procedure; revise with a sort task, not a reteach." A strong reader can spot when a revision in the right column has no anchor in the left, which is the signature of a reaction dressed up as noticing. Your map is strong when every revision traces back to a line you could show a colleague.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A strong noticing map records the observation as a verifiable description, with counts or specifics, not as a verdict. It states explicitly whether the issue is a moment or a pattern and on what evidence. It links the revision to the diagnosed cause, so a reviewer can see why this change and not a generic one. And it names the revision it rejected with the reason the evidence rules it out. Polish is a tidy reflection paragraph; evidence is the visible chain from what students did to what you will change because of it.

## Transfer Case

Move noticing from exit tickets to live, in-the-moment classroom observation during a discussion. The structure holds but the evidence is fleeting: you cannot reread a comment the way you reread a ticket. The discipline becomes capturing the description fast (a tally of how many students cited the text versus offered opinion without it) before the impression hardens into a verdict, and waiting for a pattern across several exchanges before changing your questioning. If you can adapt observe-interpret-decide to ephemeral spoken evidence without collapsing into "the discussion was flat," you have learned noticing as a habit of grounding judgment in described observation rather than as a paperwork step tied to tickets.
