## Why This Section Matters

A classroom routine is a repeated, low-decision sequence (the warm-up, the exit slip, the turn-and-talk, the cleanup) that runs almost automatically once students learn it. Routines matter because they conserve a teacher's scarcest resource, attention. When the procedure is automatic, the teacher can spend cognitive effort on the lesson rather than on crowd control. The risk in a tech-rich classroom is that a device or app silently rewrites the routine without anyone deciding it should. A polling app turns "raise your hand" into "log in and submit"; a learning platform turns "I noticed Maya struggling" into "the dashboard flagged a red cell." Each substitution changes who has access, what counts as participation, and where student data goes.

This section teaches you to treat a tech-rich routine as a decision you make on purpose, not a default the tool installs for you. The artifact you build, a Design Tension Studio map, forces that decision into the open: it names the value that the technology helps (often speed, reach, or instant feedback) and the value it threatens (often access, privacy, or a slower kind of thinking), then records the compromise you negotiated and why.

## Opening Case

Mr. Alvarez runs a daily five-minute math warm-up. For years it was a printed problem on the board; students worked it silently while he took attendance. A vendor demo convinced his department to adopt a live-polling app: students answer on phones, the screen shows a real-time bar chart, and "engagement" jumps because everyone is tapping. Three weeks in, he notices that the four students who never bring charged phones now sit out the warm-up entirely, and a fifth copies a neighbor's screen to avoid looking unprepared. The tool did not just speed up his routine; it quietly converted a universally accessible task into one gated by hardware. The question for the map is not "is polling good?" but "which value did this routine trade away, and was that trade defensible for these students?"

## Core Concept

A tech-rich routine should be analyzed as a swap, not an upgrade. Every time a tool enters a routine, it trades one set of properties for another, and a defensible teacher names both sides of the trade before keeping the tool. Three properties of a routine almost always shift when technology enters it.

| Routine property | What the analog version protects | What the digital version often trades it for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Access | Anyone present can participate (paper, voice, hands) | Participation now requires a device, login, or bandwidth |
| Data exposure | Responses stay in the room and disappear | Responses are stored, scored, and may be retained or profiled by a vendor under FERPA's "school official" exception (34 CFR § 99.31(a)(1)(i)(B)); outright sale or disclosure of identifiable records without consent is restricted. The concern is concrete enough that New York State banned facial-recognition technology in schools in 2023 after a statewide privacy review |
| Pace of thinking | Time to reason before answering | Speed and instant scoring, which can reward guessing |

The work of this section is to run any candidate routine through this swap analysis. [TPACK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_pedagogical_content_knowledge) supplies the discipline: a tool is only justified when its technological affordance genuinely serves both the pedagogical move (here, a quick diagnostic) and the content (the specific math idea), rather than serving novelty. Critical edtech studies (Selwyn, 2016) supplies the suspicion: ask who is burdened by the swap and whose data is now in motion. A routine passes when you can state the learning purpose, the value you protected, and the value you knowingly traded with eyes open.

## Worked Example

Walk through one annotated judgment call on a real routine. A seventh-grade science teacher wants a daily warm-up routine and is deciding between two designs for the same five minutes.

**Step 1. Name the learning move.** The warm-up should surface the most common error from yesterday so the teacher can open with a targeted re-teach. That is the purpose the routine must serve.

**Step 2. Lay out the value tension on the map.** Speed of feedback (a live polling app that scores instantly) competes with access (three students have unreliable home logins and arrive having missed the prompt). Naming this tension is the work; it is not a tooling preference.

**Step 3. Make the before/after decision.**

> **Before (tool-first draft):** "Students answer a TeachGen@i poll the night before; the dashboard ranks responses, and we open class with the leaderboard." A mentor reading this sees engagement language and a home-access assumption, but no fallback and no learning move tied to the data.

> **After (defensible revision):** "Students answer the same prompt in the first ninety seconds of class on any device or on a printed half-sheet. I read the three most common wrong answers off the dashboard, then re-teach that one error for three minutes. Decision recorded on the map: I rejected the home-login version because it excluded the three students with unreliable access and pushed the diagnostic outside the room where I cannot act on it."

**Step 4. Leave the evidence.** The after version names the tension (feedback speed vs. access), the negotiated decision (in-class entry with a non-digital fallback), and the rejected option with its reason. A mentor can now audit the call instead of taking "it was engaging" on trust.

## Decision Rule

For a routine, protect access before you optimize speed. Concretely: a routine is defensible only if every student physically present can complete it without owning, charging, or logging into a personal device. If the digital version cannot meet that floor, keep the digital version as an enhancement layered on top of an analog floor (the printed half-sheet, the spoken answer), never as the only door in.

Run a candidate routine through three checks before you commit it:

1. **Access floor:** Can a student with no working device still participate fully today?
2. **Data check:** What student information leaves the room, who stores it, and is that exposure proportional to the learning gain? This check has legal teeth: under FERPA (20 U.S.C. § 1232g; 34 CFR Part 99), a vendor may receive identifiable student records without consent only as a "school official" under the school's direct control, using the data solely for authorized purposes, and under the amended COPPA Rule (16 CFR Part 312, 2025) operators need separate verifiable parental consent before disclosing under-13 students' data to third parties such as targeted advertisers.
3. **Pace check:** Does the tool give students time to reason, or does its scoring clock reward fast guessing over thinking?

A routine that fails the access floor is not ready, no matter how engaging the dashboard looks.

The most common error is to read engagement metrics (taps, logins, time-on-task) as evidence of learning; the learning-analytics literature itself warns that behavioral engagement proxies such as clickstream and time-on-task do not equate to learning outcomes (Bergdahl et al., 2024). A bar chart that updates in real time feels like proof, but it only shows that students who could log in did. It is silent about the students it excluded and about whether anyone reasoned. A second misreading is to treat the tool's default settings as neutral. A polling app that defaults to a public leaderboard has made a pedagogical choice (competition, exposure) on your behalf; if you keep that default, you own that choice. The map should record which defaults you accepted and which you overrode.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the swap analysis to one concrete routine: the exit ticket. The analog exit ticket is a sticky note answered on the way out; it is universal, private, and disappears after the teacher reads it. Suppose a platform offers a digital exit ticket that auto-scores responses and builds a per-student trend line. Name the swap: you gain instant aggregation and a longitudinal record, and you trade away the access floor (students without a device cannot answer) and the privacy floor (responses now persist in a vendor system tied to student names).

A defensible revision keeps the gain without paying the full price. You let students answer on a device or on paper, you collect the paper answers into the same tally by hand, and you turn off the per-student trend line because a daily error pattern does not justify a permanent profile. The map records one accepted feature (aggregation), one rejected feature (the persistent profile), and the reason: the learning purpose was a same-day diagnostic, not a surveillance history.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: in the left column write the routine and the value the tool advertises; in the right column write the value the tool quietly costs. For the polling warm-up, the left column says "instant feedback, high engagement" and the right column says "excludes uncharged phones, rewards guessing under the clock." A strong reader of this section can look at any tech-rich routine and immediately ask: what is on the right column that the vendor did not mention? Your map is strong when its right column is as specific as its left.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A strong routine map does three things a weak one does not. First, it states the analog baseline, so a reader can see exactly what changed when the tool entered. Second, it names the access floor explicitly and shows how the final routine meets it (the printed fallback, the spoken option). Third, it records at least one tool feature you turned off or refused, with the reason, because a routine that accepts every default is a tool's design, not a teacher's. Polish (clean icons, a tidy layout) is the last layer; if the access floor is missing, no amount of polish makes the routine defensible.

## Transfer Case

Move the polling warm-up to a one-to-one district where every student has a charged, school-managed tablet. The access floor is now met by the hardware, so the dominant tension shifts: speed versus pace of thinking becomes the live conflict, and the data question sharpens because school-managed devices log everything by default. The routine does not copy over unchanged; the value you must protect changes from access to thinking time and data minimization. If you can re-derive the map for the new setting instead of pasting the old one, you understand routines as swaps rather than as fixed recipes.
