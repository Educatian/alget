# Module 2 Teaching Plan Studio

![Module 2 Teaching Plan Studio textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/02-08-module-2-teaching-plan-studio.png)

## Why This Section Matters

This capstone asks you to fuse the module's separate skills (scenario setup, constraint prompting, suggestion comparison, learner-variability adaptation, and noticing) into one coherent teaching plan rather than a stack of disconnected pieces. The distinction that decides the grade is coherence versus stapling. A stapled plan places good components next to each other with nothing holding them together; a coherent plan subordinates every component to a single objective, so each move earns its place by serving that goal. The harder professional act is naming a real tradeoff: a 40-minute period cannot do everything a standard contains, and a defensible plan chooses depth on the right idea and says so, rather than pretending to cover it all. This section is about integration and principled choice under a fixed time budget.

The artifact is a Design Tension Studio map that shows one objective, the module's moves integrated around it, a defended tradeoff, and your documented AI judgments. A reviewer should see a plan that is unmistakably your professional work, not the tool's assembled output.

## Opening Case

A candidate assembles the Module 2 capstone by pasting four things in sequence: the scenario he wrote, the three prompts he iterated, his adaptation list, and his noticing log. It looks complete; every required piece is present. But nothing refers to anything else. The scenario sets a 40-minute, no-device period, yet the adaptation list assumes a tablet app, and the noticing plan watches for an error the lesson never sets up. It is a folder, not a plan. The reviewer cannot tell what the lesson is for, because no single objective pulls the parts together. Completeness is not coherence; a plan is coherent only when removing any piece would visibly damage the goal.

## Core Concept

A coherent plan is built around one objective that every component is accountable to, and it makes its tradeoffs explicit rather than hiding them.

| Move from earlier sections | How it must serve the single objective |
| --- | --- |
| Scenario setup (02) | Fixes the binding constraints (40 minutes, no devices) the whole plan honors |
| Constraint prompting (03) | Produces a plan that provably fits those constraints |
| Suggestion comparison (04) | Keeps only activities whose cognitive demand matches the objective's verb |
| Variability adaptation (05) | Gives every learner access to the same objective |
| Noticing plan (06) | Watches for the specific error the objective is most likely to produce |

Integration means each move points back to the objective; if a component would survive unchanged no matter what the objective was, it is stapled, not integrated. The second demand is the tradeoff. A standard usually spans more than a single period can teach well, so the plan must choose: depth on the idea where the evidence shows reasoning breaks down, and an honest note that the rest moves to a later lesson. Naming the tradeoff (coverage versus depth) and defending the choice with evidence is the mark of professional judgment. Finally, because the plan was built with TeachGen@i, the map must keep your AI decisions visible (what you accepted, what you rejected, why), because a capstone is your work only when the tool's contributions are documented and bounded.

## Worked Example

Walk through one annotated judgment call on assembling the Module 2 capstone. A candidate must turn the module's separate moves into one defensible 40-minute plan for a grade-7 lesson on comparing fractions.

**Step 1. Reject stapling.**

> **Before (rejected):** Four sections pasted in sequence: a scenario, three prompts, an adaptation list, and a noticing log, none referring to the others. Annotated: this is a folder, not a plan. Nothing shows the parts working toward one goal.

**Step 2. Integrate around one objective.** Objective: "Students explain why two fractions with the same denominator compare as they do." Each module move is tied back to it: the scenario fixes the 40-minute, no-device constraints; the constrained prompts produce a plan that fits them; the adaptation gives below-level readers an audio and visual route to the same explanation task; the noticing plan watches for the area-versus-comparison confusion seen earlier in the module.

**Step 3. Name and defend the tradeoff.** Recorded on the map: "Coverage vs. depth. The standard spans comparing, ordering, and equivalence, but 40 minutes only allows depth on one. I chose comparing same-denominator fractions because the module's exit-ticket data showed that is where reasoning broke down. Ordering and equivalence move to the next lesson."

**Step 4. Keep the AI judgment visible.** The candidate logs one rejected TeachGen@i suggestion (a device-based fraction game that violated the no-device scenario constraint) with the reason. The final map shows one objective, integrated moves, a defended tradeoff, and documented AI decisions, which is what makes the capstone the candidate's professional work rather than the tool's output.

## Decision Rule

Subordinate every component to one objective and defend your coverage-versus-depth tradeoff with evidence. If a component would survive unchanged under a different objective, it is stapled and must be re-tied to the goal or cut.

Audit the capstone for coherence before submitting:

1. **One objective:** Can I state the single goal every component serves?
2. **Accountability:** Does each move point back to that goal, or could it belong to any lesson?
3. **Tradeoff:** Have I named what I chose not to cover and defended the choice with evidence?
4. **AI visibility:** Are my accepted and rejected tool suggestions documented with reasons?

A plan that is complete but not accountable to one objective is a folder, not a capstone.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is to equate completeness with coherence: having every required section present is necessary but not sufficient, because the sections can sit inert beside one another. The second is to attempt full coverage in a single period, producing a plan that touches comparing, ordering, and equivalence so lightly that students master none; refusing to choose is itself a failed decision. The third is to leave the AI's contributions invisible, submitting a tool-generated plan as if it were entirely yours. An undocumented capstone cannot show whose judgment shaped it, and the competency under assessment is precisely your judgment.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Integrate a capstone in a different subject to see the structure rather than the fractions instance. A high-school biology candidate has a 50-minute period and the objective "Students construct an evidence-based claim about why a population changed." The scenario fixes the constraints (50 minutes, a shared data set, no lab that day). The constrained prompt yields a plan that fits. Suggestion comparison rejects a flashy simulation that only asks students to watch, because the verb is construct a claim, not observe. The adaptation gives a multilingual learner a sentence frame for the claim while keeping the same reasoning demand. The noticing plan watches for the common error of citing data that does not support the claim. The tradeoff: the standard also covers mutation mechanisms, but the candidate spends the depth on claim construction because that is the assessed reasoning, and notes mechanisms for the next day. Same structure, new content, one accountable objective.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: in the left column write each component of the plan; in the right column write the sentence explaining how it serves the single objective. If any right-column cell reads "it does not, it is just included," that component is stapled. For the fractions capstone, every left-column move (scenario, prompts, adaptation, noticing) has a right-column sentence tying it to "explain why same-denominator fractions compare as they do." A strong reader can also locate the tradeoff line and check that it is defended with evidence, not asserted. Your map is strong when no component floats free of the objective.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A strong capstone map states one objective and shows every component accountable to it, so a reviewer can see integration rather than assembly. It names a coverage-versus-depth tradeoff and defends the depth choice with evidence from the module (the exit-ticket pattern), not with preference. It keeps the AI decisions visible, with at least one rejected suggestion and its reason, marking the plan as the candidate's bounded use of the tool. Polish is a clean, well-formatted plan; evidence is the demonstrated coherence and the defended choice that show a teacher's judgment, not a tool's output.

## Transfer Case

Move the capstone from a single lesson to a week-long unit plan. The integration demand scales up: now five lessons must each be accountable to a unit objective, and the tradeoff becomes a sequencing decision about which sub-skills get a full day and which share one. The risk of stapling grows, because it is even easier to line up five separate good lessons that never build on each other. The same audit applies: one unit objective, each lesson accountable to it, a defended choice about depth across the week, and documented AI decisions throughout. If you can carry the coherence test from one period to a full unit without losing the single-objective spine, you have learned integration as a planning principle rather than as a way to organize one assignment.
