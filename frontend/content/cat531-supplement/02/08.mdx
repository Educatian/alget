## Core Concept

The core idea in this section is that **Module 2 Teaching Plan Studio** should be treated as a relationship among purpose, constraint, evidence, and revision. Purpose names what the learner or audience needs to accomplish. Constraint names what limits the decision. Evidence names the source that makes the decision defensible. Revision shows what changed after the evidence was considered.

Three course anchors shape the reasoning here:

- **TPACK** helps you decide what counts as a meaningful learning or performance demand.
- **critical edtech studies** helps you notice when a design or technology choice creates risk, burden, or unsupported assumptions.
- **AI and education datafication** helps you check whether the artifact works for varied learners, audiences, or use contexts.

The anchors should do work in your artifact. If TPACK or critical edtech studies does not change a decision, it is only a citation rather than evidence of judgment.

## Worked Example

Walk through one annotated judgment call on assembling the Module 2 capstone. A candidate must turn the module's separate moves into one defensible 40-minute plan for a grade-7 lesson on comparing fractions.

**Step 1. Reject stapling.**

> **Before (rejected):** Four sections pasted in sequence: a scenario, three prompts, an adaptation list, and a noticing log, none referring to the others. Annotated: this is a folder, not a plan. Nothing shows the parts working toward one goal.

**Step 2. Integrate around one objective.** Objective: "Students explain why two fractions with the same denominator compare as they do." Each module move is tied back to it: the scenario fixes the 40-minute, no-device constraints; the constrained prompts produce a plan that fits them; the adaptation gives below-level readers an audio and visual route to the same explanation task; the noticing plan watches for the area-versus-comparison confusion seen earlier in the module.

**Step 3. Name and defend the tradeoff.** Recorded on the map: "Coverage vs. depth. The standard spans comparing, ordering, and equivalence, but 40 minutes only allows depth on one. I chose comparing same-denominator fractions because the module's exit-ticket data showed that is where reasoning broke down. Ordering and equivalence move to the next lesson."

**Step 4. Keep the AI judgment visible.** The candidate logs one rejected TeachGen@i suggestion (a device-based fraction game that violated the no-device scenario constraint) with the reason. The final map shows one objective, integrated moves, a defended tradeoff, and documented AI decisions, which is what makes the capstone the candidate's professional work rather than the tool's output.

## Decision Rule

Use the classroom value conflict first, the technology affordance second, and the policy boundary third. A tool choice is justified only when it protects students while improving a specific learning activity.

Use the following check when the artifact looks finished but the reasoning may still be hidden:

1. **Purpose check:** What should the audience be able to do after reading or using the artifact?
2. **Evidence check:** Which exact source, observation, annotation, rubric line, data pattern, or policy boundary justifies the revision?
3. **Revision check:** What changed in the artifact, and what suggestion did you reject or modify?

If you cannot answer all three questions, the artifact may be complete, but it is not yet ready as evidence of learning.

## Common Misreadings

Another misreading is to write a general teaching reflection after the artifact is finished instead of documenting the decision while it is being revised.

A colleague should be able to see why the tool belongs in this learning activity and where its limits remain.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Read this section as a small professional decision cycle, not as a list of terms. First, locate the work product: **Design Tension Studio map naming a classroom value conflict and negotiated decision**. Then ask what problem the artifact is supposed to solve inside technology-rich routines and TeachGen@i planning. A strong reader can explain the artifact in plain language before naming any theory: who will use it, what decision it supports, and what would count as evidence that it improved.

Next, connect the artifact to the course anchors: **TPACK**, **critical edtech studies**, **teacher professional judgment**. The point is not to cite every anchor. The point is to let one anchor change the artifact. If the anchor does not change wording, layout, evidence, timing, or feedback, it has not yet become design reasoning.

Finally, inspect the revision trail. A weak submission says the artifact was improved. A strong submission shows the earlier version, the issue noticed, the support requested, the decision accepted or rejected, and the reason the final version is better for the intended learner or audience.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: **claim** on the left, **evidence** on the right. The claim is the decision you think the section is teaching. The evidence is the exact phrase, example, annotation, support move, or artifact revision that makes the claim inspectable. By the end of CAT 531 02.08, your notes should make it possible to reconstruct your reasoning without asking you to explain it again.
