# School Policy Interpretation

![School Policy Interpretation textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/03-06-school-policy-interpretation.png)

## Why This Section Matters

AI policy in schools is being written faster than it can be written well. Districts, racing to respond, issue memos that are vague, contradictory, or silent on the cases that actually arise in classrooms. The teacher is the person who must turn that imperfect text into a real decision about a real student, often on the spot. This makes policy interpretation a core professional skill, not a compliance afterthought, because the same sentence can protect a student or harm one depending on how it is read.

This section matters because two opposite failures are both common and both damaging. One teacher reads policy too literally, applying the words mechanically even when doing so produces an absurd or harmful result. Another treats policy silence as permission, assuming that whatever the memo does not forbid must be allowed. Skilled interpretation avoids both: it reads for the policy's purpose, checks for higher-authority rules that override it, and recognizes that a gap is a question to be raised, not a loophole to be exploited. A teacher who interprets well protects students, stays defensible, and improves the policy by surfacing its blind spots.

## Opening Case

A district memo states: "Students may not use AI tools to complete assignments." A teacher has a student with an IEP that legally requires an AI-powered text-to-speech tool to access reading, and another student who wants to use a grammar checker on a final draft. A literal reading of the memo would strip the accommodation from the first student and forbid the grammar tool for the second. Down the hall, a colleague reads the same memo and concludes that since it only mentions "assignments," using AI to write a college recommendation letter for a student is fine, because the memo is silent on that.

Both teachers are misreading, in opposite directions. The first applies the words so literally that he would violate federal disability law and defeat the rule's obvious purpose. The second treats silence as permission and is about to put AI-generated content into a high-stakes document with a student's name on it. The memo is the same; the harm comes from how it is read. The skill the situation demands is interpreting for purpose, checking what outranks the memo, and noticing where the memo simply has not decided.

## Core Concept

Interpreting school policy well means reading a text the way a thoughtful professional reads a rule, not the way a machine matches keywords. Four moves separate sound interpretation from the two common failures.

- **Read for purpose, not just words.** Every policy exists to accomplish something. The "no AI on assignments" rule exists to stop students from offloading the thinking the assignment is meant to develop. Read against that purpose, a text-to-speech accommodation (which provides access, not thinking) clearly is not the target, even though a literal reading would sweep it in. When the words and the purpose diverge, the purpose usually governs, and you document why.
- **Check the hierarchy of authority.** Policies do not exist alone. Federal law (IDEA, FERPA, Section 504), state regulation, and district policy form a stack, and a lower rule cannot override a higher one. A district AI memo cannot cancel a legally mandated IEP accommodation. Knowing what outranks the memo in front of you is essential, because the most confident-sounding local rule may be the one with the least authority.
- **Treat silence as a question, not permission.** When a policy does not address a case, that is a gap, not a green light. The professional move is to reason from the policy's purpose to a defensible interim decision and then route the gap upward to whoever owns the policy, so it gets clarified rather than guessed at repeatedly.
- **Document the interpretation.** Because you are reading judgment into ambiguous text, you record what the text said, how you read its purpose, what higher rule applied, and why you decided as you did. That record is what makes the decision defensible if it is later questioned.

The throughline is that a policy is **an intent expressed imperfectly in words**. The literalist reads the words and ignores the intent; the loophole-seeker exploits the gap between them. The skilled interpreter recovers the intent, respects the authority stack, and flags the gaps, which protects students and strengthens the policy at the same time.

## Worked Example

A district AI policy says: "Teachers must obtain parental consent before using AI tools that collect student data." A teacher wants to use a free AI writing-feedback site with her class next week. Apply the moves.

Read for purpose: the rule exists to protect student data and family autonomy over it, not to ban AI feedback. So the question is not "is this an AI tool" (the literal trigger) but "does this tool collect student data" (the purpose). Check the hierarchy: she pulls the tool's privacy policy and finds it stores student-submitted text and shares it with third parties. Now FERPA, which outranks the district memo, is implicated, and the consent requirement is not merely district preference but legally reinforced. The free tool is squarely inside the rule's purpose.

Now handle the gap. The memo says "obtain consent" but does not specify how, or what to do for the three families who will not return a form in time. She reasons from purpose to an interim decision: she selects an alternative feedback tool that does not transmit student data off-site for the unconsented students, uses the original tool only for consented ones, and emails the policy owner asking for a standard consent template and a default rule for non-responses. She documents all of it. The literalist might have banned all AI feedback; the loophole-seeker might have used the free tool because "it's just feedback." She used the purpose and the authority stack to reach a decision that protects data, keeps the lesson running, and improves the policy by surfacing its missing procedure.

## Worked Decision: Interpreting an Ambiguous AI Policy

Follow a teacher's annotated before/after judgment while interpreting a district memo that reads: "AI tools may not be used to complete student assessments."

1. **Before (literal reading):** "The rule says no AI on assessments, so I must turn off the text-to-speech AI that my student with an IEP uses during the unit test." The only evidence is the literal sentence.
2. **Purpose named:** Annotate the intent. The rule exists to stop students from offloading their thinking, not to block accommodations. Text-to-speech does not do the thinking; it provides access.
3. **Overlapping rules checked:** Annotate the policy intersection. The student's IEP legally requires read-aloud support. The AI memo cannot override a federally mandated accommodation, and silence on this case is not permission to deny it.
4. **After (revised decision):** "I will keep the text-to-speech accommodation on for the IEP student and turn off generative answer features for everyone. I am documenting that the memo's wording is ambiguous about accommodations and routing the case to the policy owner to request clearer language."
5. **Evidence trail:** The decision is justified by reading the policy for purpose, by the IEP requirement, and by **teacher professional judgment**. The literal reading is recorded as the rejected interpretation, with the reason it would have caused harm.

Step 1 to step 4 captures the section's lesson: policy interpretation reads for intent, checks overlapping rules, and flags gaps upward, rather than applying words literally or treating silence as permission.

## Decision Rule

When an AI policy meets a real classroom case, interpret it deliberately:

1. **Recover the purpose.** Ask what harm the rule exists to prevent, and read the case against that purpose, not only against the literal words.
2. **Check the authority stack.** Identify any federal or state law (IDEA, FERPA, Section 504) that outranks the local memo and may compel or forbid the action regardless of what the memo says.
3. **Name silence as a gap.** If the policy does not address the case, do not treat that as permission; reason to a defensible interim decision and route the gap to the policy owner.
4. **Document the reasoning.** Record the text, the purpose reading, the controlling rule, and your decision so it is defensible.

When literal words and clear purpose conflict, purpose governs and you write down why; when a higher rule applies, it controls.

## Common Misreadings

The two headline errors are literalism and the silence-equals-permission move, and they pull in opposite directions from the same root: ignoring the policy's purpose. A third misreading is assuming the most local, specific rule is the most authoritative, when a district memo cannot override federal disability or privacy law. A fourth is interpreting alone and never documenting, which leaves a sound decision undefendable later. A subtler error is reading a gap as the policy writer's intent ("they must have meant to allow it"), when a gap usually means the case was never considered and needs to be raised, not assumed.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the interpretation moves to a hard case. A school's policy reads: "AI-detection tools will be used to ensure academic integrity." A teacher's detector flags a multilingual student's essay at 85%. Does the policy require her to act on the score?

Recover the purpose: the rule's aim is academic integrity, that is, ensuring students do their own work. A false accusation against a student who did do her own work serves that purpose not at all; it actively harms it. So "will be used" cannot reasonably mean "scores must trigger consequences," because that would undermine the very integrity the policy protects. Check the authority stack: due-process and nondiscrimination obligations sit above a procedural memo, and acting on a tool with a known bias against multilingual writers risks both. Those higher principles constrain how the detector may be used.

Name the gap: the policy says detectors "will be used" but is silent on how scores translate to action, which is exactly the ambiguity causing the problem. The teacher's defensible interim reading is that the detector may inform her professional review but cannot by itself be evidence, so she gathers process evidence before drawing any conclusion. She then routes the gap upward, recommending the policy specify that detector scores are advisory only. She documents the whole chain. The literal reading would have her punish a likely-innocent student; the purposive reading, checked against higher principles and the policy's gap, protects the student and the policy's actual goal.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

A strong reader of policy never stops at the words. They ask what the rule is for, what outranks it, and where it goes quiet. As you read, keep a two-column note with the **literal text** on the left and the **purpose, controlling rule, or gap** on the right. If the right column is empty, you have only read the policy, not interpreted it.

A strong analysis can answer four questions about any policy applied to a case:

1. What harm is this rule meant to prevent, and does the literal reading serve or defeat that?
2. What higher-authority law or principle governs this case regardless of the memo?
3. Is the policy actually silent here, and if so what is the defensible interim decision plus the question to route upward?
4. What documentation would make my interpretation defensible if challenged?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

Your policy memo is strong when it shows interpretation, not transcription. Quote the relevant policy text, state the purpose you read into it, name any higher rule that controls, and identify the gaps you would flag upward. The weakest memos restate the policy and declare compliance; the strongest show a teacher reasoning from purpose, catching where the literal words would cause harm, and proposing the clarification the policy needs.

A useful closing line for the artifact would read: "I read the no-AI-on-assessments rule for its purpose (preventing offloaded thinking) rather than its literal words, kept the IEP-mandated text-to-speech accommodation because federal law outranks the memo and the tool provides access rather than thinking, and flagged the memo's silence on accommodations to the policy owner for clarification."

## Transfer Case

Move from interpreting a written policy to interpreting an absence of one. A new AI tool appears for which your district has issued no guidance at all. The whole policy is now a gap, so the silence-is-not-permission principle becomes the entire decision: you cannot point to a rule, so you reason directly from the underlying purposes the district's other policies protect (privacy, equity, learning integrity), make a conservative interim choice, and proactively ask for guidance. Interpreting well when there is no text to interpret, by reasoning from purpose and authority rather than from words, is the test that you understand policy interpretation as judgment rather than as reading comprehension.
