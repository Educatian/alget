## Why This Section Matters

The arrival of generative AI broke the oldest assumption in assessment: that a finished, fluent piece of writing is evidence that a student did the thinking. For decades, a polished essay was a reasonable proxy for learning, because producing polish required effort that overlapped with the learning itself. Generative AI severs that link. A student can now submit text they could not have produced and may not even understand, and it will look indistinguishable from mastery. That single change forces every teacher to rebuild how they read student work.

This section is about that rebuild. The wrong response, and the most common one, is to fight the new tools with detection software and harsher penalties, treating integrity as a policing problem. The professional response is to shift what you collect as evidence of learning, from the artifact alone to the process that produced it. A teacher who understands this stops asking "did AI write this" and starts asking "can this student account for the thinking," which is both a fairer and a more answerable question.

## Learning Targets

- Distinguish product evidence from process evidence and explain why generative AI weakened product evidence.
- Distinguish artifact polish from evidence-based revision.
- Apply an allowed-use boundary to judge whether a student did the reserved work, rather than whether AI touched the document.
- Critique AI-detector scores as probabilistic, equity-biased hypotheses rather than integrity evidence.

## Opening Case

A composition teacher runs every submission through an AI detector. One Monday it flags three essays as "likely AI." She is about to file integrity reports when she looks at whose essays they are: all three are from her multilingual students, the ones who write in careful, formulaic sentences because they learned English structure before English idiom. None of her native-speaking students were flagged, even the two she suspects copied from a chatbot but who write loosely enough to read "human."

The detector did not catch cheaters. It caught a writing style, and that style correlates with being an English learner. If she had acted on the scores, she would have punished her most vulnerable students for the crime of writing carefully while letting fluent native speakers slide. The detector did not give her evidence; it gave her a bias dressed as a number. The real question is what evidence would actually let her judge each case fairly, and the answer is not on the detector's dashboard.

## Core Concept

The central shift in this section is from **product evidence** to **process evidence**. Product evidence is the finished artifact: the essay, the lab report, the code. Generative AI has made product evidence weakly diagnostic, because a fluent product no longer guarantees the learning behind it. Process evidence is the trail that shows thinking happening over time: drafts, outlines, version history, in-class writing, the student's ability to explain and extend their own work in conversation. AI can fake a product in seconds; it is far harder for a student to fake a four-day process they can also account for out loud.

Two ideas make this concrete and keep teachers out of trouble.

First, **detectors are probabilistic classifiers, not evidence.** An AI detector outputs a likelihood, not a fact, and it produces that likelihood by recognizing statistical patterns in text. Crucially, those patterns are not "wrongness"; they are predictability. Writing that is simple, formulaic, or structurally regular reads as more "AI-like," which is exactly how many multilingual students, younger writers, and students taught to follow templates write. The well-documented result is an elevated false-positive rate for these groups. A detector score is therefore a hypothesis with a known equity bias, never a finding, and it cannot be the basis of a consequence.

**Recent evidence.** This is not hypothetical. Liang et al. (2023) tested seven widely used GPT detectors and found they misclassified 61.3% of TOEFL essays by non-native English writers as AI-generated, while classifying native-speaker essays almost perfectly as human; simply prompting to raise linguistic complexity removed the AI flag. OpenAI retired its own AI Text Classifier in July 2023 for a "low rate of accuracy" (it caught only about 26% of AI-generated text), which underscores that even the model vendor could not make detection reliable.

Second, **integrity is defined by the assignment's allowed-use boundary, not by the presence of AI.** "Did the student use AI" is the wrong question, because the answer is often "yes, in a way you permitted." The right question is "did the student do the work the assignment reserved for them." That requires you to have stated, in advance, which steps are theirs (the brainstorming, the drafting, the revision, the final reasoning) and which a tool may assist. Without that boundary, you have no standard to judge against, and any accusation is arbitrary.

## Worked Example

Take a flat case and apply the framework. Two students submit nearly identical, fluent essays on the same topic. The detector scores both around 60%. A product-only reading treats them the same: both look polished, both look "kind of AI."

Now collect process evidence. Student A's document version history shows a three-day arc: a messy outline, two abandoned thesis statements, tracked revisions, and comments to herself. In a thirty-second hallway check she can explain why she dropped her second thesis. Student B's document was created in one eleven-minute session, pasted in whole, with no revision history, and in conversation he cannot say what his second body paragraph argues. Same product, same detector score, opposite process evidence. The integrity judgment is now obvious and defensible, and it never rested on the detector. The lesson generalizes: when you design assignments so process evidence accumulates (in-class drafting, version history, brief oral accounts), the AI-detection problem mostly dissolves, because faking the product no longer fakes the trail.

## Worked Decision: Responding to a Detector Flag

Follow a teacher's annotated before/after judgment on a suspected-AI essay. The student, Mateo, is a multilingual writer; the detector reports "82% likely AI."

1. **Before (initial impulse):** "The detector says 82%, so Mateo used AI. I will give a zero and report it." The only evidence is the percentage.
2. **Constraint named:** Annotate the tool's limits. Detectors are probabilistic and show elevated false-positive rates for multilingual and formulaic writers. An 82% score is a guess, not a finding.
3. **Process evidence gathered:** Annotate what the student work shows. Mateo's planning doc lists three AI-suggested topics, which the assignment explicitly allowed. His draft history shows five revisions over four days, and his outline matches his final argument.
4. **After (revised decision):** "I will not penalize for AI use. The allowed step was brainstorming and his draft history shows his own revision work. I will keep a short oral check in my next conference to confirm he can reconstruct the argument, and I will stop treating the detector score as a trigger for consequences."
5. **Evidence trail:** The revision is justified by the assignment's allowed-use boundary and by process artifacts, not by a detector number. The rejected move, acting on the percentage, is recorded with its reason.

Step 1 to step 4 shows the section's core lesson: integrity judgments rest on process evidence and the assignment's defined boundary, not on a probabilistic score or on prose polish.

**Hidden cost of the process-evidence move.** Submitting student work to a commercial detector or generative-AI service is itself a data-disclosure decision: the essays are education records (FERPA), under-13 contexts add COPPA, whose amended rule was finalized in 2025 with compliance through 2026, and version-history-as-evidence is a form of process surveillance whose burden falls unevenly on students with less private time, hardware, or connectivity. Choose process evidence with the same datafication lens you point at the detector.

## Decision Rule

When a piece of student work raises an AI-integrity concern, never let a detector score drive a consequence. Instead:

1. **Treat the detector as a hypothesis, not evidence**, and remember its bias against formulaic and multilingual writers.
2. **Check the allowed-use boundary you set in advance.** If the assignment permitted the step in question (brainstorming, outlining), there is no violation to investigate.
3. **Gather process evidence**: version history, drafts, in-class work, and a brief oral account where the student explains and extends their own reasoning.
4. **Judge whether the student did the reserved work**, not whether AI touched the document.

Act only when the process evidence, not the percentage, supports the conclusion. The burden of evidence rises with the severity of the consequence.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is "the detector said 90%, so it is proven," which mistakes a probability with a known bias for a fact. The second is the reverse overcorrection: assuming any AI involvement is cheating, which punishes students who used AI exactly as you allowed and pushes everyone else to hide their use. The third is believing the solution is better detection software; detection is an arms race teachers lose, while assignment design that generates process evidence is a position you can hold. The deepest misreading is treating this as a discipline problem at all. It is an assessment-design problem: the question is what evidence of learning you collect, and AI simply exposed that the old proxy (a clean product) was always thin.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Walk the framework through a realistic redesign. A biology teacher's lab reports started coming back suspiciously uniform and fluent. Her first instinct was a detector; the framework points her elsewhere.

She starts from the learning the report is supposed to evidence: interpreting their own data and connecting it to the mechanism. A chatbot can write a generic report on photosynthesis, but it cannot interpret the specific, slightly-off numbers from this group's failed trial. So she redesigns the assignment to anchor it in process and local evidence: students submit their raw data table and a photo of the setup, write the interpretation section in a timed in-class block, and answer one targeted question ("why did your group's trial diverge from the prediction") that only their actual experience can answer.

Now the allowed-use boundary is explicit: AI may help polish grammar and check vocabulary, but the interpretation must be the student's, produced in class, about their own data. Integrity is no longer a detector verdict; it is visible in whether the interpretation matches the group's real results. A student who outsourced the report would write a fluent but generic interpretation that does not fit the divergent data, and the mismatch, not a percentage, is the evidence. She solved an integrity problem by changing what counted as the work.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

A strong reader of this topic refuses to evaluate student work, or AI policy, on the product alone. They ask what process evidence exists and whether the assignment was designed to generate any. As you read, keep a two-column note with the **claim about a student's AI use** on the left and the **process evidence that would confirm or refute it** on the right. If the only thing in the right column is a detector score, the case is not yet decidable.

A strong analysis can answer four questions about any suspected-AI case:

1. What was the assignment's allowed-use boundary, stated in advance?
2. What process evidence exists, and what does it actually show?
3. Does the detector score, if any, carry a known bias against this student's group?
4. What assignment redesign would make this judgment easy next time without detection software?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

Your tension map is strong when it names the real conflict in this topic (efficient detection versus fair evidence) and resolves it with a process-evidence design rather than a policing tool. Show the allowed-use boundary you would set, the process evidence you would collect, and the equity reason you reject detector-driven consequences. The weakest maps say "use AI detectors carefully"; the strongest say "I will not act on detector scores because of their bias against multilingual writers, and instead I will design assignments so process evidence makes the judgment fair."

A useful closing line for the artifact would read: "I rejected detector scores as a trigger because they false-flag my formulaic and multilingual writers; I redesigned the task so version history and a thirty-second oral account, which are far harder to fake, carry the integrity judgment instead."

## Transfer Case

Move the problem to a math classroom, where "writing" is not the work. A student submits a perfect, fully worked problem set. There is no prose for a detector to score, and the answers are simply right or wrong. The product-versus-process distinction still governs: the process evidence becomes the student's ability to solve a structurally similar problem on a whiteboard, or to explain why a step is valid. The specific evidence changes from version history to live problem-solving, but the principle is identical: a correct product no longer proves the learning, so you collect evidence of the thinking instead. Getting to the right evidence in a setting with no text to detect is the test that you understand the principle, not just the essay-classroom version of it.
