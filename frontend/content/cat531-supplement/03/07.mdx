# AI Use Disclosure

![AI Use Disclosure textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/03-07-ai-use-disclosure.png)

## Why This Section Matters

Schools have rushed to require that students disclose their AI use, while teachers quietly use AI to write feedback, report-card comments, and recommendation letters without disclosing anything. That asymmetry is not a small inconsistency; it is a credibility problem that students notice instantly. A teacher who demands transparency she does not practice teaches students that disclosure is a rule for the powerless, not an ethical norm. Getting disclosure right, for yourself first, is therefore foundational to having any standing to ask it of students.

This section matters because disclosure is widely misunderstood as either a confession or a liability shield, when it is neither. Good disclosure does two specific jobs: it gives the reader the information they need to judge what they are reading, and it keeps accountability where it belongs. The most common failures are a one-word label that tells the reader nothing ("AI-assisted"), and the dangerous assumption that disclosing AI use transfers responsibility for accuracy to the tool. A teacher who understands disclosure can be honest about AI in a high-trust document without diluting the trust, because the disclosure informs the reader and the teacher still owns every word.

## Opening Case

A teacher uses a chatbot to draft report-card comments from her gradebook notes, edits them, and sends them home. A parent later asks her in person about a specific phrase praising her son's "growing leadership," and the teacher cannot recall the incident it refers to, because the AI invented a plausible-sounding detail she did not catch. The parent's trust takes a hit, and not only because the detail was wrong: she realizes the warm, personal-sounding comment was partly machine-written, which she was never told.

Now reverse it. A different teacher discloses at the top of the comments: "I drafted these from my own gradebook notes with AI assistance, then edited and verified each comment against my records." A parent reading that knows exactly what they are looking at and can calibrate accordingly, and when every specific claim turns out to be accurate, the disclosure increases trust rather than eroding it. Same tool, opposite outcomes. The difference is whether the disclosure was present, specific, and backed by the teacher actually owning the accuracy.

## Core Concept

Effective AI disclosure rests on four properties, and most bad disclosures fail one of them.

- **Reciprocity.** Transparency cannot be a one-way demand. If you require students to disclose AI use, professional integrity requires you to disclose your own in comparable situations. The standard you set for the less powerful party you must meet yourself; otherwise the rule is about authority, not ethics.
- **Specificity.** "AI-assisted" is nearly content-free, because it does not tell the reader what AI did versus what you did. Useful disclosure names the **division of labor**: what the tool generated, what you contributed, and what you verified. The reader needs to know whether AI drafted everything and you skimmed it, or you wrote it and AI checked grammar; those are very different things they cannot distinguish from a single label.
- **Accountability stays with the human.** This is the property people get backward. Disclosure informs the reader; it does not transfer responsibility. Saying "this was AI-assisted" does not make the tool answerable for an error; you are still fully accountable for every claim you distribute under your name. Disclosure is an act of honesty, not a disclaimer that offloads blame.
- **Psychological safety.** Disclosure norms only produce honesty if honesty is safe. If admitting AI use is automatically punished, people hide it, and you get less transparency, not more. A workable norm distinguishes permitted, disclosed use from prohibited use, so that disclosing allowed assistance never feels like a confession.

The organizing idea: **disclosure is for the reader, accountability is for the writer.** A disclosure that is vague fails the reader; a disclosure that is treated as a liability shield fails the writer's responsibility. Good disclosure does both jobs at once, telling the reader precisely what they are reading while leaving every error squarely on the human who sent it.

## Worked Example

Compare two disclosures on the same AI-assisted college recommendation letter, a high-stakes document.

Weak: "This letter was created with AI assistance." A reader cannot tell whether the teacher knows this student at all or whether a chatbot invented the praise. Worse, the phrasing reads as a hedge, as if to say "if anything is off, blame the tool." It fails specificity and quietly attempts to shed accountability.

Strong: "I wrote this letter from my own observations of [student] over two years; I used AI to tighten the prose, and I verified that every accomplishment and quality described reflects work I personally witnessed." This tells the reader exactly what the AI did (polished wording) and what it did not (supply the substance), so they can trust the content while knowing the language was assisted. It is reciprocal with what the teacher asks of students, it is specific about the division of labor, and it explicitly keeps accountability with the teacher ("I verified... I witnessed"). The strong version uses the same tool but produces trust instead of suspicion, because it serves the reader and owns the result. If the teacher had instead let AI invent achievements, no disclosure could rescue it, because the problem would no longer be transparency but fabrication.

## Worked Decision: Writing an Honest Disclosure Statement

Follow a teacher's annotated before/after judgment while writing a disclosure note for AI-assisted report-card comments families will read.

1. **Before (no disclosure, one-sided rule):** "I will use the chatbot to draft all comments and send them as my own. Students must disclose their AI use, but mine is private." The reciprocity of transparency is missing.
2. **Reciprocity named:** Annotate the asymmetry. If AI substantially shaped what families read about their child, honesty requires the teacher to disclose her use, just as she requires of students.
3. **Specificity edit:** Annotate the vague label. A first draft note reads only "Some comments were AI-assisted." She rewrites it to name the division of labor: "I used AI to draft a first version from my gradebook notes, then I edited every comment for accuracy and added each child's specific examples myself."
4. **After (accountability kept):** "I disclose my AI use and I verified every fact against my own records. If a comment is wrong, that is on me, not the tool. I rejected three AI-generated comments that did not match what I observed." Disclosure informs the reader; it does not move the responsibility.
5. **Evidence trail:** The decision is justified by reciprocal transparency and by **teacher professional judgment** (verify, then own the result). The rejected comments and the discarded vague label are recorded with reasons.

Step 1 to step 4 captures the section's lesson: disclosure is reciprocal, specific about who did what, framed so honesty is safe, and never a transfer of accountability for accuracy.

## Decision Rule

Before sending AI-assisted work, test the disclosure against its four properties:

1. **Reciprocity.** Are you disclosing what you would require a student to disclose in the same situation?
2. **Specificity.** Does the disclosure name the division of labor (what AI did, what you did, what you verified), not just slap on an "AI-assisted" label?
3. **Accountability.** Is it clear that you, not the tool, are responsible for every claim? Disclosure informs; it never transfers blame.
4. **Safety.** Does your classroom norm make disclosing permitted use safe, so honesty is rewarded rather than punished?

Disclose when AI materially shaped what the reader receives, be specific about who did what, and own the result regardless of what the tool produced.

Match each disclosure property to the failure it prevents. Holding the four apart is what lets you diagnose why a given disclosure is weak.

## Common Misreadings

The most damaging misreading treats disclosure as a liability shield, as if "AI-assisted" excuses an error; it does not, because accountability for distributed work is always the human's. A second is the vague label, which technically discloses while telling the reader nothing useful about the division of labor. A third is the double standard: requiring student disclosure while hiding your own, which destroys the norm's credibility. A fourth is building a punitive norm where any admitted AI use is penalized, which simply drives use underground and yields less honesty. Finally, some treat disclosure as the whole ethical question, when disclosure cannot fix a deeper problem like fabricated content; transparency about a fabrication is still a fabrication.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the four properties to a realistic case. A teacher uses AI to help write individualized feedback on twenty-eight lab reports and must decide whether and how to disclose to students.

Reciprocity first: her syllabus requires students to note AI assistance on their work, so consistency requires her to disclose hers; skipping it would teach the double standard directly. Specificity next: a label like "feedback was AI-assisted" would leave students unsure whether a machine judged their work or a teacher did. She writes instead, "I drafted phrasing for some feedback with AI, but every score and every specific comment about your data is my own judgment based on reading your report." That names the division of labor precisely.

Accountability: she does not let the disclosure soften her responsibility. If a comment misreads a student's data, that is her error to correct, not the tool's, and she frames it that way. Safety: she pairs the disclosure with a clear message that her use was a permitted, deliberate choice, modeling that disclosed, allowed AI use is normal and not a confession, which makes students more willing to be honest about their own. The disclosure ends up strengthening the integrity culture she is trying to build, precisely because it is reciprocal, specific, accountable, and safe.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

A strong reader of this topic separates the two jobs a disclosure does, informing the reader and locating accountability, and checks that a given disclosure does both. As you read, keep a two-column note with the **disclosure as written** on the left and the **property it satisfies or violates** on the right (reciprocity, specificity, accountability, safety). A disclosure that satisfies all four is rare on a first draft.

A strong analysis can answer four questions about any disclosure:

1. Would I require a student to disclose this same use, and am I holding myself to that?
2. Does the wording tell the reader what AI did versus what I did?
3. Is it unmistakable that I remain accountable for accuracy?
4. Does my classroom norm make honest disclosure of permitted use safe?

## Transfer Case

Move disclosure from a teacher's own work to designing the disclosure expectation you set for students. The four properties invert into design requirements: the norm you write must be reciprocal (you model it), specific (students know what level of detail to give), accountability-preserving (disclosing AI use does not excuse a student from owning their submitted work), and safe (disclosed, permitted use is not punished). Building a student disclosure policy that embodies the same four properties you apply to yourself, rather than a one-line "you must report AI use," is the test that you understand disclosure as an ethical structure rather than a label.
