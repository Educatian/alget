# Evaluation Questions Before Ratings

![Evaluation Questions Before Ratings textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/05-01-evaluation-questions-before-ratings.png)

## Why This Section Matters

Star ratings, download counts, and "teacher favorite" badges are answers to questions that someone else asked. A 4.7-star average tells you that a large, self-selected crowd liked something, but it does not tell you what they liked, who they were, or whether their classroom looked anything like yours. When a teacher leads with the rating, the rating silently sets the question, and the question it sets is usually "is this popular?" rather than "does this serve my students?" This section trains the reverse order: write the evaluation question first, then let it decide which numbers count.

Putting the question before the rating is what turns a purchase into a defensible professional judgment. A teacher who can name the instructional purpose, the population the tool must serve, and the boundary it must not cross has built an instrument that filters evidence. A teacher who starts from the rating has only inherited someone else's filter.

## Learning Targets

- Write a context-bound evaluation question that names the learner, the instructional purpose, and a non-negotiable boundary before consulting any rating.
- Explain why an aggregate rating measures appeal across an unknown population rather than fit for a specific classroom.
- Re-score a tool against your own question and document where a high external rating becomes irrelevant.
- Record a before/after trace that shows the question driving the decision, including a trade-off you accepted on purpose.

## Opening Case

A fourth-grade team needs a fluency-practice app for a class of 24 that includes 6 multilingual newcomers. One teacher opens the app store, sees 4.7 stars across 1,200 reviews, and is ready to adopt. The reviews, read closely, come overwhelmingly from parents praising the rewards animations. None mention multilingual access, and none come from a classroom resembling this one.

The decision worth recording here is not "we picked the 4.7-star app." It is whether the team can state the question that the 4.7 actually answered (general appeal), distinguish it from the question they need answered (newcomer access without exposing names to a third party), and show why the two diverge.

## Core Concept

An evaluation question is a sentence that fixes three things before any evidence is gathered: the **purpose** (what learning the tool must support), the **population** (the specific students who must be served), and the **boundary** (the constraint the tool may not violate, often a privacy or access rule). Once those three are fixed, the question behaves like a measuring instrument: it tells you which evidence is relevant and which is noise.

A rating is the opposite kind of object. An aggregate rating is a single number compressed from many raters whose purposes, populations, and boundaries are unknown and probably mixed. Compressing all that variation into one star value discards exactly the information a teacher needs. The number is real, but it answers a question you did not ask.

The gap between the two can be made explicit:

| | Evaluation question | Aggregate rating |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who set the criterion | You, for your class | An unknown, self-selected crowd |
| What it measures | Fit to a stated purpose and boundary | Average appeal across mixed contexts |
| Handles your constraints | Yes, by design | Only by accident |
| Inspectable later | Yes, the question is on the record | No, the raters' reasons are hidden |

Three course anchors keep the question honest. **[TPACK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_pedagogical_content_knowledge)** forces the purpose clause to name a real learning demand, not a vibe ("students practice decoding multisyllabic words," not "students engage"). **Critical edtech studies** presses on the population and boundary clauses, asking whose access the tool quietly assumes. **[AI and education datafication](https://doi.org/10.1080/17439884.2020.1686017)** asks what the tool does with student data once the activity is over, which often becomes the boundary clause itself.

## Worked Example

Start with a thin claim that many students write on a first pass:

> I made the equity-oriented edtech evaluation matrix with context-specific risk notes clearer.

That claim is too thin because "clearer" does not identify the audience, the problem, or the evidence. A stronger claim would read:

> I revised the equity-oriented edtech evaluation matrix with context-specific risk notes so that the intended audience can see the decision, the constraint, and the evidence source. The revision is justified by **critical edtech studies**, and I can point to the exact part of the artifact that changed.

The stronger version treats the matrix as evidence: it names what changed, ties the change to the boundary it protects, and leaves a reader able to audit the decision.

### Worked Example: Writing the Question Before the Rating

Scenario: a fourth-grade team must choose a fluency-practice app for 6 multilingual newcomers in a class of 24.

1. **Drafted rating first (rejected approach).** A teacher opens the app store, sees a 4.7-star average across 1,200 reviews, and writes "4.7, adopt it." There is no record of what the 4.7 measures.
2. **Wrote the evaluation question instead.** The team rewrote the entry as a question: "Does this tool let a newcomer attempt a passage with first-language audio support, without exposing names to a third party?" That question names the decision, the 6 learners, and one privacy constraint.
3. **Re-scored against the question.** Tool A: strong on engagement, 0 of 6 newcomers could start a passage without English text on screen, so it scores low on the actual question. Tool B: plainer interface, offers Spanish and Arabic audio glossing, scores high on the question.
4. **Annotated judgment call.** Before/after: the before-note read "Tool A, 4.7 stars, looks fun." The after-note read "Tool B selected. Tool A's 4.7 answered a different question (general appeal); our question was newcomer access, which Tool A fails. We accepted a lower engagement score to protect the access constraint."

The decision is now defensible because a reviewer can see the question, see why the high rating did not apply, and see which trade-off was accepted on purpose.

**Popularity outruns evidence.** Instructure/LearnPlatform's EdTech Evidence Report found that only about a third of 2024's forty most-used edtech tools had research meeting any of ESSA's four evidence tiers, and consumer technologies used in classrooms almost never do, so a tool's market position is measurably not a proxy for demonstrated learning impact.

## Decision Rule

Use the classroom value conflict first, the technology affordance second, and the published rating third. A tool choice is justified only when it answers your stated question, not the crowd's.

Before you let any number into the matrix, check that your evaluation question already exists in writing:

1. **Purpose check:** Does the question name the specific learning the tool must support, in language a colleague could verify?
2. **Population check:** Does it name the actual students at stake, especially the ones most likely to be left out?
3. **Boundary check:** Does it name the line (privacy, access, cost) the tool may not cross, so that a high score cannot buy its way past the boundary?

**Why the boundary is legal, not just preferential.** Under FERPA, disclosing personally identifiable information from a student's education records to a third party generally requires prior written consent (34 CFR 99.30), and the school-official exception (34 CFR 99.31) lets a vendor receive that information only for the contracted purpose, with re-disclosure barred without consent, so "sending names off-site" is a consent-bound act, not a preference. For students under 13, the FTC's amended COPPA Rule (effective June 23, 2025, with full compliance required by April 22, 2026) requires verifiable parental consent before a tool discloses children's data to third parties or uses it for targeted advertising, and bars retaining that data longer than reasonably necessary.

If a rating cannot be mapped onto your purpose, population, and boundary, it is appeal data, not fit data, and it belongs in a footnote rather than the decision line.

## Common Misreadings

The most common error is treating a high rating as a high fit. Appeal and fit are different measurements, and a tool can be loved by thousands while failing the one access constraint your class cannot waive. A second error is writing the question after seeing the rating, which lets the number quietly shape the question it is supposed to be tested against.

A defensible entry shows the question on the record before the rating, so a colleague can see that the evidence was filtered by your criterion and not the other way around.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Walk a single tool through the order this section teaches. Begin by drafting the evaluation question on its own, with no app open: "Does this tool let my 6 newcomers attempt grade-level text with first-language support, without sending names off-site?" Notice that the sentence already contains purpose, population, and boundary, and that it could be checked by someone who has never met you.

Only now open the listing and read the rating as one more piece of evidence to be sorted by the question. The 4.7 stars get mapped against the three clauses: does any review speak to newcomer access (population)? to audio support (purpose)? to data handling (boundary)? If the answer is no on all three, the rating is appeal evidence about a different question, and you log it as such rather than as support for adoption.

Finally, record the re-score. The artifact should show the tool's high external number, your question, and the line where the two part ways. That parting line, "the 4.7 answered general appeal; our question was access," is the move that converts a popularity figure into a professional judgment.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: **the question** on the left, **the number it tempts you to trust** on the right. For every rating you encounter, write the question it actually answers, then write your own. The exercise makes visible how often a published number answers "is this popular?" while the classroom needs "is this usable by these students under this rule?"

A strong reader of this section can answer four questions:

1. What question did this rating actually answer, and for whom?
2. What is my classroom's question, in purpose-population-boundary form?
3. Where do the two questions diverge enough that the rating stops being evidence?
4. What trade-off did I accept on purpose when I followed my question instead of the crowd's?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

For this section, the matrix is strong when the evaluation question sits at the top of every row, above any score, so a mentor can see that the numbers were collected to answer your question rather than to flatter the tool. Treat the rating column as the least authoritative column. If a tool's only strength is its external rating, the matrix should say so plainly and let the question decide.

A useful closing line for the artifact would sound like this: "I changed the matrix so the evaluation question (newcomer access without third-party exposure) appears before any rating. I kept out the suggestion to lead with the 4.7-star average because that number answered general appeal, a different question from the one my class needs answered."

## Transfer Case

Move the same tool to a high school AP class with no newcomers and reliable one-to-one devices. The purpose clause may survive ("students practice fluent reading of dense text"), but the population and boundary clauses change completely, and so does which rating becomes relevant. A tool that failed the fourth-grade question on newcomer access might pass here, and a glowing fourth-grade rating still would not transfer, because it answered a question about a different room. Use that shift to confirm you can rebuild the question for a new context rather than carrying over an old verdict.
