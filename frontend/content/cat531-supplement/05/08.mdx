# Module 5 Evaluation Studio

![Module 5 Evaluation Studio textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/05-08-module-5-evaluation-studio.png)

## Why This Section Matters

The studio is where the seven moves of this module stop being separate skills and become one brief. Writing the question before the rating, separating evidence from vendor claims, splitting usability from accessibility from equity, costing the tool honestly, gating the fit matrix, conditioning the recommendation, and surviving peer review are not a checklist to staple together. They have to converge on a single decision, and the hard part is that they often point in different directions. A brief that stacks the seven sections side by side is not a brief; it is a binder. This section teaches synthesis: resolving the tensions between sections into one coherent, defensible recommendation.

The capstone matters because a real adoption decision is exactly this composite. The cost section may favor one tool while the equity section forbids it, and the brief's job is not to hide that conflict behind a tidy total but to name it and resolve it in the open. A reader should be able to see the trade-off you made and the evidence that would reopen it.

## Learning Targets

- Assemble the seven module sections into a single brief whose recommendation traces back to the opening question.
- Surface a tension between sections (for example cost versus equity) instead of letting one section quietly win.
- Resolve the tension explicitly, stating which value gates which, and why.
- State the evidence that would reopen the decision, so the brief is revisable rather than final.

## Opening Case

In the studio you assemble a full brief for a video-annotation tool, pulling together the question, the evidence-versus-vendor-claims read, the usability and accessibility checks, the cost ledger, the fit matrix, and the recommendation. Each section reads fine on its own. But the cost section favored Tool A on price, while the equity section flagged that Tool A requires a personal device, excluding 5 students who rely on shared school devices, and the draft recommendation simply says "adopt Tool A," having quietly followed cost.

The decision worth recording is not which tool the brief names. It is whether the brief surfaces the cost-versus-equity conflict it contains and resolves it on purpose, rather than letting the cheapest option drift to the top unexamined.

## Core Concept

Synthesis is the act of making the sections argue with each other and then settling the argument in writing. Three skills define it:

- **Trace the thread.** Every section should connect back to the opening evaluation question. If the recommendation answers a different question than the one the brief opened with, the thread is broken and the synthesis has failed.
- **Surface the tension.** When two sections point opposite ways, the worst outcome is silence: the brief follows one and never mentions the other. Name the conflict in a sentence: "cost favors Tool A; equity forbids it."
- **Resolve with a gate.** Decide which value governs and say why. A participation barrier that excludes 5 students is not a cost the class can trade away, so the equity finding gates the cost preference. The resolution is a judgment, and the brief defends it rather than burying it.

A coherent brief also states its **reopening condition**: the future fact that would change the decision. "If the school provisions one-to-one devices, Tool A becomes viable and we re-evaluate." This keeps the recommendation honest as a current best judgment rather than a permanent verdict, and it tells a reader exactly what to watch for.

The three course anchors operate at the level of the whole brief here. **[TPACK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_pedagogical_content_knowledge)** checks that the assembled recommendation still serves the learning the question named, not a feature that crept in along the way. **Critical edtech studies** is usually the source of the tension that synthesis must resolve, since equity and access conflicts rarely sit quietly. **[AI and education datafication](https://doi.org/10.1080/17439884.2020.1686017)** can introduce a late gate, a data-handling fact from one section that overrides preferences built up across the others.

## Worked Example

A first draft often hides the real decision behind a vague improvement claim:

> I made the equity-oriented edtech evaluation matrix with context-specific risk notes clearer.

That claim is too thin because "clearer" does not identify the audience, the problem, or the evidence. A stronger claim would read:

> I revised the equity-oriented edtech evaluation matrix with context-specific risk notes so that the intended audience can see the decision, the constraint, and the evidence source. The revision is justified by **critical edtech studies**, and I can point to the exact part of the artifact that changed.

Notice the shift: the artifact is no longer just polished. The revision names the evidence source and preserves the reason a suggestion was accepted or rejected.

### Worked Example: Assembling One Coherent Brief

Scenario: in the studio you assemble a full brief for a video-annotation tool, pulling the six earlier sections together.

1. **Stacked the parts (before).** The draft lists six sections side by side: question, evidence vs vendor claims, usability and accessibility, cost, fit matrix, recommendation. Each reads fine alone, but the recommendation simply says "Adopt," with no thread back to the question.
2. **Found the internal tension.** The cost section favored Tool A on price. The equity section flagged that Tool A requires a personal device, excluding 5 students who rely on shared school devices. The two sections pointed in opposite directions, and the draft had quietly followed cost.
3. **Synthesized the tension instead of hiding it.** The revision states the conflict in one line, then resolves it: device-dependent exclusion is a participation barrier the class cannot accept, so the equity finding gates the cost preference.
4. **Annotated judgment call (after).** The closing reads: "Recommendation: adopt Tool B (slightly higher cost) for one term. This answers our original question (equitable annotation access) and resolves the cost-vs-equity tension in favor of access. What would change this: if the school provisions 1:1 devices, Tool A becomes viable and we would re-evaluate. We rejected hiding the cost-equity conflict because a reviewer must see the trade-off we made."

The capstone is defensible because the parts now converge on one decision, the trade-off is explicit, and the brief states what evidence would reopen it.

## Decision Rule

Use the opening question as the thread, the cross-section tensions as the real work, and the final recommendation as the place those tensions are resolved in the open. A capstone brief is defensible only when its recommendation traces to its question and its trade-offs are named rather than hidden.

Before you submit the assembled brief, run three checks across the whole thing:

1. **Thread check:** Does the recommendation answer the question the brief opened with, in the same terms?
2. **Tension check:** Where do two sections point opposite ways, and has the brief named that conflict instead of silently following one?
3. **Reopen check:** What future fact would change the decision, and is it stated?

If the brief reads as seven correct sections with no conflict and no resolution, it has probably hidden a tension rather than resolved one, and the synthesis is incomplete.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is treating assembly as synthesis: pasting seven sound sections together does not produce a coherent brief if they never confront each other. The second is letting one section win by default, usually cost, because it carries a clean number while equity carries a harder-to-quantify burden. The third is presenting the recommendation as final, with no statement of what would reopen it, which makes a current judgment masquerade as a permanent truth.

A defensible capstone names its internal tension, resolves it with a stated gate, threads the recommendation back to the opening question, and declares its reopening condition.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Lay the six sections of the video-annotation brief in front of you and resist closing it yet. Read the recommendation first and ask which section it followed. It says "adopt Tool A," and tracing back, that matches the cost section and nothing else; the equity section, which flagged that Tool A excludes 5 shared-device students, was never reconciled. The brief followed the cleanest number and left the conflict unspoken.

Make the conflict speak. Write it in one line: cost favors Tool A, equity forbids it because device dependence excludes 5 students from the core annotation activity. Now decide which value governs and defend the choice. A participation barrier that locks 5 students out of the assessed task is not a price the class can pay, so the equity finding gates the cost preference, and the recommendation flips to Tool B at a slightly higher cost.

Close by threading back and forward. Confirm that adopting Tool B answers the opening question, equitable annotation access, in the same words the brief began with. Then state the reopening condition: if the school provisions one-to-one devices, the device barrier disappears and Tool A returns to contention. A reviewer can now see one coherent decision, the trade-off behind it, and the exact fact that would revise it.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read an assembled brief, keep a map rather than a list: draw an arrow from the opening question to the recommendation and mark every place two sections pull in opposite directions. The quality of the synthesis lives at those marks. A brief with no marked tensions has either an unusually clean tool or, far more often, a conflict it declined to name.

A strong reader of a capstone brief can answer four questions:

1. Does the recommendation trace cleanly back to the opening question?
2. Where do the sections conflict, and is each conflict named in the brief?
3. Which value gates which in the resolution, and is the choice defended?
4. What future fact would reopen the decision, and is it stated?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

For this section, the brief is strong when the recommendation threads to the opening question, every cross-section tension is named and resolved with a stated gate, and a reopening condition is declared, so a mentor can audit not just each section but the seams between them. Treat a brief with no visible tension as a brief that hid one.

A useful closing line would sound like this: "I changed the brief so the cost-versus-equity tension is stated and resolved in favor of access, with Tool B recommended for one term and a reopening condition (one-to-one devices) named. I kept out the suggestion to present the sections as seven independent verdicts because it would have hidden the trade-off the decision actually turned on."

## Transfer Case

Carry the synthesis stance beyond this module to any composite professional decision: a curriculum adoption, a grading-policy change, a schedule redesign. Each is built from sub-analyses that can conflict, and each is well made only when the conflicts are surfaced and resolved rather than averaged or ignored. The video-annotation brief is a rehearsal for the larger decisions where the cost-versus-equity pattern recurs under different names. Use the transfer to confirm that you can assemble many partial findings into one defensible recommendation with its tensions named, in any setting where the parts disagree.
