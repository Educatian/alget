# Usability, Accessibility, and Equity

![Usability, Accessibility, and Equity textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/05-03-usability-accessibility-and-equity.png)

## Why This Section Matters

"Easy to use" is the praise teachers reach for first, and it is the one that hides the most. A tool can be effortless for the typical student in the front row and impassable for the student using a screen reader, and a separate set of students can find it perfectly usable and perfectly accessible yet still be locked out by a home-internet requirement. Usability, accessibility, and equity are three different questions, and collapsing them into one word is how access problems pass an evaluation unnoticed. This section keeps them apart on purpose.

The stakes are not abstract. When the three checks collapse into "looks fine," the students who fail the unasked checks are usually the students the system already underserves: disabled students, multilingual newcomers, students without reliable devices or connectivity. Pulling the three apart is what makes an evaluation an equity instrument rather than a comfort report.

## Learning Targets

- Define usability, accessibility, and equity as three distinct checks with three distinct populations.
- Run each check as its own test rather than inferring all three from a single smooth experience.
- Recognize when a compliance badge (for example WCAG AA) does not match the tool's core task.
- Document a decision that names which check failed, for whom, and what condition must be met before adoption.

## Opening Case

A sixth-grade team evaluates a science-simulation app for a class that includes 2 students who use screen readers and several students without reliable home internet. A teacher runs the app, finds it quick and intuitive, and writes "very easy, students click through fast, adopt." The note is true and almost entirely beside the point for the students who matter most here.

The decision worth recording is not "the app is easy." It is whether the team checked the same tasks with a screen reader (accessibility) and against the connectivity and home-practice demands (equity), and what they found when they did.

## Core Concept

The three checks answer three separate questions about three populations:

- **Usability** asks whether a typical user can accomplish the task efficiently and without confusion. Its population is the median student.
- **Accessibility** asks whether students using assistive technology or with disabilities can accomplish the same core task. Its population is disabled students, and the relevant standard is the core activity, not the menus.
- **Equity** asks whether the tool imposes burdens unrelated to ability that fall unevenly across students, such as device ownership, home connectivity, cost, or language. Its population is whoever the tool's hidden requirements exclude.

| Check | Question | Population | Common failure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Usability | Can a typical user do the task easily? | Median student | Confusing flow |
| Accessibility | Can an assistive-tech user do the core task? | Disabled students | Unlabeled controls on the main activity |
| Equity | Does the tool burden some students unfairly? | The excluded | Home-internet or device dependency |

Two traps recur. The first is the **badge mismatch**: a tool advertises WCAG AA, but the badge covers its navigation while the core learning activity (a drag-and-drop lab) has no spoken labels. Compliance on the shell is not compliance on the task. The second is **collapsing equity into accessibility**: a home-internet requirement is not an accessibility flaw, it is an equity flaw, and treating them as one hides it.

**Recent Evidence.** The current standard is WCAG 2.2, a W3C Recommendation since October 2023, so an evaluation should name which version and level a badge actually claims rather than citing generic "WCAG AA." And automated audit tools detect only roughly 30 percent of accessibility issues; the WebAIM Million project, which reports widespread home-page failures every year, counts only automatically detectable barriers and notes that true conformance is lower than its reported figures. A scanner result of "no issues found" is therefore not a clearance, and the screen-reader or keyboard walkthrough of the core task remains the decisive test.

Three course anchors guide the checks. **[TPACK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_pedagogical_content_knowledge)** keeps the accessibility test focused on the core learning task rather than the login screen. **Critical edtech studies** names the equity burdens vendors externalize onto families (devices, data plans, nightly practice). **[AI and education datafication](https://doi.org/10.1080/17439884.2020.1686017)** flags tools whose "adaptivity" degrades for students with less data history, an equity failure dressed as personalization.

**The legal floor.** For public schools the accessibility check is a compliance obligation, not just good practice: the 2024 Department of Justice rule under ADA Title II adopts WCAG 2.1 AA as the technical standard for the web content and mobile apps of state and local government entities, explicitly including public K-12 schools, community colleges, and public universities. As of 2026, the compliance deadlines have been extended to April 26, 2027 for entities serving 50,000 or more people and April 26, 2028 for smaller entities and special districts, so a tool that fails the core-task accessibility check can put a district out of compliance, which gives the badge-mismatch trap real teeth.

Before running the checks on a real tool, fix the three apart in your mind. Match each check to the population it is actually about and the direct test that exposes its failure. The point is to stop "easy to use" from absorbing the other two.

### Worked Example: Usable, Accessible, and Equitable Are Three Checks

Scenario: a sixth-grade team evaluates a science-simulation app for a class that includes 2 students who use screen readers and several students without reliable home internet.

1. **Usability check (before).** A teacher tries the app and notes "very easy, students click through fast." The early annotation reads "looks fine, adopt."
2. **Accessibility check.** Running the same tasks with a screen reader, the team finds the drag-and-drop lab steps give no spoken labels, so the 2 screen-reader users cannot complete the core activity. The vendor's "WCAG AA" badge did not match the task.
3. **Equity check.** The app also auto-saves only when online and assigns nightly home practice. Students without home internet would lose work, a burden unrelated to accessibility.
4. **Annotated judgment call (after).** The revised entry reads: "Usable for typical users: yes. Accessible for screen-reader users on the core lab: no (drag steps unlabeled). Equity risk: home-internet dependency for nightly saves. Decision: request the vendor's keyboard-accessible lab mode and move practice to in-class time before adopting. We rejected 'easy to use' as sufficient because it answered only the first of three checks."

The brief is defensible because each of the three checks is named separately and the trade-off is explicit rather than collapsed into one word.

## Decision Rule

Run usability, accessibility, and equity as three checks in that order, and never let a pass on one stand in for the others. A tool is adoptable only when all three pass for the actual students in the room, or when a named condition makes them pass.

When the tool feels smooth, run the three checks deliberately:

1. **Usability check:** Can the median student complete the task without confusion?
2. **Accessibility check:** Can your assistive-technology users complete the same core task, tested directly, not inferred from a badge?
3. **Equity check:** What does the tool require (a device, home internet, money, English) that some of your students cannot supply?

A failure on any check does not end the evaluation; it converts into a named condition ("request keyboard-accessible lab mode," "move practice to class time") that must be met before adoption.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is reading a single smooth trial as evidence for all three checks; ease for the median student says nothing about the screen-reader user or the student without internet. The second is trusting a compliance badge without confirming it covers the core task rather than the shell. The third is filing an equity burden, such as required home connectivity, under accessibility, where it quietly disappears.

A defensible matrix records the three checks on three lines, names the population each one is about, and converts each failure into a condition rather than a shrug.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Take the science app through all three lanes. Run the lab once as a sighted mouse user: smooth, so usability passes for the median student, and you log that narrowly. Now run the identical lab with a screen reader; the drag steps announce nothing, so accessibility fails on the core task, and the WCAG AA badge, which covered navigation, does not rescue it. You record the failure at the level of the activity, not the menu.

Then leave the interface and look at what the tool assumes off-screen. It auto-saves only online and assigns nightly home practice, so a student without home internet loses work through no fault of ability. That is an equity failure, filed on its own line, not folded into the accessibility note.

Close by turning both failures into conditions. The brief does not say "reject" or "adopt"; it says "adopt only if the vendor enables a keyboard-accessible lab mode and we move practice to in-class time." A reviewer can now see exactly which students were at risk, on which check, and what would clear the path.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a three-row note rather than two columns: one row each for usability, accessibility, and equity, and in each row name the population and the specific test you would run. The exercise forces the checks apart and stops "easy to use" from absorbing the other two.

A strong reader of this section can answer four questions:

1. For each check, which population is it actually about?
2. What direct test (not a badge, not a vibe) would I run for accessibility on the core task?
3. What off-screen requirement might exclude some students even when the interface is fine?
4. What condition would convert each failure into a defensible adoption?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

For this section, the matrix is strong when usability, accessibility, and equity each occupy their own row, each names its population, and each failure carries a condition, so a mentor can see no check was inferred from another. Treat a clean usability impression as the weakest evidence in the matrix, because it speaks only for the students least at risk.

A useful closing line would sound like this: "I changed the matrix so accessibility and equity are scored separately from usability, each with its population named. I kept out the suggestion to record one overall ease score because it would have hidden the screen-reader failure on the core lab and the home-internet equity risk."

## Transfer Case

Move the same app to a one-to-one high school with school-issued devices, campus wifi, and no screen-reader users this term. The equity burden (home internet) and the accessibility failure (no screen-reader user affected today) recede, and usability may now carry more of the decision. But the checks themselves do not retire: a transfer student who relies on assistive tech could enroll next week, and the accessibility defect is still real even when no current student trips on it. Use the shift to confirm that the three checks are properties of the tool and the room, recomputed for each setting, not a verdict you carry forward.
