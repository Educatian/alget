## Core Concept

The core idea in this section is that **Usability, Accessibility, and Equity** should be treated as a relationship among purpose, constraint, evidence, and revision. Purpose names what the learner or audience needs to accomplish. Constraint names what limits the decision. Evidence names the source that makes the decision defensible. Revision shows what changed after the evidence was considered.

Three course anchors shape the reasoning here:

- **TPACK** helps you decide what counts as a meaningful learning or performance demand.
- **critical edtech studies** helps you notice when a design or technology choice creates risk, burden, or unsupported assumptions.
- **AI and education datafication** helps you check whether the artifact works for varied learners, audiences, or use contexts.

Read the anchors as competing checks on quality. One may highlight the learning demand, another may expose a risk, and another may help you test transfer.

### Worked Example: Usable, Accessible, and Equitable Are Three Checks

Scenario: a sixth-grade team evaluates a science-simulation app for a class that includes 2 students who use screen readers and several students without reliable home internet.

1. **Usability check (before).** A teacher tries the app and notes "very easy, students click through fast." The early annotation reads "looks fine, adopt."
2. **Accessibility check.** Running the same tasks with a screen reader, the team finds the drag-and-drop lab steps give no spoken labels, so the 2 screen-reader users cannot complete the core activity. The vendor's "WCAG AA" badge did not match the task.
3. **Equity check.** The app also auto-saves only when online and assigns nightly home practice. Students without home internet would lose work, a burden unrelated to accessibility.
4. **Annotated judgment call (after).** The revised entry reads: "Usable for typical users: yes. Accessible for screen-reader users on the core lab: no (drag steps unlabeled). Equity risk: home-internet dependency for nightly saves. Decision: request the vendor's keyboard-accessible lab mode and move practice to in-class time before adopting. We rejected 'easy to use' as sufficient because it answered only the first of three checks."

The brief is defensible because each of the three checks is named separately and the trade-off is explicit rather than collapsed into one word.

## Decision Rule

Use the classroom value conflict first, the technology affordance second, and the policy boundary third. A tool choice is justified only when it protects students while improving a specific learning activity.

A strong submission should survive three quick tests:

1. **Purpose check:** What should the audience be able to do after reading or using the artifact?
2. **Evidence check:** Which exact source, observation, annotation, rubric line, data pattern, or policy boundary justifies the revision?
3. **Revision check:** What changed in the artifact, and what suggestion did you reject or modify?

If you cannot answer all three questions, the artifact may be complete, but it is not yet ready as evidence of learning.
