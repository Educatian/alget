# Classroom Fit Matrix

![Classroom Fit Matrix textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/05-05-classroom-fit-matrix.png)

## Why This Section Matters

A fit matrix scores tools against weighted criteria and totals the points, which looks objective and often is not. The danger is structural: when a non-negotiable requirement is entered as one scored row among many, a tool can fail that requirement completely and still win on total points, because strengths elsewhere paper over the gap. A rural classroom that loses internet three times a week does not need a tool that scores high on average; it needs a tool that works offline, full stop. This section teaches the difference between a criterion you average and a constraint you gate, because confusing the two lets deal-breakers disappear into a sum.

The matrix is a reasoning tool, not an oracle. Its weights, its rows, and its choice of what to gate are all judgments the teacher makes and must defend. Building the matrix well is how a teacher turns "it felt like the better tool" into a decision a skeptic can audit row by row.

## Learning Targets

- Distinguish a scored criterion (averaged into a total) from a gating constraint (pass or fail before scoring).
- Recognize when summing a must-have into a total lets a low score be offset by unrelated strengths.
- Reclassify a non-negotiable requirement as a hard gate and remove failing tools before they are scored.
- Document a fit decision in which a high total was overruled by a failed gate, with the reasoning shown.

## Opening Case

A rural classroom with unreliable internet builds a fit matrix for two collaboration tools, weighting usability, features, accessibility, and offline support. Tool A scores high on usability and features and posts the higher weighted total; Tool B is plainer but reliable offline. The team is ready to pick Tool A "because it scored higher," even though the building loses connectivity several times a week.

The decision worth recording is not "Tool A won the total." It is whether the team recognized that offline support is a gate, not a row, and that a tool which cannot deliver the lesson when the wifi drops should never have been scored against a tool that can.

## Core Concept

A fit matrix has two kinds of rows, and treating them the same is the central error:

- A **scored criterion** is a quality where more is better and trade-offs are legitimate. Usability, feature depth, and visual design belong here; a weak score on one can be reasonably offset by a strong score on another, because none is essential by itself.
- A **gating constraint** is a requirement the tool must meet to function in your context at all. Offline capability in a low-connectivity building, screen-reader access for a student who needs it, a signed data agreement before student accounts exist: these are pass or fail. A tool that fails the gate cannot be rescued by points elsewhere, because the points buy nothing if the lesson cannot run.

The mechanism of the trap is **compensatory scoring**. When a must-have is entered as a 1-to-5 row, a score of 1 on that row can be outweighed by 5s elsewhere, and the total reports a winner that fails the one thing that mattered. The fix is **non-compensatory gating**: move the must-have out of the scored rows, apply it as a filter, and remove any tool that fails before computing any totals. (The compensatory versus non-compensatory distinction is standard multi-criteria decision analysis, not a course invention; see Cinelli et al., 2020, for the formal taxonomy.)

A short procedure makes it operational:

1. Separate rows into gates and scored criteria.
2. Apply every gate first; eliminate tools that fail any gate.
3. Score the survivors on the remaining criteria and total the points.
4. Record which tools were gated out and why, so the total is read only among eligible tools.

The explorer shows the trap and the fix on one screen. Score Tool A on usability, features, and accessibility, then set its offline-support score and a gate flag. The compensatory total averages all four rows, so strong rows hide a weak must-have. The gated total drops to zero the moment offline support falls below the pass line you set, because a tool that fails the gate buys nothing with points elsewhere. (The gate term multiplies the total by 1 when offline meets the threshold and by 0 when it does not.)

Three course anchors discipline the matrix. **[TPACK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_pedagogical_content_knowledge)** decides which criteria reflect real learning value versus surface features, so weights are not arbitrary. **Critical edtech studies** (Selwyn, 2016) helps identify which requirements are non-negotiable for your students rather than for the median classroom. **[AI and education datafication](https://doi.org/10.1080/17439884.2020.1686017)** often supplies a gate of its own: a privacy or data-handling line a high feature score cannot purchase past, typically a signed data-privacy agreement consistent with FERPA (20 U.S.C. 1232g) and, for under-13 students, the amended COPPA Rule, before any student accounts exist.

## Worked Example

A first draft often hides the real decision behind a vague improvement claim:

> I made the evaluation matrix clearer.

That claim is too thin because "clearer" does not identify the audience, the problem, or the evidence. A stronger claim would read:

> I revised the edtech evaluation matrix so the must-have rows are gates applied before any scoring: the first pass had averaged offline support into the total, which let Tool A's polish buy back a deal-breaker, so the revision moves offline (and privacy) above the scored rows and records the eliminated tool. The revision is justified by **critical edtech studies**, and I can point to the exact rows that changed.

Notice the shift: the artifact is no longer just polished. The revision names the evidence source and preserves the reason a suggestion was accepted or rejected.

### Worked Example: When a Total Hides the Deal-Breaker

Scenario: a rural classroom with unreliable internet builds a fit matrix for two collaboration tools, weighting four criteria.

1. **First pass (before).** The team scores each tool 1 to 5 and weights the rows: usability 40 percent, features 35 percent, accessibility 20 percent, offline support 5 percent. Tool A: usability 5, features 5, accessibility 4, offline support 1, weighted total 92 percent (40 + 35 + 16 + 1). Tool B: usability 4, features 4, accessibility 4, offline support 5, weighted total 81 percent (32 + 28 + 16 + 5). Tool B's raw total (17 of 20) actually beats Tool A's (15 of 20), but the weights hand Tool A the win. The note reads "A wins, higher percent."
2. **Found the gating constraint.** Offline support is not a nice-to-have here; the building loses internet several times a week. A tool scoring 1 on offline support cannot deliver the lesson at all.
3. **Reweighted as a gate.** The team moved offline support from a scored row to a pass/fail gate. Tool A fails the gate and is removed before scoring. Tool B passes, so it is the only candidate.
4. **Annotated judgment call (after).** The revised matrix records: "Offline support reclassified from a 1-to-5 criterion to a hard gate because of building connectivity. Tool A's 92 percent is irrelevant once it fails the gate. We rejected averaging a must-have into the total because it let a deal-breaker disappear."

The matrix is defensible because the non-negotiable constraint now controls the decision instead of being diluted by points it could never offset.

## Decision Rule

Use the gating constraints first, the scored criteria second, and the total last. A tool is eligible for scoring only after it passes every gate; a high total among ineligible tools is not a finding.

Before you compute any total, audit the matrix structure:

1. **Gate check:** Which rows are requirements the tool must meet to function here at all? Pull them out of the scored rows.
2. **Elimination check:** Does any tool fail a gate? Remove it before scoring, and record why.
3. **Total check:** Among the survivors, does the weighted total reflect criteria where trade-offs are genuinely acceptable?

If a single low score on one row would be a deal-breaker in your classroom, that row is a gate, not a criterion, and leaving it in the sum is the error to fix.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is trusting the total because it looks numeric and therefore objective; a sum is only as sound as the decision to make every row compensatory. The second is entering a must-have as a weighted row and assuming a heavy weight protects it, when in fact any weight can be outscored if the row is averaged rather than gated. The third is reporting a winner without recording which tools were eliminated by gates, leaving a reader unable to see that the total was computed among eligible tools only.

A defensible matrix shows the gates above the scored rows, lists the tools each gate removed, and computes totals only for the survivors.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Build the rural-classroom matrix the wrong way first so the trap is visible. With offline support as a scored 1-to-5 row, Tool A's strong usability and features carry it to the higher weighted total even though it scores a 1 on the row that decides whether the lesson can run at all. The sum is arithmetically correct and practically false.

Now find the gate. Offline support in a building that drops connectivity several times a week is not a quality to be traded off; it is the condition of the tool working. Pull it out of the scored rows and apply it as pass or fail. Tool A fails and leaves the comparison before any total is computed; its 92 percent never enters the decision because it was never eligible.

Score only the survivor and record the structure plainly: "Offline support reclassified from a scored row to a gate because of building connectivity; Tool A eliminated at the gate; Tool B scored among survivors." A reviewer can now see that the decision turned on the requirement that actually governs the classroom, not on points the requirement could never have offset.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-list note: **gates** on one side, **scored criteria** on the other. For every row a tempting matrix would average, ask whether a failing score on it would actually end the decision in your room. If it would, the row belongs on the gates list, and the exercise reveals how often a deal-breaker has been quietly filed as a tradeable criterion.

A strong reader of this section can answer four questions:

1. Which rows are gates and which are scored criteria, and why?
2. Where could a high total hide a failed must-have through compensatory scoring?
3. Which tools should be eliminated before scoring, and what is the recorded reason?
4. Is the reported total computed only among tools that passed every gate?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

For this section, the matrix is strong when gates are listed and applied above the scored rows, eliminated tools are recorded with reasons, and the weighted total appears only for survivors, so a mentor can confirm no deal-breaker was averaged away. Treat a high total for a tool that failed a gate as a red flag, not a recommendation.

A useful closing line would sound like this: "I changed the matrix so offline support is a pass/fail gate, not a scored row, which eliminates Tool A before scoring. I kept out the suggestion to keep it as a weighted criterion because any weight still let Tool A's other strengths offset a deal-breaker."

## Transfer Case

Move the same two tools to a one-to-one suburban school with rock-solid wifi. Offline support is no longer a gate; the building never loses connectivity, so a tool that fails offline loses nothing operational. Offline support drops back to a scored criterion, or off the matrix entirely, and Tool A's higher total may now be a legitimate winner. The gates list is context-specific by design: a requirement that gates one classroom is a mere preference in another. Use the shift to confirm that you can re-sort gates and criteria for a new setting rather than carrying the rural verdict into a connected one.
