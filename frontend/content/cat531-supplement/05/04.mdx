# Cost and Sustainability

![Cost and Sustainability textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/05-04-cost-and-sustainability.png)

## Why This Section Matters

The sticker price is the smallest number in an edtech decision and the only one most evaluations record. "Free for educators" hides a per-seat tier that triggers at student forty-one; a \$1,200 license hides thirty hours of unpaid teacher setup; a generous trial hides a free tier that can vanish next budget cycle along with three years of student work. Cost is not the line on the invoice; it is the total a school actually pays over the time it actually uses the tool, in money and in teacher hours. Sustainability asks the next question: can the school keep paying that total, and can it get its data back if it stops?

Teachers carry the hidden costs personally, in evenings spent configuring a platform no one budgeted to maintain. Naming the full cost of ownership and the sustainability cliff is how an evaluation respects both the budget and the people who run the tool.

## Learning Targets

- Build a total cost of ownership across a realistic time window, including license tiers, training, and teacher time.
- Translate "free" into its true price by finding the seat cap, the paid tier, and the labor it assumes.
- Identify a sustainability cliff: a free tier that can be discontinued, a price that can rise, or an export gap that traps student data.
- Document a decision in which the cheaper sticker price loses to the more sustainable total.

## Opening Case

A department compares two platforms for 120 students over three years. Tool A lists at \$1,200 per year with vendor-led training included. Tool B advertises "free for educators," and the early instinct is "pick B, it is free." But B's free tier caps at 40 students, so 120 students require the paid tier, and B offers no contractual guarantee that the free option, or the export of student work, will exist next year.

The decision worth recording is not "we picked the free one." It is whether the department built the three-year total for both tools, including the teacher hours and the data-export risk, and let that total decide.

## Core Concept

**Total cost of ownership (TCO)** is the sum of every cost a tool imposes across the window you will use it, not the first-year license alone. For school technology it has three layers that the sticker price omits:

- **Tiered licensing.** "Free" and per-seat prices change at thresholds. A free tier capped at 40 seats costs nothing until your 41st student, then jumps to the paid tier for the whole roster.
- **Training and setup.** Vendor-led training may be bundled (a real saving) or absent, in which case the teacher absorbs the setup as unpaid labor. Thirty hours per teacher per year is a cost even when no invoice names it.
- **Teacher time over the window.** Setup recurs; multiply per-year hours by the number of years and teachers.

**Sustainability** is the separate question of whether the total can be sustained and exited:

- **Price stability:** can the vendor raise the price or end the free tier?
- **Data portability:** does the contract guarantee export of student work, including history, if you leave?

A tool with a low sticker price but a discontinuable free tier and no data export sits on a **sustainability cliff**: cheap until the day it strands your students' work.

Three course anchors keep the accounting honest. **[TPACK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_pedagogical_content_knowledge)** asks whether the cost buys learning value or only features. **Critical edtech studies** names the labor and lock-in vendors externalize onto schools and teachers. **[AI and education datafication](https://doi.org/10.1080/17439884.2020.1686017)** flags "free" tools whose real price is student data monetized elsewhere, the cost that never appears in dollars.

## Worked Example

Start with a thin claim that many students write on a first pass:

> I made the Design Tension Studio map naming a classroom value conflict and negotiated decision clearer.

That claim is too thin because "clearer" does not identify the audience, the problem, or the evidence. A stronger claim would read:

> I revised the Design Tension Studio map naming a classroom value conflict and negotiated decision so that the intended audience can see the decision, the constraint, and the evidence source. The revision is justified by **critical edtech studies**, and I can point to the exact part of the artifact that changed.

The stronger version treats the artifact as evidence: it names what changed and leaves a reader able to audit the decision.

### Worked Example: From Sticker Price to Cost of Ownership

Scenario: a department compares two platforms for 120 students over a three-year window.

1. **Sticker comparison (before).** Tool A lists at 1,200 dollars per year; Tool B advertises "free for educators." Early note: "Pick B, it is free."
2. **Built the cost of ownership.** Tool A, three years: 3 x 1,200 = 3,600 dollars, with vendor-led training included. Tool B "free" tier caps at 40 students, so covering 120 students requires the paid tier at 900 dollars per year (2,700 over three years), plus about 30 hours of teacher setup each year that no one is paid extra to do.
3. **Named the sustainability cliff.** Tool B's free tier could be discontinued; its export option does not include student work history. Tool A guarantees data export in its contract.
4. **Annotated judgment call (after).** The revised brief reads: "On dollars alone the gap is small (3,600 vs 2,700). Once 90 hours of unpaid teacher setup and the data-export gap are counted, Tool A is the more sustainable choice for our staffing. We rejected the 'free' framing of Tool B because it hid the per-seat tier and the teacher-time cost."

The decision is defensible because a reviewer can see every cost line, including the human time that the word free concealed.

## Decision Rule

Use the learning value first, the total cost of ownership second, and the sustainability terms third. A tool is affordable only when the school can pay the full multi-year total and recover its data on exit.

When a price looks attractive, run it through three tests before it enters the matrix:

1. **Tier check:** What threshold (seats, features, storage) moves this tool off its advertised price, and where does your roster sit relative to it?
2. **Labor check:** How many teacher hours, per year and across the window, does setup and maintenance require, and is anyone paid for them?
3. **Exit check:** Can the vendor raise the price or end the tier, and does the contract guarantee export of student work history if you leave?

A tool that fails the exit check is not automatically rejected, but its sticker price cannot be trusted, and the brief must record the cliff.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is equating "free" or "cheapest" with "lowest cost." Free names the invoice, not the total, and it routinely hides a seat cap or unpaid labor. The second is omitting teacher time because it has no line item; uncounted hours are still a cost, and they fall on the people least able to refuse them. The third is ignoring data portability until the day a school tries to leave and finds its students' work locked in.

A defensible brief shows the full multi-year total for each tool, names the teacher hours explicitly, and records the exit terms beside the price.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Put both tools on a three-year ledger. Tool A is arithmetic: 3 x \$1,200 = \$3,600, training included, export guaranteed. Tool B looks cheaper until you apply the tier check: 120 students exceed the 40-seat free cap, so the real price is the paid tier at \$900 per year, \$2,700 over three years. The dollar gap has already shrunk to \$900.

Now apply the labor check, the column the sticker price never shows. Tool B assumes about 30 hours of teacher setup each year, 90 hours across the window, paid by no one. Whether or not you convert those hours to dollars, they are a real cost that tilts the comparison, and the brief must name them rather than let them stay invisible.

Finally apply the exit check. Tool B's free tier can be discontinued and its export omits student work history; Tool A guarantees export by contract. On dollars alone the tools are close; once labor and the data cliff are counted, the more sustainable choice is the one with the higher sticker price. Recording that reversal, with every line shown, is the discipline of cost evaluation.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a ledger note rather than a single price: one row for license across the window, one for training, one for teacher hours, and one for exit terms. The point is to make every hidden cost a visible line so the comparison cannot be won by the smallest, loudest number.

A strong reader of this section can answer four questions:

1. What is the true multi-year total, after tiers and thresholds?
2. How many teacher hours does the tool cost, and who pays for them?
3. What event (price rise, tier end, export gap) could strand the school or its data?
4. When does the cheaper sticker price lose to the more sustainable total, and can I show the lines that prove it?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

For this section, the brief is strong when cost appears as a multi-year ledger with separate lines for license, training, teacher time, and exit terms, so a mentor can see no cost was hidden inside the word free. Treat a single sticker number as the least trustworthy figure in the brief.

A useful closing line would sound like this: "I changed the brief so cost is shown as a three-year ledger including 90 hours of teacher setup and the data-export gap. I kept out the suggestion to recommend Tool B as 'the free option' because that framing hid the per-seat tier, the labor, and the sustainability cliff."

## Transfer Case

Move the same comparison to a one-teacher elementary classroom of 22 students. Now Tool B's 40-seat free tier actually covers the roster, so the licensing line genuinely is free, and the labor line shrinks because one teacher configures one section. The tool that lost at department scale may win here. But the exit check does not change: the free tier can still be discontinued and the export still omits history, so the sustainability cliff transfers even when the price does not. Use the shift to confirm that cost is recomputed per context while sustainability terms travel with the contract.
