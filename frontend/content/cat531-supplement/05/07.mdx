## Core Concept

The core idea in this section is that **Peer Review of Evaluation Briefs** should be treated as a relationship among purpose, constraint, evidence, and revision. Purpose names what the learner or audience needs to accomplish. Constraint names what limits the decision. Evidence names the source that makes the decision defensible. Revision shows what changed after the evidence was considered.

Three course anchors shape the reasoning here:

- **TPACK** helps you decide what counts as a meaningful learning or performance demand.
- **critical edtech studies** helps you notice when a design or technology choice creates risk, burden, or unsupported assumptions.
- **AI and education datafication** helps you check whether the artifact works for varied learners, audiences, or use contexts.

Use these anchors as lenses, not as names to drop into a reflection. A useful lens changes what you notice, what you question, and what you revise.

### Worked Example: Reviewing the Reasoning, Not the Polish

Scenario: you are assigned to peer review a colleague's evaluation brief for a discussion-board tool.

1. **Weak review draft (before).** Your first comments read: "Looks polished. I would have picked a different tool. Maybe change the heading font." None of these test whether the brief's conclusion holds.
2. **Read the brief against its own question.** The brief's question is "Does the tool support equitable participation for quiet students?" The conclusion is "Adopt." But the evidence section reports only overall posting volume, with nothing about who is posting.
3. **Located the gap.** The conclusion claims equity of participation; the evidence measures total volume, which can rise while the same few students dominate. The conclusion outruns the evidence.
4. **Annotated judgment call (after).** Your revised review reads: "Strong question and clear writing. The gap is in the evidence-to-conclusion link: 'supports equitable participation' is not shown by total post counts. Suggest adding a distribution of posts per student before keeping the adopt conclusion. I removed my font and tool-preference comments because they do not test your reasoning."

The review is defensible because every comment ties back to whether the author's own evidence supports the author's own conclusion.

## Decision Rule

Use the classroom value conflict first, the technology affordance second, and the policy boundary third. A tool choice is justified only when it protects students while improving a specific learning activity.

Before submitting the equity-oriented edtech evaluation matrix with context-specific risk notes, slow down and check whether the trace can answer three questions:

1. **Purpose check:** What should the audience be able to do after reading or using the artifact?
2. **Evidence check:** Which exact source, observation, annotation, rubric line, data pattern, or policy boundary justifies the revision?
3. **Revision check:** What changed in the artifact, and what suggestion did you reject or modify?

If you cannot answer all three questions, the artifact may be complete, but it is not yet ready as evidence of learning.

## Common Misreadings

A common mistake is to defend a technology because it is engaging. Engagement matters, but it does not replace evidence about learning, access, or risk.

The artifact should show how student need, evidence, and policy boundaries shaped the revision.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Read this section as a small professional decision cycle, not as a list of terms. First, locate the work product: **equity-oriented edtech evaluation matrix with context-specific risk notes**. Then ask what problem the artifact is supposed to solve inside edtech evaluation. A strong reader can explain the artifact in plain language before naming any theory: who will use it, what decision it supports, and what would count as evidence that it improved.

Next, connect the artifact to the course anchors: **TPACK**, **critical edtech studies**, **teacher professional judgment**. The point is not to cite every anchor. The point is to let one anchor change the artifact. If the anchor does not change wording, layout, evidence, timing, or feedback, it has not yet become design reasoning.

Finally, inspect the revision trail. A weak submission says the artifact was improved. A strong submission shows the earlier version, the issue noticed, the support requested, the decision accepted or rejected, and the reason the final version is better for the intended learner or audience.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: **claim** on the left, **evidence** on the right. The claim is the decision you think the section is teaching. The evidence is the exact phrase, example, annotation, support move, or artifact revision that makes the claim inspectable. By the end of CAT 531 05.07, your notes should make it possible to reconstruct your reasoning without asking you to explain it again.

A strong reader of Peer Review of Evaluation Briefs can answer four questions:

1. What is the real decision hidden inside this topic?
2. Which constraint makes the decision difficult?
3. What evidence would persuade a skeptical reviewer?
4. What would you revise if the audience, learner, classroom, or tool changed?
