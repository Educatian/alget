# Peer Review of Evaluation Briefs

![Peer Review of Evaluation Briefs textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/05-07-peer-review-of-evaluation-briefs.png)

## Why This Section Matters

Reviewing a colleague's evaluation brief is a different skill from writing your own, and most teachers default to the wrong one. The instinct is to react: comment on the font, say which tool you would have picked, note that the writing is clean. None of that tests whether the brief's conclusion is actually earned by the brief's own evidence. Useful peer review does one thing above all else: it checks the link between what the author claimed and what the author showed, and it leaves preference and polish at the door. This section trains review as evidence-to-conclusion auditing rather than reaction.

This matters because a brief that survives weak review carries a hidden flaw into a real adoption decision. If a reviewer praises a polished brief whose conclusion outruns its data, the polish becomes a liability: it makes an unsupported recommendation look trustworthy. The reviewer's job is to be the reader who refuses to be reassured by surface and insists the reasoning hold.

## Learning Targets

- Distinguish a review that tests the evidence-to-conclusion link from one that reacts to style or personal preference.
- Read a brief against its own stated question and locate where the conclusion outruns the evidence.
- Write comments that name the specific gap and propose the evidence that would close it.
- Remove preference and polish comments that do not test the author's reasoning.

## Opening Case

You are assigned to peer review a colleague's evaluation brief for a discussion-board tool. Your first reaction is to write "looks polished, I would have picked a different tool, maybe change the heading font." Then you notice the brief's stated question is whether the tool supports equitable participation for quiet students, its conclusion is "adopt," and its evidence section reports only total posting volume, with nothing about who is posting.

The decision worth recording is not your impression of the brief. It is whether you can see that "supports equitable participation" is not demonstrated by a rise in total posts, since the same few students could be driving the increase, and whether your comments test that gap rather than your own taste.

## Core Concept

Every evaluation brief makes an implicit syllogism: here is my question, here is my evidence, therefore here is my conclusion. Peer review audits the joints of that syllogism, especially the **evidence-to-conclusion link**, the place where a conclusion can quietly claim more than the evidence shows.

The most common failure is a **construct mismatch**: the conclusion is about one thing (equitable participation) and the evidence measures another (total volume). Total posting volume can rise while participation becomes less equitable, because a few prolific students can produce the entire increase. The evidence is real and the conclusion is plausible, but the evidence does not measure the thing the conclusion claims. A reviewer's central move is to ask, for each conclusion, does the evidence offered actually measure this claim?

Three review behaviors separate testing from reacting:

- **Test the link, not the topic.** You may have chosen a different tool; that is irrelevant unless the author's evidence fails to support the author's conclusion.
- **Name the gap precisely.** "Weak evidence" is not actionable; "total posts cannot show equitable participation, you need a per-student distribution" is.
- **Propose the missing evidence.** A good review tells the author what would close the gap, turning criticism into a revision path.

Three course anchors keep review substantive. **[TPACK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_pedagogical_content_knowledge)** helps you check whether the brief's evidence even measures a learning construct or just an activity count. **Critical edtech studies** (Selwyn, 2016; Perrotta & Selwyn, 2019) sharpens the equity reads, where conclusions most often outrun aggregate numbers. **[AI and education datafication](https://doi.org/10.1080/17439884.2020.1686017)** prompts you to question metrics the tool itself reports, since a vendor dashboard may count what is easy rather than what matters.

## Worked Example

Start with a thin claim that many students write on a first pass:

> I made the equity-oriented edtech evaluation matrix with context-specific risk notes clearer.

That claim is too thin because "clearer" does not identify the audience, the problem, or the evidence. A stronger claim would read:

> I revised the equity-oriented edtech evaluation matrix with context-specific risk notes so that the intended audience can see the decision, the constraint, and the evidence source. The revision is justified by **critical edtech studies** (Selwyn, 2016), and I can point to the exact part of the artifact that changed.

The stronger version treats the matrix as evidence: it names what changed and leaves a reader able to audit the decision.

### Worked Example: Reviewing the Reasoning, Not the Polish

Scenario: you are assigned to peer review a colleague's evaluation brief for a discussion-board tool.

1. **Weak review draft (before).** Your first comments read: "Looks polished. I would have picked a different tool. Maybe change the heading font." None of these test whether the brief's conclusion holds.
2. **Read the brief against its own question.** The brief's question is "Does the tool support equitable participation for quiet students?" The conclusion is "Adopt." But the evidence section reports only overall posting volume, with nothing about who is posting.
3. **Located the gap.** The conclusion claims equity of participation; the evidence measures total volume, which can rise while the same few students dominate. The conclusion outruns the evidence.
4. **Annotated judgment call (after).** Your revised review reads: "Strong question and clear writing. The gap is in the evidence-to-conclusion link: 'supports equitable participation' is not shown by total post counts. Suggest adding a distribution of posts per student before keeping the adopt conclusion. I removed my font and tool-preference comments because they do not test your reasoning."

The review is defensible because every comment ties back to whether the author's own evidence supports the author's own conclusion.

## Decision Rule

Use the brief's own question first, the evidence-to-conclusion link second, and your stylistic reactions last (usually not at all). A review comment is useful only when it tests whether the author's evidence supports the author's conclusion.

For each conclusion in a brief, run three checks:

1. **Question check:** What did the brief set out to determine, and does the conclusion answer that question?
2. **Link check:** Does the evidence offered actually measure the construct the conclusion claims, or a proxy that can move independently of it?
3. **Closure check:** If there is a gap, what specific evidence would close it, and can you name it for the author?

A comment that survives these checks belongs in the review; a comment about font, layout, or your own tool preference does not, unless it changes whether the reasoning holds.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is mistaking polish for soundness; clean writing can carry an unearned conclusion, and praising the polish reinforces the flaw. The second is reviewing the tool instead of the brief, substituting your choice for an audit of the author's argument. The third is flagging "weak evidence" without naming the gap or the fix, which leaves the author no path to revise.

A defensible review ties every comment to the author's own question and evidence, and proposes the specific data that would close any gap it identifies.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Take the discussion-board brief and review it twice. The first pass is the reflex pass: polished, different tool preferred, font note. Read those three comments back and ask what each one tests about the brief's reasoning. None of them do; they describe your taste and your hypothetical choice, and the author could accept all three and still ship an unsupported conclusion.

The second pass starts from the brief's own question: equitable participation for quiet students. Now read the evidence section against that question. It reports total posting volume, which is a count of activity, not a measure of who participates. The conclusion "adopt, supports equitable participation" rests on evidence that cannot distinguish a class where everyone posts more from a class where three students post much more.

Write the review that names this exactly: praise the clear question and writing, then state the link failure and the fix, "a per-student distribution of posts would show whether participation is equitable; the current total cannot." Remove the font and preference comments, and say you removed them. The review is now an audit of the author's reasoning, which is the only thing peer review is for.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read a brief, keep a three-column note: the author's question, the evidence offered, and the conclusion drawn. The review writes itself from the gaps between the columns, especially where the conclusion's construct does not match the evidence's measure. If a comment of yours does not point to one of those gaps, it is probably reaction, not review.

A strong reviewer of an evaluation brief can answer four questions:

1. What question did the brief set out to answer?
2. Does the evidence offered measure the construct the conclusion claims?
3. Where exactly does the conclusion outrun the evidence?
4. What specific evidence would close the gap, and did I tell the author?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

For this section, the review is strong when every comment ties to the brief's own question and evidence, names each evidence-to-conclusion gap precisely, and proposes the data that would close it, so a mentor can see the review tested reasoning rather than taste. Treat a comment about polish or personal tool preference as noise to be cut unless it changes whether the conclusion holds.

A useful closing line would sound like this: "I changed my review to focus on the link between the brief's evidence (total post volume) and its conclusion (equitable participation), and I proposed a per-student distribution as the missing measure. I removed my font and tool-preference comments because they did not test the author's reasoning."

## Transfer Case

Move from reviewing a peer's brief to reviewing your own before submission, reading it as a hostile reviewer would. The same three columns apply: your question, your evidence, your conclusion. The discipline is harder on your own work because you know what you meant, so you must check whether the page actually shows it. A self-review that catches your own construct mismatch (you concluded "improves learning" but measured time-on-task) is worth more than any external comment. Use the shift to confirm that evidence-to-conclusion auditing is a stance you can take toward any brief, including the one you wrote.
