# Adoption Recommendation

![Adoption Recommendation textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/05-06-adoption-recommendation.png)

## Why This Section Matters

A recommendation is where an evaluation becomes a commitment, and the temptation is to flatten it into one word: adopt or reject. Real evidence is rarely that clean. A tool can show strong learning gains while its data agreement sits unsigned; the learning evidence argues for yes and the privacy evidence argues for not yet, and a bare verdict in either direction discards half of what the evaluation found. This section teaches the conditional recommendation: a statement that names scope, attaches conditions, and declares what would reverse it, so the decision matches the evidence instead of overrunning it.

A conditional recommendation also protects the people downstream. An administrator who reads "adopt" launches a district rollout; an administrator who reads "pilot grades 6 to 8 for one semester, conditional on a signed agreement by August 15, withdrawn if unmet" knows exactly what was authorized and what was not. Precision in the recommendation is how a teacher keeps a decision honest after it leaves their hands.

## Learning Targets

- Write a recommendation that matches the strength and the gaps of the evidence rather than rounding to adopt or reject.
- Specify scope (who and what), step (pilot versus rollout), and condition (what must be true first).
- Attach a reversal trigger that states what evidence or deadline would withdraw the recommendation.
- Document why an unconditional adopt and a flat reject were both rejected in favor of a conditional path.

## Opening Case

A committee evaluated an adaptive-math platform and found solid learning gains in a four-week trial, but the data-processing agreement is unsigned. The draft recommendation reads, in full, "We recommend adopting the platform." One sentence, no scope, no conditions, no mention of the unsigned agreement that could expose student data.

The decision worth recording is not the verdict itself. It is whether the committee can write a recommendation that honors the strong learning evidence and the unresolved privacy gap at the same time, instead of letting one side of the evidence vanish.

## Core Concept

A defensible recommendation has four parts, and a bare "adopt" has only a fraction of one:

- **Scope** names exactly what is recommended and for whom: "grades 6 to 8 math," not "the platform." Scope prevents a narrow finding from authorizing a broad rollout.
- **Step** names the size of the commitment: a one-semester pilot is a different act from a district-wide adoption, and the evidence usually supports the smaller step first.
- **Condition** names what must be true before the step proceeds: "a signed data-processing agreement must be in place before any student account is created." A condition converts an unresolved gap into a gate rather than a silence.
- **Reversal trigger** names what would withdraw the recommendation: "if the agreement is not signed by August 15, the recommendation lapses and the pilot does not start." A trigger makes the recommendation falsifiable and time-bound.

The reason both extremes fail is that they ignore part of the evidence. An **unconditional adopt** overstates the side the evidence does not support (here, privacy). A **flat reject** wastes the side it does support (here, learning). The conditional recommendation is not a hedge; it is the only statement that uses all of the evidence at once.

Three course anchors keep the recommendation matched to evidence. **TPACK** anchors the scope to the specific learning the evidence actually covered (math, grades 6 to 8), not to the tool in general. **Critical edtech studies** insists that an unresolved risk becomes an explicit condition rather than a footnote. **AI and education datafication** is frequently the source of the condition itself, since data-handling gaps are the risks most often left unsigned.

A defensible recommendation is built in order, narrowest commitment outward. Put the four parts in the sequence you would write them, from the claim that limits everything to the trigger that can undo it.

### Worked Example: From Bare Verdict to Conditional Recommendation

Scenario: a committee evaluated an adaptive-math platform. Evidence: solid learning gains in a 4-week trial, but the data-processing agreement is unsigned.

1. **First draft (before).** The recommendation reads: "We recommend adopting the platform." One sentence, no scope, no conditions.
2. **Matched the recommendation to the evidence.** Learning evidence is strong; privacy is unresolved. An unconditional "adopt" overstates the privacy side, and a flat "reject" wastes the learning evidence.
3. **Wrote it conditionally.** Scope: grades 6 to 8 math only. Step: a one-semester pilot, not a district rollout. Condition: the signed data-processing agreement must be in place before any student account is created. Reversal trigger: if the agreement is not signed by August 15, the recommendation is withdrawn and the pilot does not start.
4. **Annotated judgment call (after).** The revised recommendation reads: "Recommend a one-semester grade 6 to 8 pilot, conditional on a signed data agreement by August 15; if unmet, no launch. We rejected an unconditional adopt because the privacy evidence did not support it, and rejected a flat reject because the learning evidence did."

The recommendation is defensible because a stakeholder can see exactly what is being recommended, on what conditions, and what would reverse it.

## Decision Rule

Use the strength of the learning evidence first, the unresolved risks second, and the size of the commitment last. A recommendation is defensible only when its scope, conditions, and reversal trigger match what the evidence does and does not support.

Before you submit a recommendation, test it against three questions:

1. **Scope check:** Does the recommendation name exactly who and what, limited to what the evidence covered?
2. **Condition check:** Is every unresolved risk written as a condition that must be met before the step proceeds, rather than mentioned and waived?
3. **Reversal check:** Is there a stated trigger (a deadline, a missing signature, a failed metric) that would withdraw the recommendation?

If the recommendation cannot be reversed by any future fact, it is an opinion, not a defensible decision, and it should be rewritten with a trigger.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is treating a conditional recommendation as indecision; in fact it is more decisive than a bare verdict because it states precisely what will and will not happen and under what terms. The second is mentioning a risk in passing and then recommending adoption anyway, which acknowledges the gap without gating it. The third is writing a scope broader than the evidence, so a four-week math trial silently authorizes a year-long, all-subject rollout.

A defensible recommendation states scope, condition, and reversal trigger explicitly, and records why both the unconditional adopt and the flat reject were rejected.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Start from the bare draft, "we recommend adopting the platform," and ask what evidence it rests on and what it ignores. The learning gains are real but cover only four weeks of grades 6 to 8 math; the privacy agreement is unsigned. The one-sentence verdict overstates the privacy readiness and silently widens the scope beyond what was tested.

Rebuild it part by part. Scope it to grades 6 to 8 math, the only learning the evidence covers. Set the step to a one-semester pilot, since a four-week trial cannot justify a district rollout. Attach the condition that the data-processing agreement be signed before any student account is created, converting the unresolved risk into a gate. Add the reversal trigger: if the signature is not in place by August 15, the recommendation lapses and the pilot does not begin.

Close by recording the two rejected alternatives and why. Unconditional adopt was rejected because the privacy evidence did not support it; flat reject was rejected because the learning evidence did. A stakeholder reading the result sees a decision that uses every piece of the evidence and can be held to a date.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a four-slot note: scope, step, condition, reversal. For any recommendation you encounter, fill the slots; the empty ones are exactly where a bare verdict has overrun or wasted the evidence. A recommendation with no condition and no trigger should read as incomplete, not as confident.

A strong reader of this section can answer four questions:

1. What is the precise scope, and does it stay within what the evidence covered?
2. Which unresolved risks are written as conditions rather than waived?
3. What specific event would reverse the recommendation?
4. Why were both the unconditional adopt and the flat reject rejected?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

For this section, the recommendation is strong when scope, step, condition, and reversal trigger are all stated and the two extremes are explicitly rejected, so a mentor can see the decision matches the evidence on every side. Treat a one-word verdict as unfinished, regardless of how confident it sounds.

A useful closing line would sound like this: "I changed the recommendation to a one-semester grades 6 to 8 pilot, conditional on a signed data agreement by August 15, withdrawn if unmet. I kept out the suggestion to write a simple 'adopt' because it would have overstated the unresolved privacy evidence."

## Transfer Case

Move to a tool with the opposite evidence profile: the data agreement is signed and airtight, but the learning evidence is a single anecdote from one teacher. The four parts still apply, but the condition now attaches to the learning gap, not the privacy gap: recommend a small pilot conditional on collecting comparison data this term, with a reversal trigger if the pilot shows no gain. The structure transfers even though the risk that drives the condition has flipped. Use the shift to confirm that you build the recommendation from where the evidence is weakest, not from a fixed template.
