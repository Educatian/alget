## Why This Section Matters

A vendor's job is to make a tool sound effective; a teacher's job is to find out whether it is. Those are not the same task, and the gap between them is where most weak adoption decisions live. "Boosts scores 35 percent," "research-backed," "trusted by 10,000 classrooms": each phrase is engineered to read as evidence while often resting on a study the vendor funded, a sample of a dozen students, or no comparison group at all. This section teaches teachers to slow down at exactly the moment a claim sounds strongest and ask what kind of evidence, if any, stands behind it.

The skill is not cynicism. Some vendor claims are well supported, and dismissing every claim is as lazy as believing every claim. The professional move is to grade each claim by the evidence that backs it, then decide how much weight that evidence can bear. A teacher who can do this protects students from tools that work only in a brochure.

## Learning Targets

- Separate a vendor's claim from the evidence offered for it, naming the study design behind any effect size.
- Identify the three weaknesses that most often inflate edtech claims: no control group, tiny or self-selected samples, and a conflicted funder.
- Assign an evidence weight to a claim and justify the weight from the design, not the headline number.
- Document a decision that keeps a promising tool on the table while logging its claim as unproven.

## Opening Case

A vendor pitches a writing-feedback tool with the headline "Boosts writing scores by 35 percent, peer-reviewed." The number is large, the word peer-reviewed is reassuring, and the temptation is to write "adopt." But the cited study turns out to have been run by the vendor's own team, on 11 students, over three weeks, with no comparison class. The 35 percent is simply the rise from a pretest to a posttest.

The decision worth recording is not whether the tool is good or bad. It is whether the teacher can name precisely why a 35 percent pre-post gain with no control and n = 11 cannot, on its own, justify a purchase, and what evidence would.

## Core Concept

A **claim** is an assertion of effect ("improves writing"). **Evidence** is the design and data offered to support it. The two are routinely conflated because vendors present evidence-shaped language ("35 percent," "study") in place of evidence-grade design. Reading critically means refusing to let a number stand in for the design that produced it.

Three weaknesses inflate edtech claims more than any others, and each has a plain-language test:

- **No control group.** A pre-post gain cannot separate the tool from everything else that happened (normal teaching, maturation, practice effects). Ask: compared to what? If there is no comparison, the gain is uninterpretable as a tool effect.
- **Small or self-selected sample.** Eleven students, or volunteers who opted in, cannot represent a class of 24, let alone a district. Ask: who, and how many, and were they chosen or did they choose?
- **Conflicted funder.** A study run or paid for by the seller has an incentive baked in. Ask: who would lose money if this study had found no effect?

**Where this comes from.** These design checks mirror two named standards teachers actually meet in district procurement. The What Works Clearinghouse Procedures and Standards Handbook (Version 5.0) reserves its top rating, "Meets WWC Group Design Standards Without Reservations," for low-attrition randomized controlled trials, and only single-case designs may lack a comparison group, which grounds the "no control group" weakness above. The ESSA tiers of evidence (Strong, Moderate, Promising, Demonstrates a Rationale) descend in rigor with design strength, and the top three tiers all require a statistically significant favorable effect on student outcomes, which grounds the whole rule that evidence weight comes from design, not the headline number.

Each weakness lowers the **evidence weight** you assign, regardless of how large the headline effect is. A 35 percent gain on a flawed design carries less weight than a 6 percent gain from a randomized comparison.

Three course anchors sharpen the read. **[TPACK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_pedagogical_content_knowledge)** asks whether the claimed outcome is even the learning you care about, since a tool can raise a narrow score while teaching little. **Critical edtech studies** (Selwyn, 2021) names the marketing machinery and the incentive to overstate. **[AI and education datafication](https://doi.org/10.1080/17439884.2020.1686017)** flags claims about "personalization" or "AI accuracy" that hide what data the tool collects to make them.

## Worked Example

A first draft often hides the real decision behind a vague improvement claim:

> I made the Design Tension Studio map naming a classroom value conflict and negotiated decision clearer.

That claim is too thin because "clearer" does not identify the audience, the problem, or the evidence. A stronger claim would read:

> I revised the Design Tension Studio map naming a classroom value conflict and negotiated decision so that the intended audience can see the decision, the constraint, and the evidence source. The revision is justified by **critical edtech studies**, and I can point to the exact part of the artifact that changed.

Notice the shift: the artifact is no longer just polished. The revision names the evidence source and preserves the reason a suggestion was accepted or rejected.

### Worked Example: Separating a Vendor Claim From Evidence

Scenario: a vendor pitches a writing-feedback tool with the headline "Boosts writing scores by 35 percent."

1. **Found the claim as written (before).** The brochure says "35 percent improvement, peer-reviewed study." A teacher annotates: "Sounds strong, adopt." No source examined yet.
2. **Traced the claim to its study.** The cited study was run by the vendor's own team on 11 students for 3 weeks, with no comparison group. The "35 percent" was the rise from a pretest to a posttest with no control.
3. **Reweighed each claim.** Vendor claim: 35 percent gain. Evidence weight: low, because there is no control group, the sample is 11, and the funder is the vendor. A pre-post rise with no comparison cannot separate the tool from normal instruction.
4. **Annotated judgment call (after).** The revised note reads: "Claim kept on the table but logged as low-weight, conflicted, n=11, no control. Decision: pilot in one section ourselves with a comparison class before any purchase. We rejected the brochure's framing of 35 percent as established evidence."

The brief is now defensible because a reviewer sees the exact gap between what the vendor claimed and what the evidence can actually support.

## Decision Rule

Use the claimed outcome first, the study design second, and the funder third. A claim earns weight only when the design can actually support the size of the effect it asserts.

When a vendor number looks persuasive, run it through three tests before it enters the matrix:

1. **Design check:** Was there a comparison group? A pre-post-only result cannot be read as a tool effect.
2. **Sample check:** How many students, and were they chosen or self-selected? Small or volunteer samples cannot generalize to your class.
3. **Funder check:** Who paid for or ran the study, and who profits if it shows an effect?

A claim that fails any of these is not banned from your evaluation; it is simply logged at low weight, with a note on what evidence (a controlled pilot, an independent study) would raise it.

## Common Misreadings

One misreading is to treat "peer-reviewed" or "research-backed" as a verdict rather than a starting point. Those phrases describe a process, not a result, and a weak study can survive review. The opposite misreading is to dismiss every vendor claim as marketing; that throws away tools that may genuinely help and replaces evidence with reflex.

A defensible brief shows the claim, the design behind it, and the weight assigned, so a colleague can see that the conclusion tracks the evidence rather than the headline.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Take the 35 percent claim and walk it to the floor. First, write the claim verbatim and resist scoring it. Then locate the study it rests on and extract three facts: comparison group (none), sample (11), funder (the vendor). Each fact is a deduction from the claim's credibility, and you record the deduction rather than the impression.

Next, ask the [TPACK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_pedagogical_content_knowledge) question the brochure skips: 35 percent of what? If the posttest rewarded surface features the tool happens to coach, the number could rise while real writing did not improve. The claimed outcome and the valued outcome may not be the same thing, and the brief must say so.

Finally, convert the read into a decision that wastes neither the tool nor the truth. The tool stays a candidate, but its claim is logged as low-weight and conflicted, and the next step is a controlled pilot you run, not a purchase you justify with the vendor's slide. That sequence, claim to design to weight to bounded next step, is the whole discipline.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: **claim** on the left, **evidence weight and why** on the right. For each vendor assertion, write the design fact that raises or lowers its weight. The column on the right should never just repeat the headline number; it should explain what the design can and cannot support.

A strong reader of this section can answer four questions:

1. What exactly is being claimed, and what outcome does it really measure?
2. What study design stands behind the claim, and what are its weaknesses?
3. What weight does that design justify, independent of the effect size?
4. What evidence would raise the weight enough to justify adoption?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

For this section, the brief is strong when every claim is paired with its evidence weight and the reason for that weight, so a mentor can audit whether the conclusion follows the evidence. Treat a large headline number as a prompt to investigate, never as a column total. If the only support is a vendor-run pre-post study, the brief should record it as such and route to a local pilot.

A useful closing line would sound like this: "I changed the brief so the 35 percent claim is logged at low weight with its design noted (no control, n=11, vendor-funded). I kept out the suggestion to feature the 35 percent in the recommendation because the design cannot support a number that large as a tool effect."

**A live example of the gap.** As of 2025, Khan Academy had not run a randomized controlled trial of its widely promoted Khanmigo AI tutor (citing expense), while independent observers noted vendors marketing generative-AI tutors with little to no evidence that they match human tutoring, exactly the control, sample, and funder questions this section trains you to ask before believing an efficacy claim.

## Transfer Case

Move from a vendor claim to an independent claim: a district study, run by a neutral research office, reports a 6 percent gain from a randomized comparison across 400 students. The same three tests now run in the tool's favor: there is a control, the sample is large, the funder is neutral. The smaller number deserves more weight than the vendor's larger one. Use this reversal to confirm that you are grading evidence by design, not by the size of the number or the warmth of the source.
