# Readiness Review

![Readiness Review textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/08-08-readiness-review.png)

## Why This Section Matters

A readiness review is the gate a teacher passes through before deploying an AI tool with real students. The defining feature of a gate, as opposed to a celebration, is that it can return a no-go. A review that only confirms what everyone hopes is not a review; it is a rubber stamp. This closing section asks you to check each artifact the toolkit produced against a go or no-go criterion, to attach a verdict to each one, and to hold a no-go when the evidence requires it, even when launch is scheduled for next week.

This matters because the cost of a premature launch falls on students and families. Unsent consent forms, an uncollected baseline, a rubric that punishes accommodation: any one of these is enough to harm someone if the tool goes live anyway. A readiness review that names the failing item and blocks launch until it closes is the most consequential act of professional judgment in the whole toolkit.

## Learning Targets

- Run a readiness review as a go/no-go gate: assign a traceable verdict to each prior artifact, let a single safety, consent, or equity blocker hold the launch, and record a rejected boundary-reversing shortcut.
- Distinguish artifact polish from evidence-based revision.
- Document a bounded AI/software support move with accepted and rejected suggestions.
- Use annotations, support requests, and revision notes to make a classroom technology decision more defensible.

## Opening Case

A team is set to launch an AI tutor next week. The lead writes a readiness note: "Everything looks good, we are ready." But consent forms have not gone home, and the pre-tool baseline has not been collected. The cheerful note has skipped the two items that should block launch. A real readiness review would have caught both, returned a no-go on consent, and a conditional on the baseline. The question this section answers is how to run the review as a gate that ties each verdict to a specific prior artifact and is willing to say not yet.

## Core Concept

A readiness review is a structured audit of the artifacts produced in earlier sections, each given one of three verdicts:

- **Go** means the artifact meets its criterion and presents no blocker. The tension map names a real conflict and a bounded decision; the troubleshooting transcript shows edits and a principled rejection; the rubric scores judgment with its equity note.
- **No-go** means an item is missing or unsafe and launch must wait. Unsent consent forms are a classic no-go: without them the tool is being used on students whose families never agreed, and no amount of enthusiasm overrides that.
- **Conditional** means an item is acceptable but incomplete, with a defined action that must finish before or during launch. An evidence plan with a defined baseline that has not yet been collected is conditional: collect it before week one or the later result row is untestable.

The review's integrity depends on traceability: each verdict points to a specific prior artifact, so the verdict is checkable rather than impressionistic. It also depends on the willingness to reject a tempting shortcut. "Launch now and gather consent afterward" reverses the policy boundary and must be refused; recording that refusal is part of the review. Teacher professional judgment is the anchor that makes a no-go possible, because it locates the authority to halt a launch with the teacher rather than with the schedule.

## Decision Rule

When running a readiness review, audit each prior artifact against its criterion first, assign go, no-go, or conditional second, and refuse any shortcut that reverses a boundary third. Before you treat the review as finished, confirm it answers:

1. **Traceability check:** Does each verdict point to a specific prior artifact and its criterion?
2. **Blocker check:** Is any safety, consent, or equity item a no-go that must close before launch?
3. **Integrity check:** Did you record any tempting shortcut you rejected, and why it would reverse a boundary?

## Common Misreadings

<inline-check question="A reviewer writes: 'Seven of eight items pass, so we are ready to launch,' even though the eighth is unsent consent. What error is this?" explanation="Averaging the verdicts. Readiness is not a majority vote; a single safety, consent, or equity no-go blocks the launch regardless of how many other items are green." options='[{"text":"No error; a strong majority means ready.","correct":false},{"text":"Averaging verdicts, which lets a single blocking no-go be outvoted.","correct":true},{"text":"Failing to add a polished summary.","correct":false},{"text":"Citing too many prior artifacts.","correct":false}]'></inline-check>

The most damaging error is treating the review as a formality whose only acceptable outcome is approval. If the review cannot return a no-go, it is not protecting anyone. A second error is averaging the verdicts, declaring overall readiness because most items passed; readiness is not a majority vote, and a single consent failure blocks the launch regardless of how many other items are green. A third error is accepting a plausible shortcut, such as gathering consent after launch, which trades a real boundary for convenience.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Imagine you are the teacher about to deploy an AI reading tutor. Pull each prior artifact and judge it against its own criterion: is the tension named, is the tool bounded with a rejection, does the rubric score judgment, does the memo answer consent and name a fallback, does the evidence plan have a baseline? Assign go, no-go, or conditional to each, citing the section it came from. When you hit the unsent consent forms, write no-go and stop the launch clock; when you hit the uncollected baseline, write conditional with a deadline. Then state the overall verdict honestly: not ready now, ready when the named items close.

The artifact is the review itself: a verdict per prior section, each traceable, an honest overall verdict, and at least one recorded rejection of a boundary-reversing shortcut.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

Read a readiness review by asking whether it could ever say no. If every line trends toward approval, it is a celebration, not a gate. Check that each verdict cites a specific artifact and that a single blocker is allowed to stop the whole launch. Keep a two-column note: the prior artifact and its criterion on the left, the verdict and its evidence on the right. A strong review lets an administrator see exactly what is ready, what is blocked, and why launch is or is not safe this week.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A readiness review meets the standard when a mentor teacher can see a traceable verdict for each prior artifact, an honest overall verdict that a blocker can override, and a recorded rejection of any boundary-reversing shortcut. Presentation is the last layer; the per-artifact verdicts and the willingness to fail are load-bearing. If a verdict has no cited artifact, annotate the gap and ask BigAL to compare your review against an exemplar that returns a no-go rather than to make the summary sound more confident. A useful closing line reads: "I tied each verdict to a prior artifact and held a no-go on unsent consent, and I rejected the suggestion to launch first and collect consent later because it reverses the policy boundary."

## Transfer Case

Move the readiness review from a single classroom to a school-wide rollout. The gate logic transfers, but the blockers scale: consent must be tracked across many sections, the fallback must work in rooms with different device access, and the evidence baseline must be comparable across teachers. A single building-level no-go, such as one grade lacking consent, can hold the whole rollout. Rebuilding the review for the school-wide case, rather than reusing the single-class version, shows you can run readiness as a gate at any scale.
