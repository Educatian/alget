# Rubric Language for AI Use

![Rubric Language for AI Use textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/08-04-rubric-language-for-ai-use.png)

## Why This Section Matters

When teachers first write rubric language about AI, they tend to reach for a ban: full points if no AI was used, zero if any was. The instinct is understandable, but the rule scores the wrong thing. It rewards abstinence rather than judgment, it cannot be verified, and it penalizes exactly the students who use AI as an accommodation. This section is about writing rubric rows that assess the quality of a student's reasoning about AI use, which is the actual learning target in a course on defensible technology decisions.

This matters because a rubric is a policy in miniature. Whatever it scores, students will optimize, and a rubric that scores "no AI" teaches students to hide tools rather than to use them thoughtfully. A rubric that scores disclosed, examined judgment teaches the habit the course exists to build, and it does so without creating an equity penalty that the rubric never names.

## Opening Case

A teacher writes a rubric row: "AI Use (10 pts): full points if no AI was used; zero if any AI was used." A bilingual student who used a translation aid to check phrasing scores zero, while a fluent peer who used AI to outline but does not admit it scores full marks. The rule has produced the opposite of fairness, and it has measured honesty rather than learning. The question this section answers is how to rewrite that row so it scores what the course actually values: the student's reasoning about when and how to use a tool.

## Core Concept

A rubric criterion is well written when the thing it scores is a construct the assignment is meant to develop, and when that construct is observable in the student's work. For AI use, three principles separate a defensible row from a counterproductive one:

- **Score the judgment, not the presence.** The construct is the quality of the student's decision-making: where they used AI, what they accepted, what they rejected, and why. Presence of a tool is neither good nor bad on its own and is usually unverifiable anyway.
- **Make the construct observable.** A defensible row points the grader at something concrete: a disclosed list of AI use, at least one accepted suggestion, at least one rejected suggestion, and a reason tied to course evidence. If the grader has nothing to point at, the row scores impressions.
- **Name the equity risk inside the row.** A context-specific risk note states, in the rubric itself, that the score depends on disclosed reasoning and never on whether a tool was present, so students using AI as an accommodation are protected by design rather than by the grader's goodwill.

| Property | Ban-style row | Judgment-scoring row |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it measures | Tool presence (a proxy) | Quality of disclosed reasoning (the construct) |
| Verifiability | Unverifiable | Observable accept/reject decisions |
| Equity effect | Penalizes accommodation users | Risk note protects them explicitly |
| Behavior it trains | Hide tool use | Disclose and examine tool use |

Critical edtech studies supplies the equity lens that exposes how a neutral-sounding ban lands unevenly. TPACK keeps the criterion aimed at the learning target rather than at a surface behavior.

## Worked Example

Compare a rubric row that confuses tool presence with misconduct against one that scores the student's judgment.

### Annotated judgment call: rubric language that scores AI use, not AI presence

Scenario: a teacher is writing a rubric row for an assignment where students may use AI. The first draft punishes any AI use; the revised row scores the quality of the student's judgment about AI use, which is the actual learning target.

Before (rubric language that confuses presence with misconduct):

> AI Use (10 pts): Full points if no AI was used. Zero points if any AI was used.

Walk the row against the equity matrix, criterion by criterion:

1. Equity check. A blanket ban advantages students who already write fluently and penalizes ELL students and students who use AI as an accommodation. The before row creates a context-specific risk it does not name.
2. Construct check. The course target is teacher judgment about tools, not abstinence. Scoring "no AI" rewards the wrong behavior and is unverifiable.
3. Evidence check. The row gives the grader no observable signal. There is nothing to point to in the student's work.

After (annotated rubric row, equity risk noted):

> AI Use and Judgment (10 pts): 9 to 10 = student names where AI was used, shows at least one suggestion they accepted and one they rejected, and explains the reason against course evidence. 6 to 8 = AI use is disclosed but rejection reasoning is thin. 0 to 5 = AI use is undisclosed or unexamined. Risk note: this row is judged on disclosed reasoning, never on whether a tool was present, so students using AI as an accommodation are not penalized.

The after row is stronger because it makes the construct observable (accept and reject decisions), names the equity risk in the rubric itself, and gives the grader something specific to point to.

## Decision Rule

When writing rubric language for AI use, define the construct first, make it observable second, and write the equity risk note third. Before you treat a row as finished, confirm it answers:

1. **Construct check:** Does the row score the quality of reasoning the assignment is meant to teach, rather than a proxy like tool presence?
2. **Observability check:** What concrete signal in the student's work does the grader point to for each performance level?
3. **Equity check:** Does the row name, in its own text, how it protects students who use AI as an accommodation?

A frequent error is believing that a stricter rule is a fairer rule. Severity and fairness are independent; a blanket ban is severe and unfair at once. Another error is leaving the equity protection implicit, trusting the grader to apply it case by case. If the protection is not written into the row, it does not exist for the student who reads the rubric. A third error is writing performance levels that describe effort or amount of writing rather than the quality of the AI-related judgment, which lets the construct drift back toward word count.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Suppose you are adding an AI row to a research-paper rubric. Start by naming the construct in one sentence: students should make and disclose defensible decisions about where AI helped and where they overrode it. Then write the top level as something a grader can verify: a disclosure of use, a named accepted suggestion, a named rejected suggestion, and a reason connected to course material. Write the lower levels as degradations of that same evidence, not as penalties for using the tool. Finally, add the risk note in plain language so any student, including one using AI as an accommodation, can see they will be scored on reasoning rather than on the presence of a tool.

The artifact is the rewritten row plus its risk note, with the construct stated and each level tied to an observable signal.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

Read a rubric row by asking what behavior it will produce. If the row rewards hiding tools, it will train concealment; if it rewards disclosed reasoning, it will train judgment. Check whether each performance level names something a grader can point at. Keep a two-column note: the construct the row claims to measure on the left, the observable signal for it on the right. A strong row makes the grader's job concrete and makes the equity protection visible to the student before they submit.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A rubric row meets the standard when a mentor teacher can see the construct, the observable signal at each level, and the written equity risk note. Formatting polish is the last layer; the construct and the risk note are load-bearing. If a level lacks an observable signal, annotate it and ask BigAL to compare your row against an exemplar that scores judgment rather than to make the wording sound stricter. A useful closing line reads: "I scored disclosed reasoning instead of tool presence and wrote the equity risk into the row, and I rejected the blanket-ban version because it penalized accommodation users and measured the wrong thing."

## Transfer Case

Move the row from a writing assignment to a coding assignment where students may use an AI pair programmer. The construct holds: disclose where the tool helped, show an accepted and a rejected suggestion, justify against course concepts. The observable signal changes from prose reasoning to commit notes or a brief usage log, and the equity note now protects students who rely on the tool for accessibility. Rewriting the row for code, rather than reusing the writing version, confirms you can carry the principle across content areas.
