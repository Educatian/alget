# Facilitator Notes for DTS

![Facilitator Notes for DTS textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/08-01-facilitator-notes-for-dts.png)

## Why This Section Matters

A facilitator note is the written memory of a live decision. When a teacher runs a Design Tension Studio (DTS) session, the room produces opinions, energy, and a tentative plan; the facilitator note is what survives after the room empties. If that note only records that the group "liked the AI tool," it preserves a mood, not a judgment. The professional skill in this section is writing notes that capture the value conflict the group actually wrestled with, the decision they negotiated, and the conditions attached to that decision, so that a colleague who was not in the room can audit and continue the work.

This matters because technology decisions in schools are rarely made once. They are revisited when a new family objects, when a device fails, or when an administrator asks why a tool was chosen. A facilitator note written to be defensible becomes the document that answers those later questions. A note written only to feel finished becomes a liability.

## Opening Case

A 7th-grade humanities team meets to decide whether to adopt an AI essay-feedback tool. The conversation is lively and the team leaves saying yes. The facilitator writes: "Team is enthusiastic; adopting the AI feedback tool next unit." Two weeks later a parent asks why their child's essay was read by an AI vendor, and the principal asks the facilitator what safeguards the team agreed to. The note answers neither question, because it recorded enthusiasm instead of the negotiated decision and its conditions.

The contrast worth studying is between a note that records what people felt and a note that records what the group committed to and on what terms. Only the second kind protects students and the teacher when the decision is later questioned.

## Core Concept

A Design Tension Studio treats a technology decision as a conflict between two goods that cannot both be maximized at once. The facilitator's job is to keep both goods visible while the group negotiates a position between them. A facilitator note is strong when it captures four things that a tension map should contain:

- **The two axes of the tension.** Name both goods in competition, for example "faster, more frequent feedback for every student" against "students keep authorship and teachers keep authority over judgment." A note that names only one axis hides the conflict.
- **Where the risk lands.** Identify which students bear the cost of the decision. English-language learners and students with IEPs are more likely to be misread by a generic essay model, so an equity cost that is invisible at the whole-class level becomes visible when you ask who is most affected.
- **The negotiated position, not the preference.** Record the specific compromise the group reached, including any bounds placed on the tool, rather than the mood of the room.
- **The evidence to collect next.** A negotiated decision is a hypothesis about practice. The note should name what would confirm or disconfirm it.

| Note element | Weak version | Defensible version |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tension | "We talked about pros and cons." | Axis A (feedback speed) vs Axis B (authorship and teacher judgment). |
| Risk location | "Good for the class." | ELL and IEP students most likely to be misread; 6 flagged-for-support students named. |
| Decision | "Adopting the tool." | AI drafts comments; teacher reviews and signs each before release. |
| Next evidence | (absent) | One-week sample comparing AI drafts to teacher-final comments. |

The course anchors sharpen these moves. TPACK keeps you asking whether the tool actually serves the content and pedagogy, not just the workflow. Critical edtech studies presses you to ask whose burden and whose data the tool quietly assumes. Together they turn a note from a record of consensus into a record of reasoning.

## Worked Example

Consider the difference between a facilitator note that polishes the meeting and one that preserves the decision. A first-pass note often reads:

> Facilitator note: The AI essay tool is engaging and saves grading time. Recommend adopting it for the unit.

This is polish. It names a benefit and a recommendation but no tension, no risk, and no bound on the tool. A reader cannot tell what the team agreed to constrain.

### Annotated judgment call: a facilitator note that names the tension

Scenario: a CAT 531 facilitator is running the Design Tension Studio with the 7th-grade humanities team that wants the AI feedback tool. The note must surface the value conflict, not hide it.

Before (polish without a named tension):

> Facilitator note: The AI essay tool is engaging and saves grading time. Recommend adopting it for the unit.

Walk the note against the studio map, one axis at a time:

1. Name the value conflict. Axis A is "faster, more frequent feedback for every student." Axis B is "students keep authorship and teachers keep authority over judgment." The before note only names Axis A, so the tension is invisible.
2. Locate who bears the risk. ELL students and students with IEPs are most likely to be misread by a generic essay model, so the equity cost lands unevenly. The before note treats the class as one undifferentiated group.
3. Record the negotiated decision, not the preference. The team agrees the tool may generate draft comments, but the teacher reads and signs every comment before it reaches a student, and no comment is sent on flagged-for-support students without a human first pass.

After (annotated facilitator note with the tension named and a negotiated decision):

> Facilitator note (08.01): Value conflict on the studio map is feedback speed (Axis A) versus student authorship and teacher judgment authority (Axis B). Negotiated decision: AI drafts comments; teacher reviews and signs each one before release; comments on the 6 flagged-for-support students get a mandatory human first pass. Evidence to collect next: a 1-week sample of AI draft comments versus teacher-final comments to measure how often the teacher overrode the model.

The after note is stronger because a mentor teacher can audit it: the tension is on the map, the equity risk is located on named students, and the negotiated decision is something the team can be held to and can later test with evidence.

## Decision Rule

When writing a facilitator note, put the named value conflict first, the tool's affordance second, and the policy boundary third. A decision is only as defensible as the conditions attached to it. Before you close the note, confirm it answers three questions a later reader will ask:

1. **Tension check:** What two goods were in conflict, and where did the group land between them?
2. **Risk check:** Which students bear the cost of this decision, and what protects them?
3. **Bound check:** What is the tool explicitly not allowed to do, and what evidence will test whether the decision held?

A note that cannot answer all three may feel complete but will not survive a challenge.

## Common Misreadings

The most frequent error is mistaking enthusiasm for agreement. A room can feel unanimous about adopting a tool while disagreeing entirely about how it should be bounded, and a note that records the warmth erases that unfinished negotiation. A second error is recording the decision without its conditions, which lets the tool drift toward uses no one actually approved. The note should make the boundary as visible as the choice.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Take a concrete situation: your team has agreed to pilot an AI feedback tool, and you are the one writing the note that goes into the shared unit folder. Begin by stating the tension in the team's own terms, not in abstract language, so a colleague recognizes the real argument. Then write the negotiated decision as a rule someone could follow without you in the room: who does what, in what order, and with what human checkpoint.

Next, attach the bound. The team did not approve "using AI for feedback"; it approved "AI drafts, teacher signs, mandatory human first pass for six named students." That precision is what separates a defensible note from a vague endorsement. Finally, name the evidence that will tell you whether the decision was sound, so the note points forward to verification rather than closing the question prematurely.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

Read a facilitator note the way an auditor would. Ask whether you could reconstruct the meeting's real decision from the note alone, and whether you could tell a skeptical parent exactly what the tool is and is not permitted to do. As you read your own draft, keep a two-column check: on the left, the claim the note makes; on the right, the condition or evidence that makes the claim inspectable. A strong note lets a reader who missed the meeting hold the team to its commitments without needing anyone to explain them again.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A facilitator note reaches the standard when a mentor teacher can see the value conflict, the bounded decision, and the named risk without asking a follow-up question. Visual neatness is the last layer; the load-bearing layers are the tension, the bound, and the evidence plan. If any of those is missing, mark the gap with an annotation and ask BigAL for a targeted comparison between your note and a defensible exemplar rather than a general explanation of facilitation. A useful closing line reads: "I recorded the negotiated decision and its bound because the team's agreement was conditional, and I left out the suggestion to adopt the tool unconditionally because no one in the room actually approved that."

## Transfer Case

Move the same note to a high-stakes setting: a self-contained special-education classroom where the AI essay model is more likely to misread student writing. The tension axes stay the same, but the risk axis dominates, and the negotiated decision should shift toward a heavier human first pass or no AI feedback on certain students at all. Rewriting the note for this setting, rather than copying it, is the test of whether you understand facilitator notes as judgment rather than as a template.
