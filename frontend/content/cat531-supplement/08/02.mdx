# TeachGen@i Troubleshooting

![TeachGen@i Troubleshooting textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/08-02-teachgen-i-troubleshooting.png)

## Why This Section Matters

When a teacher uses a generative tool like TeachGen@i to draft a quiz, a reading, or a parent message, the first output is almost never usable as-is. The professional skill is not prompting; it is troubleshooting: noticing where the model's default assumptions miss the actual class, editing the prompt to correct them, and knowing which suggestions to refuse outright. A clean final prompt hides all of that reasoning. A troubleshooting transcript, by contrast, shows the teacher's judgment at work, turn by turn, including the suggestion the teacher declined.

This matters because a generative tool encodes assumptions about reading level, cultural reference, and who should make grading and contact decisions. Those assumptions are invisible until a student is misserved by them. A teacher who can document the troubleshooting trail can defend not only the final artifact but the line where the tool's authority stopped and the teacher's began.

## Opening Case

A 5th-grade teacher asks TeachGen@i for a five-question water-cycle quiz and accepts the first output. In class, three students stall on the wording, one item assumes a family ski trip, and the reading level is closer to 9th grade than 5th. The output looked finished, so the teacher trusted it. The failure was not the model; it was the absence of troubleshooting between the request and the use. The interesting question is what the teacher should have noticed and corrected before the quiz reached students, and how to keep a record of those corrections.

## Core Concept

Generative output should be treated as a draft from a confident but context-blind collaborator. Troubleshooting is the disciplined process of testing that draft against the real class and recording each correction as a turn in a transcript. A defensible troubleshooting transcript distinguishes three kinds of moves:

- **A teacher edit** corrects a default the model could not know: reading level, local examples, the standard being assessed. Each edit should name the constraint it fixes.
- **A rejected suggestion** is a place where the model offered to do something that belongs to teacher judgment or to policy, not to a tool. Recording the rejection, and why, is the most defensible move in the transcript.
- **A verification step** is what the teacher will do to confirm the edited output is sound, such as having a colleague check revised items against the grade band.

The constraints that drive edits usually fall into a short list: reading level (a measurable target such as a Lexile band), equity of reference (examples that do not assume household income or family experiences), and alignment to the actual standard rather than a plausible-sounding topic. Critical edtech studies adds the question the model never asks itself: which students do these defaults quietly disadvantage? TPACK keeps the edits anchored to content and pedagogy rather than to surface fluency.

The line between an edit and a rejection is the heart of the section. An edit improves what the tool produces; a rejection protects a decision the tool should never make, such as auto-grading reasoning or contacting families.

## Worked Example

A first draft of a troubleshooting record often collapses the whole process into "I made the quiz with AI." That erases the judgment. Compare a record that hides the troubleshooting with one that preserves it.

### Annotated judgment call: troubleshooting a TeachGen@i prompt transcript

Scenario: a teacher used TeachGen@i to generate a quiz on the water cycle and the first prompt produced biased and off-grade items. The troubleshooting transcript should show teacher edits and at least one rejected suggestion, not a clean final prompt.

Before (prompt that hides the troubleshooting):

> Prompt: "Make a 5-question water cycle quiz for my class."
> Output accepted as-is.

Walk the transcript, turn by turn, and annotate where teacher judgment had to intervene:

1. Turn 1 problem. The output assumed reading at a 9th-grade Lexile band; the class is 5th grade. Teacher edit: add "for 5th-grade readers, Lexile 740 to 940, one sentence per stem."
2. Turn 2 problem. Item 3 used a snow-skiing example that assumes family ski trips. Teacher edit: replace with a rain-barrel example that does not assume household income.
3. Rejected suggestion. TeachGen@i offered to auto-grade short answers and email scores to parents. Teacher rejects it: short-answer judgment on science explanations is the teacher's call, and parent contact is governed by district policy, not a tool default.

After (annotated troubleshooting transcript):

> Prompt v3: "Write 5 water-cycle items for 5th-grade readers (Lexile 740 to 940), one sentence per stem, examples that do not assume household income or family trips. Return items only; I will grade short answers myself."
> Annotation: edited reading level (turn 1), swapped the ski example for a rain barrel (turn 2), rejected auto-grading and auto-email to parents (kept teacher judgment and district consent rules). Evidence to collect next: have a colleague check the 5 revised stems against the 5th-grade band.

The after transcript is stronger because the teacher's reasoning is inspectable: each edit is tied to a named constraint (reading level, equity, policy boundary), and the one rejected suggestion shows where the tool's authority stopped.

## Decision Rule

When troubleshooting generative output, fix the content and reading-level defaults first, audit the examples for hidden assumptions second, and refuse any suggestion that moves grading judgment or family contact to the tool third. Before you treat a transcript as finished, confirm it answers:

1. **Constraint check:** Which named constraint (reading level, equity, standard alignment) did each edit correct?
2. **Authority check:** Which suggestion did you reject, and why does that decision belong to a teacher or to policy rather than to the tool?
3. **Verification check:** How will you confirm the edited output is sound before students see it?

## Common Misreadings

One misreading is treating a longer or more detailed prompt as the goal. Prompt length is not the deliverable; the documented chain of corrected assumptions is.

 A second misreading is recording only the edits and dropping the rejection. The rejection is often the most defensible move in the whole transcript, because it shows the teacher holding a boundary the tool was happy to cross. A transcript with no rejected suggestion usually means the teacher has not yet found the tool's overreach.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Picture yourself preparing next week's vocabulary reading with TeachGen@i. Generate a first draft, then read it as a skeptic of its defaults. Check the reading level against a concrete band rather than a feeling. Scan each example for an assumption about money, travel, or family structure, and replace the ones that exclude. Then watch for the moment the tool offers to take over a judgment: scoring open responses, ranking students, sending messages home. That is the moment to write a rejection into the transcript with a reason tied to professional judgment or district policy.

The finished artifact is not the polished prompt. It is the annotated transcript: the edits with their constraints, the rejection with its reason, and the verification step you will run before any student is affected.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

Read a troubleshooting transcript by asking whether each change is tied to something you could point at: a Lexile band, a standard, an equity assumption, a policy rule. Generic edits ("made it clearer") signal that the troubleshooting has not yet happened. Keep a two-column note as you review: the model's default on the left, the teacher's correction and its reason on the right. A strong transcript lets a colleague see exactly where the model was wrong and where the teacher refused to let it decide.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A troubleshooting transcript meets the standard when a mentor teacher can trace every edit to a named constraint and find at least one principled rejection. Polish on the final prompt is the last concern; the reasoning chain is the artifact. If a turn lacks a stated constraint, annotate it and ask BigAL to compare your transcript against a defensible exemplar rather than to rewrite the prompt for you. A useful closing line reads: "I edited reading level and examples against named constraints, and I rejected auto-grading and parent emails because those judgments belong to me and to district policy, not to the tool."

## Transfer Case

Move the same task to a parent-communication draft instead of a quiz. The troubleshooting pattern transfers: the model will default to a tone and reading level, and it will cheerfully offer to send the message for you. The edits now concern tone, accuracy, and privacy of student detail, and the rejection is letting the tool contact a family without your review. Rebuilding the transcript for this setting, rather than reusing the quiz version, demonstrates that you understand troubleshooting as a transferable discipline.
