# Ethobot Setup and Debrief

![Ethobot Setup and Debrief textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/08-03-ethobot-setup-and-debrief.png)

## Why This Section Matters

An ethics dialogue tool like Ethobot is only as valuable as the debrief that follows it. Students will argue, change their minds, or dig in, and the screen captures the whole transcript, but a transcript is not yet learning evidence. The teaching skill in this section is the debrief: reading a transcript closely enough to locate the exact turn where a student's ethical reasoning moved, and to name the prompt that caused the movement. A debrief that says "good discussion" preserves none of that. A debrief that pins the shift to a turn lets the teacher reuse the move that worked.

This matters because moral reasoning develops through specific provocations, not through general engagement. If you cannot say what reasoning a student held before a turn and after it, you cannot tell whether the activity changed anyone's thinking or merely entertained them. Locating the pivot is what turns a fun activity into documented intellectual growth.

## Opening Case

A high-school class debates with Ethobot about whether the school should use facial recognition for attendance. Afterward the teacher writes: "Great energy, students loved arguing with the bot." That note records enthusiasm but cannot answer the question that matters: did anyone's reasoning actually change, and what changed it? Buried in the transcript, one student moved from "if it stops people sneaking in, just use it" to "facial recognition is too risky for the people it gets wrong." The whole point of the activity lives in that movement, and the celebratory note loses it.

## Core Concept

Debriefing an ethics dialogue is the practice of marking reasoning movement, not measuring participation. A reasoning shift has three locatable parts in a transcript:

- **The starting frame.** Quote the turn where the student states their initial reasoning and name the value it rests on. An efficiency-only frame ("just use it if it works") is different from a rights frame or an error-cost frame.
- **The pivot.** Identify the specific turn, usually a question, where the frame breaks. The most productive pivots ask who bears the cost of being wrong, who is excluded, or what the student would say if they were the affected party.
- **The revised frame.** Quote the turn where the student reasons differently and name the new value that has entered, such as fairness, disparate error cost, or rights.

The pedagogical payoff is that once you can name the pivot, you can reuse it. A question like "who is most likely to be wrongly flagged?" is portable: it can be turned on any classroom tool. Critical edtech studies supplies the disparate-impact lens that makes such pivots possible, since it directs attention to who is misread by a system. The bounded role of the tool also belongs in the debrief: Ethobot can provoke reasoning, but it should not be the authority that declares a student's position right or wrong; that judgment stays with the teacher and the class.

### Annotated judgment call: an Ethobot debrief that marks where reasoning changed

Scenario: a high-school class ran an Ethobot dilemma about whether a school should use facial recognition for attendance. The debrief annotation must mark the exact transcript turn where a student's ethical reasoning shifted, not just say the discussion went well.

Before (debrief that reports tone, not movement):

> Debrief note: Good engagement. Students enjoyed arguing with Ethobot about facial recognition.

Walk the transcript and annotate the reasoning, turn by turn:

1. Turn 4, starting position. Student: "If it stops people sneaking in, just use it." This is a pure efficiency frame with no rights consideration.
2. Turn 7, the pivot. Ethobot asks who is most likely to be misidentified. Student: "Wait, if it works worse on darker skin then some kids get marked absent when they were there." This is the turn where a fairness and false-positive concern enters.
3. Turn 9, revised position. Student: "Maybe attendance by ID card is fine and facial recognition is too risky for the people it gets wrong." The reasoning moved from efficiency-only to a rights-and-error-cost frame.

After (annotated Ethobot debrief):

> Debrief annotation (08.03): Reasoning shift located at turn 7. Before turn 7 the student framed facial recognition as pure efficiency (turn 4). The pivot was the disparate false-positive prompt from Ethobot. After turn 7 the student weighed who bears the error cost and chose the lower-risk option (turn 9). Teaching move next: ask the class to generalize the turn-7 question ("who is most likely to be wrongly flagged?") to one other classroom tool.

The after annotation is stronger because it pins the ethical change to a specific transcript turn and the prompt that caused it, so another teacher can see what reasoning move actually did the work.

## Decision Rule

When debriefing an ethics transcript, locate the reasoning frame first, mark the pivot turn second, and name the transferable question third. Before you treat a debrief as finished, confirm it answers:

1. **Frame check:** What value did the student's reasoning rest on before and after the shift?
2. **Pivot check:** Which specific turn caused the movement, and what made it work?
3. **Transfer check:** What portable question can the class now apply to another tool or dilemma?

## Common Misreadings

The most common error is grading the debrief on warmth: lively discussion is read as successful learning. Energy and reasoning change are different variables, and a class can have abundant energy with no movement at all. A second error is summarizing the conclusion and skipping the pivot, which discards the one reusable thing the activity produced. A third is letting the tool's verdict stand in for the teacher's judgment; Ethobot provokes, but it does not adjudicate whose ethics are correct.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Imagine debriefing a transcript on whether students should be allowed to use AI to draft college essays. Read for the starting frames: some students will argue from convenience, others from fairness to peers who do not have the tool. Find the turn where a question reframes the issue, perhaps "what is the essay supposed to show about you?" Mark it. Then state what the class can carry forward, such as the habit of asking what an assessment is actually meant to measure before deciding whether a tool undermines it.

The artifact is the annotated transcript: starting frame quoted, pivot turn identified with the prompt that caused it, revised frame quoted, and the transferable question named for the next discussion.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

Read a debrief by checking whether you could replay the moment of change from the annotation alone. If the note names a turn number, quotes the before and after, and identifies the provoking question, the reasoning is inspectable. Keep a two-column note while reviewing: the student's frame on the left, the turn and prompt that moved it on the right. A strong debrief lets a colleague adopt your most effective question without having watched the class.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

An Ethobot debrief meets the standard when a mentor teacher can point to the exact turn where reasoning changed and the prompt that did the work. Reporting tone is not enough; the located pivot is the evidence. If the shift is asserted but not pinned to a turn, annotate the gap and ask BigAL to compare your debrief against an exemplar that locates a pivot rather than to summarize the discussion. A useful closing line reads: "I located the reasoning shift at a specific turn and named the question that caused it, and I did not let Ethobot's framing stand in for the class's own judgment."

## Transfer Case

Move the debrief to a younger setting, a 5th-grade dialogue about whether a class points app should rank students publicly. The pivot questions change in vocabulary but not in kind: "how would it feel to be last on the screen every day?" is the age-appropriate version of "who bears the cost?" Annotating where a 5th grader's reasoning moves, rather than reusing the high-school annotation, shows that you can run the debrief practice across developmental levels.
