# Evidence Collection Checklist

![Evidence Collection Checklist textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/cat531-supplement/08-07-evidence-collection-checklist.png)

## Why This Section Matters

Teachers make claims about technology all the time: "the AI tool helped," "engagement went up," "it saved time." Most of these claims are hopes wearing the clothes of evidence. The skill in this section is the discipline of evidence collection: writing claims that another person could check, with a named source, a baseline to compare against, a result, and a statement of what would have changed your mind. An evidence-collection checklist is the instrument that turns a teacher's impression into something a colleague or administrator can audit.

This matters because technology decisions get challenged, and an unverifiable claim collapses under the first hard question. A claim built to be checked survives the question and, more importantly, tells you the truth: sometimes the tool helped only some students, or helped on one dimension and not another. Evidence that can disconfirm your hope is the only kind worth collecting.

## Learning Targets

- Write an auditable evidence-collection checklist row that records the claim, source, baseline, dimensioned result, disconfirmation check, and a verification path a reviewer could follow.
- Distinguish artifact polish from evidence-based revision.
- Document a bounded AI/software support move with accepted and rejected suggestions.
- Use annotations, support requests, and revision notes to make a classroom technology decision more defensible.

## Opening Case

A teacher writes in a checklist: "The AI tool improved student writing." A department chair asks three questions: improved compared to what, on which dimension, and how would you know if it had not? The teacher has no baseline, no specified outcome, and no comparison. The claim, which felt true, turns out to be untestable. The question this section answers is how to rewrite that single row so it names the artifact, the baseline, the measured result, and the way anyone could re-check it.

## Core Concept

An evidence row is auditable when a stranger could locate the artifacts, reconstruct the comparison, and re-run the check. Three properties separate a real evidence row from an opinion dressed as one:

- **A named source.** State which artifact, which class, and which date range, so the evidence can be found. "Improved" with no referent cites nothing.
- **A baseline and comparison.** A claim of improvement implies a before and an after measured the same way. Without a baseline, scored on a common instrument, there is no improvement to speak of, only a feeling.
- **A disconfirmation condition.** Strong evidence states what would have changed your mind and reports whether you looked. Checking whether weaker writers gained less, and finding that they did, bounds the claim honestly instead of overselling it.

A useful structure for an auditable row is: claim, source, baseline, result, disconfirming evidence sought, and how it could be verified by someone else. Reporting separate outcomes matters too; a tool can raise organization scores while leaving argument quality flat, and collapsing those into "improved writing" hides the real finding. [AI and education datafication](https://doi.org/10.1080/17439884.2020.1686017) is a useful caution here: a dashboard number is not automatically evidence, and a metric is only as good as the comparison and the population behind it. Teacher professional judgment supplies the honesty to bound a claim rather than to inflate it.

## Worked Example

Compare a checklist row that is really an opinion with one built to be audited.

### Annotated judgment call: turning a vague claim into a checked evidence row

Scenario: a teacher is filling the evidence-collection checklist that backs a technology decision. A weak row asserts a result; a strong row names the artifact, the source, the date, and how the claim could be verified by someone else.

Before (a checklist row that is really just an opinion):

> Evidence row: The AI tool improved student writing.

Walk the row against what makes evidence auditable:

1. Source check. "Improved" cites nothing. Name the artifact and where it lives (which assignment, which class, which date range).
2. Comparison check. Improvement implies a before and an after. The before row has no baseline, so the claim cannot be tested.
3. Disconfirmation check. Strong evidence says what would have changed your mind. The before row admits no possibility of being wrong.

After (annotated evidence row):

> Evidence row (08.07): Claim = AI revision coach helped on organization, not on argument quality. Source = 22 paired drafts, period 3 English, Oct 6 to Oct 17, stored in the unit folder. Baseline = pre-AI essays scored on the same 4-point organization rubric. Result = organization mean rose 2.4 to 3.1; argument-quality mean flat at 2.6. Disconfirming evidence I looked for = whether weaker writers gained less; they did, so the claim is bounded to mid-range writers. Verifiable by = a colleague re-scoring 5 random pairs blind.

The after row is stronger because another teacher can locate the artifacts, see the baseline and the comparison, and re-check the result, which is the difference between collected evidence and a hopeful claim.

## Decision Rule

When building an evidence row, name the source first, establish the baseline and comparison second, and state the disconfirmation condition third. Before you treat a row as finished, confirm it answers:

1. **Locate check:** Could a stranger find the exact artifacts from your description?
2. **Compare check:** Is there a baseline measured on the same instrument as the result?
3. **Disconfirm check:** What would have changed your mind, did you look, and how could someone re-verify the result?

## Common Misreadings

<inline-check question="A row reports: 'Writing improved: post-AI mean was 3.1 on the rubric.' The most common error is mistaking a number for evidence. A platform dashboard reporting "85 percent engagement" is a metric, not a finding, until you know the baseline and the population it describes. A second error is reporting only the outcome that confirms the hope and folding away the dimension that stayed flat; honest evidence reports the flat result too. A third is omitting the disconfirmation condition, which is what separates evidence from advocacy: a claim that could never be wrong is not evidence.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Suppose you piloted an AI feedback tool in two sections and want to record whether it helped. Name the artifacts: which sections, which assignment, which dates, where the drafts live. Establish the baseline by scoring pre-tool work on the same rubric you will use after. Report the result by dimension, not as a single verdict, so organization and argument are separated. Then state what you checked that could have undercut the claim, such as whether the gains concentrated in students who were already strong, and report what you found. Close with how a colleague could re-score a sample blind to confirm it.

The artifact is the completed row: source, baseline, dimensioned result, disconfirmation sought, and a verification path another teacher could run.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

Read an evidence row by trying to attack it. Ask where the artifacts are, what the comparison is, and what would have falsified the claim. If the row answers all three, it is auditable; if it answers none, it is an opinion. Keep a two-column note: the claim on the left, the source-baseline-disconfirmation that backs it on the right. A strong row lets a skeptic re-check the result without asking you for anything you did not already write down.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

An evidence row meets the standard when a mentor teacher could locate the artifacts, see the baseline, read a dimensioned result, and find a disconfirmation condition. Neat formatting is the last layer; the source, the comparison, and the disconfirmation are load-bearing. If the baseline is missing, annotate the gap and ask BigAL to compare your row against an exemplar that includes a baseline rather than to restate the claim more confidently. A useful closing line reads: "I named the source and baseline and reported the result by dimension, and I rejected the single sentence 'it improved writing' because it had no baseline and admitted no way to be wrong."

## Transfer Case

Move the checklist from an academic outcome to a behavioral one, such as whether an AI nudging tool reduced late assignment submissions. The structure transfers: name the source (which class, which weeks), set a baseline (late-rate before the tool), report the result, and seek disconfirmation (did the drop hold once a holiday week was excluded?). Rebuilding the row for the behavioral claim, rather than reusing the writing example, shows you can apply evidence discipline to any outcome a tool is supposed to affect.
