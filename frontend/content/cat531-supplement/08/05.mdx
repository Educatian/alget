## Core Concept

The core idea in this section is that **Discussion Prompts by Module** should be treated as a relationship among purpose, constraint, evidence, and revision. Purpose names what the learner or audience needs to accomplish. Constraint names what limits the decision. Evidence names the source that makes the decision defensible. Revision shows what changed after the evidence was considered.

Three course anchors shape the reasoning here:

- **TPACK** helps you decide what counts as a meaningful learning or performance demand.
- **critical edtech studies** helps you notice when a design or technology choice creates risk, burden, or unsupported assumptions.
- **AI and education datafication** helps you check whether the artifact works for varied learners, audiences, or use contexts.

The anchors should do work in your artifact. If TPACK or critical edtech studies does not change a decision, it is only a citation rather than evidence of judgment.

### Annotated judgment call: a discussion prompt that opens judgment instead of closing it

Scenario: a facilitator is writing the module-3 discussion prompt for CAT 531. The first draft is a yes-or-no question that yields one-line posts; the revised prompt requires students to weigh a tension and commit to a position with evidence.

Before (closed prompt that produces thin posts):

> Discussion prompt (Module 3): Is AI good or bad for the classroom? Post your opinion.

Walk the prompt against what good discussion judgment requires:

1. Decision check. "Good or bad" has no decision in it; students restate a stance with no constraint to wrestle. A strong prompt names a specific decision a teacher would actually face.
2. Evidence check. The before prompt asks for opinion, so nothing requires students to cite a course anchor or a case detail.
3. Tension check. There is no value conflict to negotiate, so posts do not have to engage anyone else's reasoning.

After (annotated discussion prompt):

> Discussion prompt (Module 3, 08.05): A 4th-grade teacher wants to use an AI reading-level rewriter so every student gets the same article at their own level. Name the value conflict (access versus shared common text), take a position on whether she should use it for next week's lesson, and justify it with one course anchor (TPACK or critical edtech studies) and one detail from the case. Then reply to a peer who chose the opposite call. Facilitator note: I rejected the auto-suggested follow-up "What does everyone think?" because it reopens opinion instead of pressing on the tension.

The after prompt is stronger because it forces a concrete decision, names a real tension to negotiate, requires evidence, and the facilitator annotation shows one suggested move that was deliberately rejected.

## Decision Rule

Use the classroom value conflict first, the technology affordance second, and the policy boundary third. A tool choice is justified only when it protects students while improving a specific learning activity.

Use the following check when the artifact looks finished but the reasoning may still be hidden:

1. **Purpose check:** What should the audience be able to do after reading or using the artifact?
2. **Evidence check:** Which exact source, observation, annotation, rubric line, data pattern, or policy boundary justifies the revision?
3. **Revision check:** What changed in the artifact, and what suggestion did you reject or modify?

If you cannot answer all three questions, the artifact may be complete, but it is not yet ready as evidence of learning.

Then ask what problem the artifact is supposed to solve inside facilitation readiness. Next, connect the artifact to the course anchors: **TPACK**, **critical edtech studies**, **teacher professional judgment**. The point is not to cite every anchor. The point is to let one anchor change the artifact. If the anchor does not change wording, layout, evidence, timing, or feedback, it has not yet become design reasoning.

Finally, inspect the revision trail. A weak submission says the artifact was improved. A strong submission shows the earlier version, the issue noticed, the support requested, the decision accepted or rejected, and the reason the final version is better for the intended learner or audience.
