## Why This Section Matters

A discussion prompt is an instructional instrument, and most online discussions are weak because the prompt was weak. Ask "is AI good or bad for the classroom?" and you get a wall of one-line opinions that never touch each other. The skill in this section is designing prompts that force a concrete decision, name a genuine tension to negotiate, require evidence, and oblige students to engage a peer who chose differently. A well-built prompt does the pedagogical work before anyone posts; a poorly built one cannot be rescued by good facilitation.

This matters because discussion is where teacher candidates rehearse the judgment the course is trying to build. If the prompt lets them restate a stance, they practice nothing. If the prompt makes them weigh competing goods and commit with evidence, they practice exactly the defensible reasoning they will need when they make real technology decisions in their own classrooms.

## Opening Case

A facilitator posts the Module 3 prompt: "Is AI good or bad for the classroom? Post your opinion." The board fills with thirty posts that agree with each other and cite nothing. No one has to choose, no one has to weigh a cost, and no one has to respond to a classmate. The discussion looks active and teaches little. The question this section answers is what to change in the prompt itself so the same students produce reasoned, evidence-backed, mutually engaged argument.

## Core Concept

A productive discussion prompt is built from four design components, and a prompt that omits any of them tends to collapse into opinion exchange:

- **A concrete decision.** Replace the abstract valence question ("good or bad?") with a specific choice a teacher would actually face, attached to a named person and situation. A decision creates stakes that an opinion does not.
- **A named tension.** State the two competing goods the student must negotiate, such as access versus a shared common text. Without a tension there is nothing to deliberate, only something to assert.
- **An evidence requirement.** Oblige students to cite at least one course anchor and one detail from the case, so posts rest on reasoning rather than gut feeling.
- **A reply obligation that engages disagreement.** Require a response to a peer who made the opposite call, which forces students to take other reasoning seriously rather than talk past it.

The facilitator's own judgment shows in what gets cut as much as in what gets added. A generic follow-up like "what does everyone think?" reopens opinion and undoes the tension the prompt worked to create, so a strong design records that such a move was deliberately rejected. Critical edtech studies, the line of work associated with Neil Selwyn, helps you choose tensions that are real (often involving who is burdened or excluded), and [TPACK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_pedagogical_content_knowledge) keeps the decision tied to genuine content and pedagogy rather than to novelty.

## Worked Example

Compare a closed prompt that produces thin posts with a designed prompt that produces deliberation.

### Annotated judgment call: a discussion prompt that opens judgment instead of closing it

Scenario: a facilitator is writing the module-3 discussion prompt for CAT 531. The first draft is a yes-or-no question that yields one-line posts; the revised prompt requires students to weigh a tension and commit to a position with evidence.

Before (closed prompt that produces thin posts):

> Discussion prompt (Module 3): Is AI good or bad for the classroom? Post your opinion.

Walk the prompt against what good discussion judgment requires:

1. Decision check. "Good or bad" has no decision in it; students restate a stance with no constraint to wrestle. A strong prompt names a specific decision a teacher would actually face.
2. Evidence check. The before prompt asks for opinion, so nothing requires students to cite a course anchor or a case detail.
3. Tension check. There is no value conflict to negotiate, so posts do not have to engage anyone else's reasoning.

After (annotated discussion prompt):

> Discussion prompt (Module 3, 08.05): A 4th-grade teacher wants to use an AI reading-level rewriter so every student gets the same article at their own level. Name the value conflict (access versus shared common text), take a position on whether she should use it for next week's lesson, and justify it with one course anchor ([TPACK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_pedagogical_content_knowledge) or critical edtech studies) and one detail from the case. Then reply to a peer who chose the opposite call. Facilitator note: I rejected the auto-suggested follow-up "What does everyone think?" because it reopens opinion instead of pressing on the tension.

The after prompt is stronger because it forces a concrete decision, names a real tension to negotiate, requires evidence, and the facilitator annotation shows one suggested move that was deliberately rejected.

## Decision Rule

When designing a discussion prompt, build in the concrete decision first, the named tension second, and the evidence-and-reply requirement third. Before you post a prompt, confirm it answers:

1. **Decision check:** Does the prompt require a specific choice a teacher would actually face, not a stance on an abstraction?
2. **Tension check:** Are two competing goods named so students have something genuine to negotiate?
3. **Evidence-and-engagement check:** Must students cite an anchor and a case detail, and must they respond to a peer who disagreed?

## Common Misreadings

The most common error is judging a prompt by the volume of posts it generates. A bad prompt can produce many shallow posts; the measure is whether students had to reason and engage each other, not how full the board looks. A second error is adding a warm, open follow-up to "encourage participation," which usually dissolves the tension and returns the thread to opinion. A third is naming a fake tension, two options that are not really in conflict, which lets students pick the obvious side without deliberation.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Suppose you are writing the Module 5 prompt on data privacy. Resist "should schools protect student data?" because everyone agrees. Instead give a decision: a teacher can adopt a free analytics dashboard that improves feedback but shares anonymized clickstream data with the vendor. Name the tension as better feedback versus student data exposure. Require one anchor and one case detail. Require a reply to someone who chose the opposite. Then check your draft for any sentence that lets students escape into general opinion, and cut it the way the facilitator cut "what does everyone think?"

The artifact is the designed prompt plus a short facilitator note recording at least one move you rejected and why.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

Read a discussion prompt by predicting the posts it will produce. If you can imagine a satisfying post that cites nothing and engages no one, the prompt is too open. Check that the decision is concrete, the tension is real, and the reply obligation forces engagement with disagreement. Keep a two-column note: the design component on the left, the exact sentence in the prompt that delivers it on the right. A strong prompt has a sentence for each component and no sentence that reopens mere opinion.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A discussion prompt meets the standard when a mentor teacher can see the concrete decision, the named tension, the evidence requirement, and a recorded rejected move. Wording polish is the last layer; the design components are what carry the prompt. If a component is missing, annotate the gap and ask BigAL to compare your prompt against an exemplar that forces deliberation rather than to make it sound more inviting. A useful closing line reads: "I built in a concrete decision and a real tension with an evidence and reply requirement, and I rejected the open follow-up because it would have returned the thread to opinion."

## Transfer Case

Move the prompt design to a synchronous, in-class debate rather than an asynchronous board. The components transfer: a concrete decision, a real tension, an evidence requirement. The reply obligation becomes a structured rebuttal round, and the rejected move might be an open "any other thoughts?" that lets the energy dissipate. Rebuilding the prompt for the live format, rather than pasting the board version into a slide, shows that you understand prompt design as a transferable instrument for deliberation.
