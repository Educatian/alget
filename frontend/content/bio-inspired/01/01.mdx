# Cellular Solids

> **"The properties of a cellular solid depend on the properties of the material of which it is made and on its relative density. Of these, relative density is the more important."**
> - Lorna Gibson and Michael Ashby, *Cellular Solids: Structure and Properties*

---

## Opening: The Toucan That Should Not Be Able to Fly

A toco toucan carries a beak that is roughly one third of its total body length, yet that beak adds only about one twentieth of the bird's mass. If the beak were solid keratin or solid bone, the bird would be hopelessly nose-heavy and could not stay aloft. Evolution solved a sharp engineering problem here: build a large, stiff, impact-tolerant structure while spending almost no mass on it.

The solution is not a clever outer shape. It is what fills the inside. The toucan beak is a thin keratin shell wrapped around a sparse three-dimensional network of bony struts and membranes, an internal foam that occupies a large volume with very little solid. That same idea, **put material only where the load path needs it and let air do the rest**, is the design strategy behind cancellous bone, wood, cork, and the bee's honeycomb. It is also the strategy aerospace engineers copy when they build sandwich panels and additively manufactured lattices.

This section treats that family of structures, the **cellular solids**, and the single governing parameter that controls their mechanics: relative density.

> **The Big Picture:** A cellular solid lets an engineer dial stiffness, strength, and energy absorption up or down by changing geometry rather than changing material. Understanding how properties scale with relative density is what turns "make it lighter" into a quantitative design move.

## What Is a Cellular Solid?

A cellular solid is an assembly of cells (polygons or polyhedra) with solid edges or faces, packed so they fill space. The solid is concentrated in thin walls and struts; the rest is void. Two geometric families dominate:

| Type | Geometry | Mechanical Character |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2D Honeycomb | Prismatic cells (extruded hexagons) | Strongly anisotropic; stiff along the prism axis, soft in-plane |
| 3D Foam (open or closed cell) | Interconnected struts (open) or plates (closed) | Approximately isotropic; similar response in all directions |

The distinction matters in design. A honeycomb is the right choice when the load direction is known and fixed (the core of a flat panel loaded in bending). A foam is the right choice when impacts can come from any direction (a crash layer).

## Biologically Inspired Design as a Search Process

One of the central messages of *Biologically Inspired Design: Computational Methods and Tools* is that biomimicry becomes scalable only when it is treated as a repeatable search-and-transfer process rather than a collection of inspiring anecdotes.

For cellular solids, that means the stronger design question is not "What natural object looks like my part?" It is "What biological systems solve lightweight load support, impact absorption, or distributed stiffness with minimal material?" That shift changes the whole process: the engineer searches by **function and constraint**, not by visual resemblance.

### A Function-Based Search Workflow

The book describes tools such as AskNature, engineering-to-biology thesauri, and function-based biologically inspired design. A practical classroom version of that workflow looks like this:

1. **State the engineering function clearly.** Example: support compressive load with low mass.
2. **Translate the function into biological search terms.** Example: lightweight support, crush resistance, distributed load, energy absorption.
3. **Search biology for analogous mechanisms.** Honeycomb, cancellous bone, cork, and beaks become relevant because they solve related structural functions.
4. **Extract the design principle.** Place material only where the load path needs it.
5. **Transfer that principle into engineering form.** Lattice infills, sandwich-panel cores, crash foams, and additively manufactured cellular structures.

| Workflow Step | Cellular-Solids Version |
| --- | --- |
| Engineering Problem | Support load with less mass |
| Biological Strategy | Cellular architecture in bone, wood, and honeycomb |
| Transfer Rule | Put material along load paths instead of filling all volume |
| Engineering Check | Verify stiffness, buckling resistance, and manufacturability |

This is what keeps biomimicry from becoming superficial. The goal is not to borrow a shape because it looks natural; the goal is to transfer a function-structure relationship.

## The Biological Mechanism, Quantitatively

Nature integrates cellular structures everywhere, from the macroscopic down to the microscopic level. In each case the organism is managing the same trade-off between mass and mechanical performance.

- **Cancellous (Spongy) Bone:** Found at the ends of long bones (such as the human femur) and inside vertebrae. Its porous trabecular network has a relative density of roughly $0.1$ to $0.3$, meaning 70 to 90 percent of the volume is marrow-filled void. Crucially, the trabeculae actively remodel along lines of principal stress (Wolff's Law), so the solid sits exactly where the load path runs. This absorbs impacts and resists shear fracture while keeping the skeleton light enough for agile movement.
- **Wood and Cork:** Xylem cells in trees form a micro-tubular honeycomb, typically a relative density near $0.15$ for a light softwood. Because the cells are aligned with the trunk, wood is stiff along the grain (the load-bearing direction) and far softer across it, the same anisotropy a manufactured honeycomb shows. Cork's closed cells let it compress into a bottle neck and seal without expanding laterally, because its Poisson behavior is nearly zero.
- **The Bee's Honeycomb:** The hexagonal geometry uses the minimum wall length to enclose a given cell area. The Honeycomb Conjecture, proven by Thomas Hales in 1999, confirms that a hexagonal tiling beats squares and triangles in perimeter-to-area efficiency, so the bee spends the least possible wax per unit of stored honey.
- **Bird Beaks:** The toucan beak from the opening is a closed keratin shell over a bony foam. The shell carries bending and the foam stabilizes the shell against local buckling, exactly the sandwich-panel logic engineers use in aircraft skins.

> **Reflect:** Look around your immediate environment. Can you spot any man-made objects that might be secretly using a cellular solid structure inside to save weight (for example, the inside of a flat-pack desk panel, or the sole of your running shoe)?

## The Governing Parameter: Relative Density and the Gibson-Ashby Laws

The mechanical properties of any cellular solid are governed less by the base material than by one metric, the **relative density**:

$$ \bar{\rho} = \frac{\rho^*}{\rho_s} $$

Here $\rho^*$ is the density of the overall cellular solid (including the air) and $\rho_s$ is the density of the pure solid base material. The void fraction (porosity) is simply $1 - \bar{\rho}$.

Gibson and Ashby showed that the deformation mechanism of the cell walls fixes how each property scales with $\bar{\rho}$. For an **open-cell foam**, the struts bend before they stretch, and that bending mechanism gives:

$$ \frac{E^*}{E_s} \approx C_1\,\bar{\rho}^{\,2}, \qquad C_1 \approx 1 $$

$$ \frac{\sigma_{pl}^*}{\sigma_{ys}} \approx C_2\,\bar{\rho}^{\,3/2}, \qquad C_2 \approx 0.3 $$

The first relation gives the relative Young's modulus (stiffness); the second gives the relative plastic collapse stress (strength), where $\sigma_{ys}$ is the yield strength of the solid. The **squared** stiffness law is the headline: halve the relative density and stiffness falls to $0.5^2 = 0.25$, one quarter of its former value. Strength, scaling with the $3/2$ power, falls more gently.

A second deep point is that geometry, not just density, controls direction-dependence. A **regular hexagonal honeycomb** has relative density $\bar{\rho} \approx \tfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\,(t/\ell)$, where $t$ is wall thickness and $\ell$ is wall length. Loaded **along the prism axis** the walls stretch, so stiffness scales linearly, $E_3^*/E_s = \bar{\rho}$. Loaded **in-plane** the walls bend, so stiffness scales with the cube, $E_1^*/E_s \approx 2.3\,(t/\ell)^3$. That difference is the quantitative reason honeycombs are called anisotropic, as the next worked example makes concrete.

## Worked Example 1.1: Honeycomb Anisotropy

**Problem:** A regular hexagonal aluminum honeycomb has wall thickness-to-length ratio $t/\ell = 0.10$. Find its relative density, and compare its stiffness loaded along the prism axis versus loaded in-plane. By what factor is it stiffer in the strong direction?

**Step 1: Relative density.**

$$ \bar{\rho} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\left(\frac{t}{\ell}\right) = 1.155 \times 0.10 = 0.1155 $$

So the honeycomb is about 88 percent air.

**Step 2: Out-of-plane (axis) stiffness.** Walls stretch, scaling is linear:

$$ \frac{E_3^*}{E_s} = \bar{\rho} = 0.1155 $$

**Step 3: In-plane stiffness.** Walls bend, scaling is cubic:

$$ \frac{E_1^*}{E_s} = 2.3\left(\frac{t}{\ell}\right)^3 = 2.3 \times (0.10)^3 = 2.3 \times 10^{-3} = 0.0023 $$

**Step 4: Anisotropy ratio.**

$$ \frac{E_3^*}{E_1^*} = \frac{0.1155}{0.0023} = \boxed{50.2} $$

The honeycomb is roughly **50 times stiffer** along its prism axis than across it, from one geometry, at a single relative density. This is exactly why a sandwich panel is built with the honeycomb axis running through the thickness: the stiff direction carries the through-thickness shear that the bending of the panel demands.

> **Think About It:** Drop $t/\ell$ to $0.05$ and rerun the arithmetic: the ratio climbs to about $200$. Thinner walls make the honeycomb lighter but far more direction-sensitive, so misaligning the load becomes catastrophic.

## Engineering Translation: Aerospace Sandwich Panels

Why do materials and aerospace engineers obsess over biological foams? Because the design currencies are **specific stiffness** (stiffness per unit mass) and **specific strength** (strength per unit mass).

Instead of a solid block of aluminum for a wing skin or fuselage section, engineers biomimic skull bone: sandwich a light, thick cellular core (aluminum or Nomex honeycomb) between two thin, high-strength skins (carbon fiber or titanium). Separating the skins drives up the second moment of area $I$, and bending stiffness scales with $I$, so the panel gains enormous bending stiffness for almost no added mass. Less mass means less fuel and cheaper payload to orbit.

### Why This Example Matters Methodologically

The sandwich panel reveals the deeper lesson of the book: successful bio-inspired design transfers an **abstract principle**, not a literal biological form. Aircraft wings are not shaped like bone. Designers abstract a rule:

- separate load-bearing skins with a lightweight core,
- increase bending efficiency through geometry,
- and use internal architecture to stabilize the section without filling it solid.

That move from organism to transferable design rule is what makes biology usable as an engineering knowledge base rather than just a source of inspiration.

## Worked Example 1.2: Drone Landing Gear Redesign

**Problem:** A drone landing gear made of solid polymer weighs **500 g**. Inspired by the trabecular structure of an eagle's femur, you redesign it with an internal Voronoi lattice while keeping the exact same outer volume. The solid polymer density is $\rho_s = 1.2\ \text{g/cm}^3$; the new lattice part has overall density $\rho^* = 0.36\ \text{g/cm}^3$. Find the relative density, the mass saved, and the resulting stiffness penalty.

**Step 1: Relative density.**

$$ \bar{\rho} = \frac{\rho^*}{\rho_s} = \frac{0.36}{1.2} = \boxed{0.30} $$

The new structure is 70 percent empty air space.

**Step 2: Mass savings.** Outer volume is unchanged, so mass scales directly with density:

$$ m^* = 500\ \text{g} \times 0.30 = 150\ \text{g} \;\Rightarrow\; \Delta m = 500 - 150 = \boxed{350\ \text{g saved}} $$

**Step 3: Stiffness penalty (open-cell scaling).** Using $E^*/E_s \approx \bar{\rho}^{\,2}$:

$$ \frac{E^*}{E_s} = (0.30)^2 = \boxed{0.09} $$

The lattice keeps only 9 percent of the solid's bulk stiffness. That sounds severe, but note the honest comparison: **specific** stiffness is $\frac{E^*/\rho^*}{E_s/\rho_s} = \frac{\bar{\rho}^2}{\bar{\rho}} = \bar{\rho} = 0.30$, lower than solid for pure axial stiffness. The lattice wins not on axial stiffness per kilogram but on **bending stiffness** (where pushing material outward raises $I$) and on **energy absorption** during crush. 

**Interpretation:** Saving 350 g on a delivery drone is a large gain in battery range, and the gear still survives hard landings because an open-cell lattice fails gracefully by progressive strut buckling rather than shattering. The trade is deliberate: you accept reduced axial stiffness to buy mass savings and controlled energy absorption.

## Summary: Key Principles

### Key Equations

| Concept | Equation |
| --- | --- |
| Relative density | $\bar{\rho} = \rho^*/\rho_s$ |
| Porosity | $1 - \bar{\rho}$ |
| Open-cell foam stiffness | $E^*/E_s \approx \bar{\rho}^{\,2}$ |
| Open-cell foam strength | $\sigma_{pl}^*/\sigma_{ys} \approx 0.3\,\bar{\rho}^{\,3/2}$ |
| Honeycomb (axis / in-plane) | $E_3^*/E_s = \bar{\rho}$; $\;E_1^*/E_s \approx 2.3(t/\ell)^3$ |

### Key Takeaways

- A cellular solid concentrates material in thin walls and struts and fills the rest with void.
- Relative density is the master variable; geometry decides how much of the base material's performance you keep.
- Stiffness of open-cell foams scales with the **square** of relative density, so light foams lose stiffness fast but strength more slowly.
- Honeycombs are strongly anisotropic: stiff (linear) along the prism axis, soft (cubic) in-plane.
- Good bio-inspired design transfers the **function-structure principle**, not the literal organism shape.
