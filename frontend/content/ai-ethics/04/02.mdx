## Why "I Agree" Is Not Consent in Most AI Deployments

Modern interfaces collect consent millions of times per day through a single click. The medical-research, legal, and ethics literatures developed the concept of **informed consent** over decades, anchored on four conditions: disclosure, comprehension, voluntariness, and competence. A click on "I Agree" satisfies disclosure on a technicality and almost nothing else.

This module covers what informed consent requires, how it breaks under algorithmic conditions, the dark-pattern designs that exploit the breakage, and what a defensible consent flow looks like for AI systems — drawing on the Belmont Report, GDPR, FTC guidance, the Norwegian *Deceived by Design* report, and Brignull's dark-pattern taxonomy.

---

## The Four Conditions of Informed Consent

Informed consent in research ethics (Belmont Report, 1979) requires:

**Disclosure.** The subject is told what will happen and what risks attend it, in terms they can understand.

**Comprehension.** The subject actually understands the disclosure. The bar is comprehension, not exposure.

**Voluntariness.** The subject can refuse or withdraw without penalty. Coercion or undue influence invalidates consent.

**Competence.** The subject has the capacity to make the decision. Children, people in crisis, and people under impairment have reduced or absent competence and need surrogate decision-makers.

These four conditions evolved for face-to-face medical research where a clinician could check each one. AI deployments break each condition in characteristic ways.

---

## How Each Condition Breaks Under AI

**Disclosure breaks** when the system's behavior is too complex to summarize honestly in advance. A recommender system's actual data uses depend on emergent profiles built over months. No upfront disclosure can list them.

**Comprehension breaks** when the disclosure is technically accurate but practically opaque. Privacy policies routinely require college-graduate reading levels and run thousands of words. Most users do not read them, and those who do often cannot translate the prose into operational understanding.

**Voluntariness breaks** when refusing the AI tool means losing access to the underlying service. A student who refuses the LMS does not get a fair grade. A patient who refuses the AI triage does not get faster care. Schools, employers, and platforms exploit this by making consent legally voluntary but materially coerced.

**Competence breaks** when the user is a minor, in distress, in a high-pressure decision context, or otherwise cognitively constrained. COPPA addresses one slice of this (under-13 commercial collection); the rest is largely unregulated.

When all four conditions are weakened simultaneously — as in most consumer AI deployments — the consent collected is legally documented but ethically thin.

## Dark Patterns: When Design Actively Defeats Consent

Harry Brignull catalogued the *dark patterns* that interfaces use to nudge users away from informed choices. Several map directly to AI consent flows.

**Pre-ticked checkboxes** for data sharing. GDPR explicitly prohibits these for opt-in consent, but they persist outside EU jurisdictions.

**Asymmetric prominence**: the "Accept All" button is large and brightly colored; "Reject All" requires three clicks through a settings menu.

**Confirmshaming**: "No thanks, I don't want better recommendations." Framing refusal as self-harm pressures users toward consent.

**Misdirection**: prominent privacy promises while consequential settings are buried.

**Roach motel**: easy to opt in, hard to opt out. Account deletion that requires a phone call to a department open four hours per day is the canonical example.

**Forced action**: tying access to a core feature to consent for unrelated data uses, in violation of GDPR's purpose-limitation principle.

The Norwegian Consumer Council's *Deceived by Design* (2018) documented these patterns at major platforms. The EU Digital Services Act (2024) explicitly prohibits dark patterns. The FTC has used Section 5 enforcement against several US deployments.

## Special Cases Where Consent Cannot Carry the Weight

Consent is the wrong primary tool in three contexts.

**Public goods and population-level decisions.** Predictive policing, public-health surveillance, and infrastructure analytics affect populations. Individual consent, even if perfectly executed, does not address the distributional question. These systems require democratic deliberation, not aggregated clickthroughs.

**Deeply asymmetric power.** A job applicant "consenting" to a hiring algorithm has no real alternative. A patient "consenting" to a hospital's triage system does not negotiate the deployment. Consent here is real but insufficient; due-process protections must supplement it.

**Children and developmentally vulnerable users.** Capacity to consent develops over time. Surrogate consent (parents, guardians) and special design protections are required. COPPA codifies one floor; ethical practice extends further.

In each case, treating consent as the primary ethics infrastructure misroutes the problem. The right move is to limit deployment scope, build in oversight, and treat consent as one layer of many.

---

## Designing a Defensible Consent Flow

Five practices distinguish defensible consent flows.

**Layered notice.** A short, plain-language summary of the most consequential uses, with progressive disclosure for users who want detail. The Norwegian model regulators have adopted this as a minimum.

**Just-in-time disclosure.** Consent is requested at the moment the data is needed for a specific purpose, not bundled at signup.

**Symmetric controls.** Accept and refuse are equally prominent, equally costly to choose.

**Withdrawal that is real.** "Delete my account" actually deletes the data, including from training sets where feasible. CCPA right to deletion and GDPR right to erasure operationalize this; current generative-model architectures struggle to satisfy it cleanly.

**Surrogate and granular consent for vulnerable users.** Parental consent for minors, separate consent for sensitive uses (biometrics, location), and the right to use the service with reduced features rather than refused service.

A consent flow that meets these five conditions clears the legal floor and most of the ethical floor. It is rarely the cheapest design choice, which is why dark patterns proliferate in deployments where regulatory pressure is weak.
