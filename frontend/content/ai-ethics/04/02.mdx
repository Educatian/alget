# Module 2: Consent in Algorithmic Contexts

> **"Consent is not a moment; it is a relationship that the design either honors or undermines."**

---

## Opening: The Click That Cost 5 Billion Dollars

In 2018, the Cambridge Analytica disclosures revealed that data from roughly 87 million Facebook users had been harvested through a personality-quiz app and repurposed for political profiling. The users of that app had clicked "I Agree." Many of the 87 million had not even touched the app; their data was pulled through friend connections under permissions that the platform later restricted. In 2019, the U.S. Federal Trade Commission imposed a 5 billion dollar penalty on Facebook, the largest privacy fine in FTC history at the time, finding that the company had deceived users about their ability to control their personal information.

The lesson is not that the consent screens were missing. The lesson is that they were present, legally documented, clicked, and ethically empty. Modern interfaces collect consent millions of times per day through a single click. The medical-research, legal, and ethics literatures developed the concept of **informed consent** over decades, anchored on four conditions: disclosure, comprehension, voluntariness, and competence. A click on "I Agree" satisfies disclosure on a technicality and almost nothing else.

This module covers what informed consent requires, how it breaks under algorithmic conditions, the dark-pattern designs that exploit the breakage, and what a defensible consent flow looks like for AI systems, drawing on the Belmont Report, GDPR, FTC guidance, the Norwegian *Deceived by Design* report, and Brignull's dark-pattern taxonomy.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this section, you will be able to:

## The Four Conditions of Informed Consent

Informed consent in research ethics (Belmont Report, 1979) requires four conditions to hold together. Weaken any one and the consent loses its moral force, even if the paperwork is perfect.

| Condition | What It Demands | The Test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Disclosure | The subject is told what will happen and what risks attend it | Is the relevant information actually presented? |
| Comprehension | The subject actually understands the disclosure | Could the subject explain the consequences in their own words? |
| Voluntariness | The subject can refuse or withdraw without penalty | Is there a real, non-punishing alternative to saying yes? |
| Competence | The subject has the capacity to make the decision | Is the subject an autonomous adult, unimpaired and uncoerced? |

**Disclosure** sets a floor: information must be presented in terms the subject can understand, not buried. **Comprehension** raises the bar from exposure to understanding; showing someone a document is not the same as their grasping it. **Voluntariness** protects against coercion and undue influence; a yes extracted under threat is not consent. **Competence** recognizes that children, people in crisis, and people under impairment have reduced or absent capacity and need surrogate decision-makers.

These four conditions evolved for face-to-face medical research where a clinician could check each one in person. AI deployments break each condition in characteristic ways.

## How Each Condition Breaks Under AI

**Disclosure breaks** when the system's behavior is too complex to summarize honestly in advance. A recommender system's actual data uses depend on emergent profiles built over months. No upfront disclosure can list them, because the uses are not known at signup time.

**Comprehension breaks** when the disclosure is technically accurate but practically opaque. Privacy policies routinely require college-graduate reading levels and run thousands of words; one widely cited 2008 estimate (McDonald and Cranor) put the time to read every policy a typical user encounters in a year at roughly 200 hours. Most users do not read them, and those who do often cannot translate the prose into operational understanding.

**Voluntariness breaks** when refusing the AI tool means losing access to the underlying service. A student who refuses the LMS does not get a fair grade. A patient who refuses the AI triage does not get faster care. Schools, employers, and platforms exploit this by making consent legally voluntary but materially coerced.

**Competence breaks** when the user is a minor, in distress, in a high-pressure decision context, or otherwise cognitively constrained. COPPA addresses one slice of this (under-13 commercial collection); the rest is largely unregulated.

When all four conditions are weakened simultaneously, as in most consumer AI deployments, the consent collected is legally documented but ethically thin.

## Dark Patterns: When Design Actively Defeats Consent

Harry Brignull catalogued the *dark patterns* that interfaces use to nudge users away from informed choices. Several map directly to AI consent flows.

**Pre-ticked checkboxes** for data sharing. GDPR explicitly prohibits these for opt-in consent (consent must be a clear affirmative act), but they persist outside EU jurisdictions.

**Asymmetric prominence**: the "Accept All" button is large and brightly colored; "Reject All" requires three clicks through a settings menu.

**Confirmshaming**: "No thanks, I don't want better recommendations." Framing refusal as self-harm pressures users toward consent.

**Misdirection**: prominent privacy promises while consequential settings are buried.

**Roach motel**: easy to opt in, hard to opt out. Account deletion that requires a phone call to a department open four hours per day is the canonical example.

**Forced action**: tying access to a core feature to consent for unrelated data uses, in violation of GDPR's purpose-limitation principle.

## Worked Case Analysis: The District Learning Platform

Apply the framework to a concrete scenario. A school district adopts a learning platform whose terms of service grant the vendor rights to use student work for "product improvement," which includes training the vendor's AI models. The district requires every student to use the platform to submit assignments. Students click "I Agree" at first login.

**Step 1: Walk the four conditions.**

| Condition | Status here | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Disclosure | Partial | "Product improvement" is vague; model-training use is not named in plain terms. |
| Comprehension | Failed | Terms are written at a graduate reading level for a population of minors. |
| Voluntariness | Failed | Refusing the platform means not submitting required coursework, so refusal is penalized. |
| Competence | Failed | The users are minors; capacity to consent to data uses is not yet developed. |

Three of four conditions fail outright and the fourth is only partial. The click is documented consent but carries almost no ethical weight.

**Step 2: Map the stakeholders and their interests.**

| Stakeholder | Primary interest | Risk exposure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Student | Fair grade, privacy of schoolwork | Work absorbed into training data; no opt-out |
| Parent or guardian | Protect the child, retain a say | Surrogate consent bypassed by direct click |
| District | Functioning tool, legal cover | Liability if consent is later ruled invalid |
| Vendor | Training data, low friction | Reputational and regulatory exposure |

**Step 3: Identify the dark patterns at work.** The bundling of model-training consent with required-coursework access is **forced action**. Presenting one "I Agree" gate with no granular options is **asymmetric prominence** in its starkest form (refusal is not even offered). The vague "product improvement" wording is **misdirection**.

**Step 4: Decide the redesign.**

| Option | Meets ethics floor? | Tradeoff |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Keep single "I Agree" gate | No | Cheapest; legally fragile; ethically empty |
| Add parental surrogate consent + granular opt-out for training use | Yes | Vendor loses some training data; district adds workflow |
| Strip training rights from the contract entirely | Yes (strongest) | Vendor may raise price or refuse terms |

The defensible decision is the middle option at minimum: separate the required-use consent from the optional model-training consent, route the training-use decision through a parent or guardian, name the use in plain language, and let students use the platform fully even if training use is declined. This removes the forced-action coupling and restores voluntariness and surrogate competence.

## Special Cases Where Consent Cannot Carry the Weight

Even a well-designed consent flow is the wrong primary tool in three contexts.

**Public goods and population-level decisions.** Predictive policing, public-health surveillance, and infrastructure analytics affect populations. Individual consent, even if perfectly executed, does not address the distributional question. These systems require democratic deliberation, not aggregated clickthroughs.

**Deeply asymmetric power.** A job applicant "consenting" to a hiring algorithm has no real alternative. A patient "consenting" to a hospital's triage system does not negotiate the deployment. Consent here is real but insufficient; due-process protections must supplement it.

**Children and developmentally vulnerable users.** Capacity to consent develops over time. Surrogate consent (parents, guardians) and special design protections are required. COPPA codifies one floor; ethical practice extends further.

In each case, treating consent as the primary ethics infrastructure misroutes the problem. The right move is to limit deployment scope, build in oversight, and treat consent as one layer of many.

## Designing a Defensible Consent Flow

Five practices distinguish defensible consent flows.

**Layered notice.** A short, plain-language summary of the most consequential uses, with progressive disclosure for users who want detail. European regulators have adopted layered notice as a minimum expectation.

**Just-in-time disclosure.** Consent is requested at the moment the data is needed for a specific purpose, not bundled at signup.

**Symmetric controls.** Accept and refuse are equally prominent and equally costly to choose.

**Withdrawal that is real.** "Delete my account" actually deletes the data, including from training sets where feasible. CCPA right to deletion and GDPR right to erasure operationalize this; current generative-model architectures struggle to satisfy it cleanly because data already absorbed into model weights is hard to extract.

**Surrogate and granular consent for vulnerable users.** Parental consent for minors, separate consent for sensitive uses (biometrics, location), and the right to use the service with reduced features rather than refused service.

## Counterarguments and Limitations

A fair treatment has to engage the objections.

**"Consent fatigue makes all of this counterproductive."** Critics note that more consent prompts can worsen outcomes; users habituate and click through everything. This is a real finding, but it argues for *fewer, better-targeted* prompts (just-in-time, layered), not for abandoning consent. The fatigue is a symptom of bundling, not of consent itself.

**"Strong consent will break free services."** Some argue that ethical consent flows make ad-supported and data-supported products unviable. The honest reply is that some business models depend on consent being thin; the GDPR and DSA reflect a democratic judgment that those models must change rather than that the protections must yield.

**"Comprehension is an impossible standard."** No layperson fully understands a deep model's data flows, so perfect comprehension is unattainable. True, which is exactly why consent should not be the only safeguard. The remedy is to pair achievable comprehension (of consequences, not internals) with structural limits on deployment, as in the special-case contexts above.

## Summary

### Key Takeaways

- Informed consent rests on four conditions: disclosure, comprehension, voluntariness, and competence. Weakening any one drains the consent of moral force.
- AI deployments break all four in characteristic ways, so a clicked "I Agree" is often legally documented but ethically thin.
- Dark patterns (asymmetric prominence, confirmshaming, roach motel, forced action, misdirection, pre-ticked boxes) are deliberate designs that defeat voluntariness; GDPR, the DSA, and FTC enforcement target them.
- Consent cannot carry the weight alone for public goods, asymmetric-power, or vulnerable-user contexts; those need deliberation, due process, and surrogate protections.
- A defensible flow uses layered notice, just-in-time disclosure, symmetric controls, real withdrawal, and surrogate or granular consent.
