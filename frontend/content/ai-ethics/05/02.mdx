# AI Governance Frameworks: NIST, EU AI Act, UNESCO

> **"The question is no longer whether to govern AI, but whose rules apply when your model crosses a border."**

---

## Opening: When the Absence of a Framework Became a Scandal

Between 2013 and 2019, the Dutch tax authority used a self-learning risk-scoring algorithm to flag families suspected of childcare-benefit fraud. The system disproportionately flagged people with dual nationality and low incomes, and the flags triggered aggressive clawbacks that pushed an estimated 26,000 families into financial ruin. The political fallout, known as the *toeslagenaffaire* (childcare benefits affair), brought down the entire Dutch government in January 2021. The Dutch Data Protection Authority later fined the tax administration for unlawful and discriminatory processing.

The striking detail for a governance student is this: almost every safeguard that would later become mandatory under the EU AI Act, including data governance, human oversight, transparency, and bias monitoring, was absent. The harm was not caused by exotic technology. It was caused by an ordinary risk model deployed without a governance structure around it. That is precisely the gap the frameworks in this section exist to close.

> **The Big Picture:** Governance frameworks are not bureaucratic overhead bolted onto a finished model. They are the design constraints that decide, before a single prediction is made, who is accountable, which risks must be measured, and what the system is forbidden to do.

## Why Three Frameworks Now Dominate AI Governance

In 2026, three governance frameworks are the references AI practitioners cannot avoid: the **NIST AI Risk Management Framework** (US, voluntary, 2023), the **EU AI Act** (binding regulation, in force 2024), and the **UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI** (international standard, 2021, endorsed by all 194 member states). They differ in scope, enforceability, and tone, but together they define the operational floor that any production AI system has to meet.

This module covers each framework's core structure, where they overlap, where they disagree, and how a practitioner uses them in combination. **ISO/IEC 42001** (AI management systems standard) is briefly noted as the implementation bridge.

| Framework | Type | Geographic anchor | Teeth | What it governs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| NIST AI RMF | Voluntary guidance | United States | None (reputational) | The organization producing the system |
| EU AI Act | Binding law | European Union (extraterritorial) | Fines up to 7% turnover | The system in deployment |
| UNESCO Recommendation | Normative standard | Global (194 states) | National implementation | The society hosting the system |
| ISO/IEC 42001 | Certifiable standard | Global | Audit / certification | The management system |

## NIST AI Risk Management Framework

NIST released the AI RMF in January 2023 as a **voluntary, flexible** framework intended to adapt across industries. It does not impose penalties; it provides guidance and a shared vocabulary.

The framework is built on four core functions:

**Govern.** Establish a culture of risk management. Roles, responsibilities, accountability, and oversight processes. This is the institutional foundation.

**Map.** Categorize the AI system's context, intended purpose, stakeholders, and the risks specific to deployment. Map is where engineering and ethics intersect: you cannot manage risks you have not articulated.

**Measure.** Quantify and qualify the identified risks. Performance metrics, fairness metrics, robustness checks, drift indicators. The act of measuring forces specificity.

**Manage.** Treat the measured risks. Mitigation, transfer, acceptance, or avoidance. Document the choices.

The companion **AI RMF Playbook** offers concrete actions for each function. NIST also released a **Generative AI Profile** in July 2024 that adapts the framework to large language models and diffusion models.

The strength of NIST is **internalization**: it shapes how organizations think and structure their AI work. The weakness is **enforceability**: voluntary adoption produces uneven uptake, and organizations can claim alignment without auditable evidence.

> **Key Insight:** Govern-Map-Measure-Manage is a cycle, not a checklist. Each deployment loops through it again because context, data, and threats drift over time.

## The EU AI Act

The EU AI Act is the world's first comprehensive AI law. It entered into force August 2024 and is being phased in by category: the prohibited-practice bans applied from February 2025, general-purpose AI obligations from August 2025, and most high-risk obligations through 2026 and 2027. Unlike NIST, it is binding and carries fines up to 7% of global turnover.

The Act's structure is **risk-tiered**:

**Prohibited AI** (Article 5). Systems that pose unacceptable risk: social scoring, untargeted scraping of facial images to build recognition databases, real-time biometric surveillance in public (with narrow law-enforcement exceptions), emotion recognition in workplaces and schools (with narrow exceptions), and exploitative manipulation of vulnerable groups.

**High-risk AI** (Annex III). Systems used in employment, education, critical infrastructure, law enforcement, migration, and others. Subject to strict obligations: risk management system, data governance, technical documentation, transparency, human oversight, accuracy/robustness, post-market monitoring, and conformity assessment.

**General-purpose AI (GPAI) models** (Articles 51-55). Foundation models with specific obligations on documentation, copyright compliance, and (for systemic-risk models) safety testing and incident reporting.

**Limited-risk AI**. Transparency obligations: disclosure when content is AI-generated, when interacting with a chatbot, when deepfakes are used.

**Minimal-risk AI**. Most consumer applications. Voluntary codes of conduct.

The Act is enforced by national authorities and the new **AI Office**. Penalties range from €7.5M (or 1% turnover) for incorrect information up to €35M (or 7% turnover) for prohibited-AI violations.

For practitioners outside the EU, the Act has extraterritorial reach: any AI system whose output is used in the EU is in scope. This is the *Brussels effect* observers have already documented for GDPR.

## UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI

UNESCO adopted the Recommendation in November 2021, endorsed by all 194 member states. It is non-binding but normatively powerful. Its centerpiece is a set of principles broader than the technical risk tiers of the EU Act and the operational functions of the NIST RMF.

Core principles include:

- Proportionality and do-no-harm
- Safety and security
- Fairness and non-discrimination
- Sustainability (including environmental cost of AI)
- Right to privacy
- Human oversight and determination
- Transparency and explainability
- Responsibility and accountability
- Awareness and literacy
- Multi-stakeholder governance

UNESCO's distinctive contributions are **environmental sustainability**, **gender equality**, and the **emphasis on AI literacy as a public good**. It also explicitly addresses Global South concerns about technology transfer and capacity building, which the NIST and EU frameworks largely do not.

The Recommendation is not enforceable, but member states agreed to develop national implementations. The Recommendation also frames a **Readiness Assessment Methodology** that countries use to evaluate their AI ecosystem.

## ISO/IEC 42001: The Management System Bridge

ISO/IEC 42001 (2023) is the first international standard for an **AI management system**. It is to AI what ISO/IEC 27001 is to information security: a certifiable, audited management system that organizations can implement to demonstrate alignment with both NIST RMF and EU AI Act expectations.

For an enterprise that needs to operationalize compliance across jurisdictions, ISO 42001 is becoming the practical bridge. It does not replace either NIST or the EU Act; it provides a single management system that satisfies the documentation and oversight demands of both.

## Where the Frameworks Overlap and Disagree

**Overlap.** All three converge on transparency, accountability, human oversight, fairness, and risk management. An AI system that satisfies any one substantially is unlikely to badly violate another.

**Disagreement on enforceability.** EU AI Act is binding with fines; NIST is voluntary; UNESCO is normative. Multinational deployments need to pick the strictest applicable bar.

**Disagreement on scope.** NIST is risk-management focused. EU Act adds market-access rules. UNESCO adds environmental and equity dimensions. A team optimizing only for EU Act compliance can miss UNESCO's sustainability principle.

**Disagreement on subject.** The EU Act regulates the **system in deployment**. NIST RMF guides the **organization producing the system**. UNESCO frames the **society that hosts the system**. All three are needed.

## Worked Case Analysis: A Hiring-Screening Model Going to Market

A US human-resources software vendor builds an AI tool that ranks job applicants from their resumes and recorded video interviews, and it wants to sell the tool to employers in both the US and the EU. Walk it through all three frameworks.

**Step 1: Classify under the EU AI Act.** AI used in employment, specifically for recruitment and candidate evaluation, sits squarely in Annex III as **high-risk**. The video-interview component also touches emotion-recognition concerns; emotion recognition in the workplace is among the Article 5 prohibited practices, so the inferred-emotion feature is not merely high-risk, it may be **banned outright** when used to evaluate employees, and is at minimum legally hazardous for candidates. The legal floor is therefore: drop or quarantine the emotion-inference feature, and treat the ranking model as high-risk.

**Step 2: Apply NIST Govern-Map-Measure-Manage.** Govern: name an accountable owner and an oversight committee. Map: document intended use (ranking, not auto-rejecting), affected stakeholders, and known risks (proxy discrimination via resume gaps, university prestige, name-based ethnicity signals). Measure: compute selection-rate parity across protected groups, for example the four-fifths rule, and stress-test on perturbed resumes. Manage: if the female-to-male selection ratio is 0.62 (below the 0.80 four-fifths threshold), that is disparate impact; the response is feature ablation, reweighting, or human-in-the-loop review before any candidate is filtered out.

**Step 3: Cross-check against UNESCO.** UNESCO surfaces gaps the technical frameworks understate: equitable access (does the video format disadvantage applicants with disabilities or poor connectivity?), AI literacy (are candidates told an algorithm scored them, and can they contest it?), and human determination (a human, not the model, must make the final hiring call).

**Step 4: ISO 42001.** If the vendor wants to prove this to every customer without re-auditing per client, it adopts ISO 42001 so the management system is certified once and reused.

### Stakeholder and Tradeoff Table

| Stakeholder | Primary interest | Risk if framework ignored | Framework that protects it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Job applicant | Fair, contestable evaluation | Silent algorithmic rejection, proxy bias | EU Act (human oversight), UNESCO (literacy, contestability) |
| Employer (customer) | Legal, accurate screening | Liability for discriminatory hiring | EU Act high-risk obligations |
| Vendor | Market access, reputation | Fines up to 7% turnover, market ban | EU Act, ISO 42001 |
| Regulator / society | Non-discrimination, transparency | Erosion of trust in automated hiring | UNESCO principles, NIST accountability |

### The Decision

| Option | Legal status (EU) | Ethical cost | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ship as built (with emotion inference, no oversight) | Likely prohibited + high-risk non-compliant | High: bias, no recourse | Reject |
| Ship ranking only, no human review | High-risk, non-compliant | Medium: unaccountable filtering | Reject |
| Ranking + mandatory human review + bias audits + applicant disclosure | High-risk, compliant | Low, residual proxy-bias risk monitored | **Adopt** |

The compliant path is more expensive to build, but it is the only one that survives all three frameworks and the only one that would have prevented a *toeslagenaffaire*-style failure.

## A Practitioner Workflow

When designing an AI deployment that will be reviewed by any of these frameworks:

1. **Categorize under the EU AI Act** (prohibited / high-risk / limited-risk / minimal). This determines the legal floor.
2. **Apply NIST AI RMF Govern-Map-Measure-Manage** as the internal management approach. This satisfies most EU Act high-risk obligations and produces auditable artifacts.
3. **Cross-check against UNESCO principles**, especially environmental cost and equitable access. This catches ethical gaps the technical frameworks miss.
4. **If the organization needs certifiable compliance, adopt ISO 42001** as the management system.

The combination is overdetermined for most teams. Pick the framework that aligns with your jurisdiction and stakeholder expectations as the primary, and use the others as cross-checks.

## Counterarguments and Limitations

Framework-driven governance is not self-justifying, and a serious practitioner should hold the critiques in view.

**Compliance theater.** A team can produce every required document and still ship a harmful system. Auditable artifacts measure process, not outcomes. NIST's voluntariness makes this especially easy to game.

**Innovation and cost burden.** Critics argue the EU AI Act's conformity assessments fall hardest on startups and open-source developers, who lack compliance staff, potentially entrenching large incumbents who can absorb the overhead.

**Pace mismatch.** Regulation moves in years; model capability moves in months. The Act was drafted before the generative-AI surge and had to bolt on GPAI provisions late, a sign that static rules struggle against a moving target.

**Principles without precision.** UNESCO's principles are broadly endorsed precisely because they are abstract. "Fairness" and "sustainability" do not, by themselves, tell an engineer which metric to optimize or which threshold to enforce.

**The response, not a refutation.** None of these defeats the case for governance; the *toeslagenaffaire* shows the cost of its absence. The mature position is that frameworks are necessary but not sufficient: they set the floor, and engineering judgment plus genuine accountability must raise the ceiling.

## Summary

### Key Takeaways

- Three frameworks dominate: **NIST AI RMF** (voluntary, organization-focused), the **EU AI Act** (binding, deployment-focused), and the **UNESCO Recommendation** (normative, society-focused); **ISO/IEC 42001** bridges them as a certifiable management system.
- The EU AI Act's risk tiers (prohibited, high-risk, limited, minimal) set the legal floor, and its extraterritorial *Brussels effect* reaches any system whose output is used in the EU.
- NIST's **Govern-Map-Measure-Manage** cycle produces the auditable artifacts that satisfy most high-risk obligations.
- UNESCO catches the gaps the technical frameworks miss: sustainability, equity, AI literacy, and Global South capacity.
- Frameworks set the floor; they do not guarantee good outcomes. Watch for compliance theater, cost burden on small actors, and the regulation-versus-capability pace mismatch.
