# Why Education Is a Special Case for AI Ethics

> **"The logs easily could have been generated by the automated syncing of course material to devices logged into Canvas but not being used during an exam."**
> - Electronic Frontier Foundation, on the 2021 Dartmouth Geisel investigation

---

## Opening: When the Data Decided

In spring 2021, Dartmouth's Geisel School of Medicine charged 17 medical students with cheating. The evidence was not a proctor's eyewitness account or a flagged answer pattern. It was a retrospective review of a year of student activity logs from Canvas, the learning management system that holds lecture files and course material. Administrators read the log "pings" during exams as proof that students had opened restricted files.

The reading was wrong. A technical review found that the pings could be generated automatically when Canvas synced course material to a logged-in device that the student was not even touching. There was no reliable way to distinguish deliberate access from background refresh. After students, faculty, and rights groups pushed back, the dean dismissed all honor-code charges and cleared the transcripts. But several students had already faced suspension or unprofessional-conduct marks that can end a medical career before it starts.

The Dartmouth case is not a story about a uniquely bad LMS. It is a clean illustration of why educational AI and analytics are ethically distinct. A data-driven inference, applied at scale, fed a high-stakes decision about minors-to-young-adults in a context they could not opt out of, and it nearly bypassed the due process a human accuser would have had to honor.

> **The Big Picture:** Most AI ethics frameworks were written for consumer products and adult users who can walk away. Education breaks three of those background assumptions at once. This section names those breaks, maps the three US legal frameworks that partly cover them, and gives you a workflow to run before any learner-facing deployment.

## What Makes Education Distinct

Educational technology produces some of the most ethically charged AI deployments in any domain. Three structural features make it distinct.

First, **the decision subjects are often minors**. Children and adolescents have a legal and moral status (limited capacity to consent, special privacy protections, vulnerability to persuasion) that adult consumer markets do not share at scale.

Second, **schools and universities are coercive contexts**. A student who refuses to use the LMS does not get a fair grade. A child who refuses the screening test does not get the supports. The ordinary opt-out logic of consumer products fails here: refusing the AI tool means refusing the institution.

Third, **education shapes life trajectories**. A misclassification in a hiring screen affects one job application. A misclassification in an early-grade reading-risk model can route a child into a lower track for years, with measurable downstream effects on income, health, and civic participation.

| Background assumption of consumer AI ethics | How education breaks it |
| --- | --- |
| Users are autonomous adults who can consent | Subjects are often minors with limited capacity to consent |
| Users can opt out without losing essential access | Refusing the tool can mean refusing grades, supports, or enrollment |
| Harms are recoverable and individual | Tracking and labeling effects compound over years and cohorts |

These breaks create a body of education-specific AI ethics anchored on three legal frameworks (FERPA, COPPA, IDEA) and an evolving set of professional norms (AERA, NEPC, EDUCAUSE, the OECD's *AI and the Future of Skills*, and UNESCO's 2021 *Recommendation on the Ethics of AI*). Reiser and Dempsey's 5th edition of *Trends and Issues in Instructional Design and Technology* added chapters on AI ethics in education for this reason.

## Legal Framework 1: FERPA

The **Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act** (1974, US) governs **educational records** and parental/student rights regarding them. For AI ethics, the salient FERPA implications are:

- **Personally identifiable information (PII) in educational records cannot be disclosed without consent.** A vendor that ingests student records for AI training is constrained by the institution's FERPA exposure, not the vendor's preferred terms of service.
- **The "school official" exception** allows certain third parties to access records under direct institutional control with a legitimate educational interest. AI vendors operating under this exception inherit FERPA obligations.
- **Parents have rights until age 18; the rights transfer to the student in higher education.** AI consent flows must distinguish these stakeholders.

FERPA does not by itself define what is *ethical* use of educational data. It defines what is *legal*. A FERPA-compliant deployment can still violate norms about surveillance, equity, or autonomy. The Dartmouth logs, for instance, were institutional records the school was entitled to review; FERPA was no barrier at all, which is precisely why FERPA compliance cannot be the end of the analysis.

## Legal Framework 2: COPPA

The **Children's Online Privacy Protection Act** (1998, US) governs commercial collection of personal information from children under 13. Salient implications:

- **Verifiable parental consent is required** before collecting PII from under-13 users in commercial services.
- **Schools can act as agents for parents** in the educational context under specific conditions, but the conditions are narrow and frequently misunderstood.
- **Behavioral and biometric data are increasingly treated as PII** under FTC enforcement.

The narrowness of the school-agent path is not hypothetical. In May 2023 the FTC settled with the ed-tech provider **Edmodo** for $6 million, finding that the company had collected children's names, emails, birthdates, and persistent identifiers (used for advertising) from roughly 600,000 under-13 students in 2020 alone, and had **unlawfully outsourced its COPPA compliance to the schools** rather than obtaining proper consent itself. The order required Edmodo to delete the improperly collected data and, notably, to **destroy any models or algorithms trained on it**. The lesson for instructional designers: "the school said it was fine" is not a COPPA defense for a vendor, and a free platform may be free because the children are the product.

COPPA's coverage stops at 13, but the ethical concerns it raises (capacity to consent, special vulnerability) extend further. Adolescents from 13 to 18 occupy a regulatory gap where the legal floor is lower than the ethical ceiling.

## Legal Framework 3: IDEA and Algorithmic Disability Decisions

The **Individuals with Disabilities Education Act** governs special education in the US. As AI tools increasingly inform decisions about identification of disabilities, eligibility for services, and IEP design, IDEA's procedural-due-process requirements (notice, parental participation, evaluation by qualified personnel, right to appeal) apply.

A frequent ethics failure in this area: an AI early-warning system flags a child for "tier 2 intervention" without producing the documentation and parental notification IDEA would have required for a comparable human-driven decision. The AI route appears to bypass procedural due process, which is itself the violation. The Dartmouth case shows the same pattern outside the IDEA context: when an automated inference substitutes for a process that a human accuser would have had to follow, the missing process is the harm.

## Ethics Concerns the Law Only Partly Addresses

Four concerns extend beyond what FERPA, COPPA, or IDEA cover.

### Surveillance and Autonomy

Online proctoring, classroom-cam analytics, and engagement-tracking tools transform classrooms into instrumented environments. Even when these tools are FERPA-compliant, they may violate autonomy norms. A student who cannot study without being recorded is in a different educational relationship than one who can. The line between formative monitoring and pervasive surveillance is contested, but the burden of justification belongs to the deploying institution.

### Equity and the Distribution of AI Benefits

AI tutors, grading aids, and adaptive systems are not equally available across schools. When the wealthier school deploys a strong AI tutor and the under-resourced school cannot, the technology widens rather than narrows the achievement gap. The ethical question is not "is the tool good?" but "who has access to the tool, and what happens to those who do not?" UNESCO's 2021 Recommendation on the Ethics of AI explicitly raises this concern in education.

### Dark Patterns and Persuasive Design

Engagement-optimization in learner-facing AI can shade into manipulation: streaks, badges, notification cadences, and recommendation defaults that exploit limited self-regulation in younger learners. The educational frame allows persuasion that consumer-facing products would not. The ethics question: what kinds of persuasion are pedagogically justified, and which exploit developmental vulnerability?

### Algorithmic Authority and Teacher De-skilling

When a system tells the teacher what to do (which students to focus on, which interventions to apply, what the lesson plan should be), the system's recommendations carry a kind of authority that quietly displaces teacher judgment. Over time the teacher's professional skill atrophies, and the institution becomes dependent on the vendor. This concern is structural rather than individual, and it is not visible in any single deployment.

## A Pre-Deployment Workflow for Educational AI

Before deploying any AI system in a learner-facing context, work this checklist:

1. **Whose records are involved? Whose consent is required?** Map FERPA, COPPA, IDEA exposure for every data flow. Treat vendor-side data movement as institutional exposure.
2. **What decisions does the system make or inform?** For each decision, would a human-driven equivalent require notice, parental participation, or due process? If yes, the AI route requires the same.
3. **What is the equity profile of access?** Who is using the tool, and who is not? What happens to learners who cannot or will not use it?
4. **What is the surveillance footprint?** What is recorded, who can see it, how long is it retained, and what cannot be opted out of?
5. **Where does the persuasion sit?** What engagement mechanisms are deployed, and would they be acceptable on consumer-facing children's products?
6. **What is the teacher-authority impact?** How does the system change what teachers do, and is the change reversible if the vendor relationship ends?
7. **What is the audit trail?** Can the institution explain a specific decision to a parent or student a year from now? If not, the deployment is below the procedural-due-process floor.

The workflow is intentionally heavy. Educational AI is high-stakes by definition because the subjects are minors, the context is coercive, and the trajectories are durable.

## Worked Case Analysis: The Webcam "Engagement Analytics" Offer

**Scenario.** A K-8 district is offered a *free* AI "engagement analytics" platform. It uses webcam emotion detection to score how attentive each student appears and flags students who seem inattentive so teachers can intervene. The vendor states it is "FERPA-aligned" via the school-official exception.

We run the workflow and record where each step lands.

**Step 1 (records and consent).** The webcam feed and the derived attention scores are PII in educational records. The students are K-8, so a large share are **under 13**, putting this squarely under COPPA, not just FERPA. The vendor leans on the school-official exception, but the Edmodo order is directly on point: a vendor cannot outsource COPPA compliance to the school and call it consent. **Exposure: high (COPPA + FERPA).**

**Step 2 (decisions and due process).** The flag informs an intervention decision. If a flag ever feeds a referral for evaluation, IDEA notice and parental-participation rights attach. The automated route currently produces no such record. **Exposure: high (IDEA-adjacent due process).**

**Step 3 (equity).** Emotion-detection models are documented to perform unevenly across skin tone, neurodivergent expression, and cultural display norms. A child who shows attention differently is more likely to be falsely flagged. **Exposure: high (disparate error rates).**

**Step 4 (surveillance).** Continuous webcam capture in a classroom is the maximal surveillance footprint, and there is no real opt-out in a coercive setting. **Exposure: high.**

Now compare the stakeholders and what each gains or loses:

| Stakeholder | Potential benefit | Potential harm | Can they opt out? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Student (under 13) | Earlier teacher attention | False "inattentive" label; constant recording | No (coercive context) |
| Teacher | A focus signal | De-skilling; liability for acting on bad flags | Partially |
| Parent | Visibility into engagement | No real consent; data sold or repurposed | Rarely informed |
| District | "Free" analytics, perceived rigor | COPPA liability; equity lawsuit; reputational harm | Yes (decline now) |
| Vendor | Data and model training | FTC enforcement (see Edmodo) | n/a |

The tradeoff is lopsided. The benefit is a weak, low-validity attention proxy. The costs concentrate on the least powerful party (the minor who cannot opt out) and the validity of the core signal is contested.

| Decision option | Verdict | Reasoning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adopt as offered | Reject | Fails Steps 1-4; "free" implies data monetization; emotion detection is low-validity and inequitable |
| Adopt with consent + audit logging | Still reject for under-13 webcam capture | Even with consent, continuous biometric capture of minors fails the surveillance and equity tests |
| Decline; pilot a non-biometric formative tool with opt-in | **Defensible** | Removes the COPPA biometric exposure and the worst surveillance footprint while keeping a pedagogical aim |

**The deepest concern surfaces at Step 4 (surveillance) compounded by Step 3 (equity):** continuous biometric capture of children, with disparate false-flag rates, in a setting where no one can refuse. "FERPA-aligned" is true and irrelevant; it answers a question that was never the binding constraint.

## Counterarguments and Limitations

A rigorous treatment has to take the other side seriously.

**"This is precautionary paralysis. Schools will never adopt anything useful."** The workflow does not say *never deploy*; it says *justify the deployment at the level the stakes demand*. The webcam case fails not because it is AI but because its core signal is low-validity and its costs fall on minors who cannot consent. A high-validity, opt-in, non-biometric tool clears the same workflow easily. The bar scales with the stakes.

**"Refusing AI also has a cost: the under-resourced school stays behind."** This is real, and it is the equity concern (Step 3) pointing the other way. Declining a flawed tool is not the same as declining all tools. The ethical move is to push for access to *good* tools, not to accept a bad one because it is free.

**"The law is the consensus on what is acceptable; over-reading ethics is just personal preference."** Law is a *floor*, often a lagging one. COPPA stops at 13; FERPA permits broad institutional record review; neither anticipated webcam emotion detection. The 13-to-18 regulatory gap is the clearest case where the legal floor sits well below any defensible ethical ceiling.

**Limitation of this framework itself.** FERPA, COPPA, and IDEA are US statutes. Outside the US, GDPR (and its rules on automated decision-making and children's data) and national education laws govern instead, and the specifics differ. The *structure* of the analysis (minors, coercion, durable trajectories, due process, equity) transfers; the legal citations do not.

## Summary

### Key Takeaways

- Education breaks three background assumptions of consumer AI ethics at once: subjects are often **minors**, the context is **coercive**, and the effects on **trajectories are durable**.
- **FERPA, COPPA, and IDEA** define what is legal, not what is ethical. Compliance is a floor, not a finish line.
- A vendor **cannot outsource COPPA compliance to a school** (the $6M Edmodo order), and "free" ed-tech often monetizes children's data.
- When an automated inference substitutes for a process a human decision-maker would owe (notice, participation, appeal), **the missing process is itself the harm** (Dartmouth, IDEA-adjacent flags).
- Four concerns sit largely beyond the law: **surveillance/autonomy, equity of access, persuasive dark patterns, and teacher de-skilling**.
- Run the **seven-step workflow** before any learner-facing deployment, and weight the tradeoff toward the party who cannot opt out.

### Key Distinctions

| Term | Meaning | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Legal vs. ethical | Compliant vs. defensible | FERPA-compliant surveillance can still be unethical |
| School-official exception | FERPA path for vendor access | Does not by itself satisfy COPPA consent |
| Procedural due process | Notice, participation, appeal | AI routes inherit it; they do not escape it |
