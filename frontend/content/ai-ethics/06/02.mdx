# Surveillance, Dark Patterns, and Learner Autonomy

> **"The student is the one party in the educational data economy who is least able to say no."**
> - paraphrasing the through-line of Selwyn, *Distrusting Educational Technology*

---

## Opening: The Proctoring Case That Reached a Federal Court

In August 2022, a federal judge in *Ogletree v. Cleveland State University* ruled that a remote-proctoring "room scan," in which a student was required to pan a webcam around his private bedroom before an online chemistry exam, was an unreasonable search under the Fourth Amendment. The university had argued the scan was routine, consented to, and necessary to preserve academic integrity. The court disagreed: the scan was a government search of a private home, the student's "consent" was coerced by the requirement to take the exam, and less intrusive alternatives existed.

The case is worth holding onto because it compresses the entire module into one event. A tool marketed as benign analytics ("we just verify the testing environment") turned out to be surveillance; the consent was not real consent because the context was coercive; and the harm was invisible until someone with standing forced it into the open. Most educational AI never reaches a courtroom, which is precisely the problem. The defaults ship, the metrics improve, and no one with the student's interest in mind is in the room when the design decisions are made.

This module gives you the vocabulary and the workflow to be that person. It draws on Selwyn's *Distrusting Educational Technology*, Williamson's work on the data-fication of schooling, the National Academies *Governance of Artificial Intelligence* report, the Norwegian Consumer Council's *Deceived by Design*, and ongoing FTC enforcement (notably the 2023 Edmodo action) against deceptive ed-tech.

## What Educational AI Inherits From Persuasive-Tech Design

The same dark patterns that consumer-tech regulators are now prosecuting in the EU under the Digital Services Act show up regularly in educational AI products: streaks, badges, notification cadences, engagement-optimized recommendations, and surveillance dashboards branded as analytics. The educational frame allows persuasion that consumer products would not. The premise is that the persuasion is for the learner's benefit, so the safeguards that apply to manipulating adults can be relaxed when manipulating children "for their own good."

That premise is wrong twice. It overestimates how well designers can predict what is good for any given learner, and it underestimates how durable the habits cultivated by design are. A streak mechanic does not just shape tonight's study session; it trains a reward circuit the learner carries forward.

## Educational Surveillance: From Formative Monitoring to Pervasive Recording

A formative-assessment system that records which problems a student got wrong is monitoring. A platform that records keystroke timing, eye-gaze, webcam-detected emotion, and conversation-window time on every page is surveillance. The line between them is contested but real, and you can operationalize it with three tests.

**Aggregation depth.** Monitoring tracks specific learning-relevant signals. Surveillance combines streams (behavior + biometric + social + temporal) into profiles that exceed any single signal's purpose. The danger is emergent: keystroke cadence alone is innocuous, but keystroke cadence plus webcam affect plus time-of-day can infer mental-health state that no one consented to disclose.

**Asymmetry of access.** In monitoring, the student can usually see what is recorded. In surveillance, the dashboard is for the institution; the student is rarely shown their own profile. Asymmetry is the signature of a power imbalance: the watched party cannot audit the watcher.

**Inability to opt out.** Monitoring tied to a specific assignment is bounded. Surveillance that runs whenever the student is logged in cannot be declined without leaving the institution. This is why "consent" in schools is so fragile: refusing the tool means refusing the course.

Online proctoring platforms operationalize all three properties at once, which is why *Ogletree* (above) was decided the way it was. The FTC's 2023 enforcement against Edmodo for COPPA and FTC Act violations, which alleged that the company conditioned classroom use on children's data and pushed compliance burdens onto teachers, marks a regulatory turn from "notice" toward "you may not collect this at all."

A defensible educational AI deployment chooses **monitoring rather than surveillance**: bounded scope, learner-visible records, real opt-out for non-essential streams.

## Dark Patterns Specific to Learner-Facing AI

Several engagement-optimization patterns are widely deployed in educational AI and are ethically distinct from their consumer counterparts because the audience includes minors whose self-regulation is still developing.

| Dark pattern | Mechanism | Developmental vulnerability exploited |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Variable-ratio rewards | Streaks, surprise badges, unpredictable drops | Immature impulse control; slot-machine reinforcement |
| Loss-aversion design | Streak-loss anxiety, lost leaderboard rank, progress shaming | Still-developing emotional self-regulation |
| Manufactured social pressure | "X classmates are working now," false peer presence, AI peer comments | Heightened adolescent peer-conformity sensitivity |
| Engagement-optimized recommendation | Objective is time-on-platform, not learning | Cannot evaluate whether content serves their own goals |
| Dark consent flows | Bundled consent, no granular control | Weak bargaining position; parents rarely read flows |

**Variable-ratio reward schedules** mimic the reinforcement that makes slot machines effective. They reliably extend session length and shorten between-session intervals; their effect on actual learning is far less clear, and they exploit the limited self-regulation of younger learners.

**Loss-aversion design** weaponizes a robust cognitive bias. Deploying it on minors who are still developing emotional self-regulation is hard to justify pedagogically.

**Manufactured social pressure** uses live peer-presence cues and AI-generated peer comments. We previously catalogued ALGET's own AI peer-note injection as an example; it is not unique to this codebase, and the Norwegian Consumer Council's *Deceived by Design* documents many more.

**Engagement-optimized content recommendations** drift toward content that holds attention regardless of learning value, by Goodhart's law: when time-on-platform becomes the target, it ceases to be a good measure of learning.

**Dark consent flows** bundle product improvement, behavioral profiling, and required functionality with no granular control, inherited from consumer SaaS even though learners and parents have a weaker bargaining position.

Each of these is **legal in most jurisdictions today**. Each is **ethically contested**. The EU Digital Services Act has begun to constrain some; US enforcement is uneven.

## Learner Autonomy as Ethical Anchor

Self-determination theory (Deci and Ryan) frames human motivation around three needs: **autonomy** (volition over one's actions), **competence** (effective interaction with the environment), and **relatedness** (genuine connection with others). Educational AI that cultivates these supports learning. Educational AI that undermines them, particularly autonomy, produces compliance without development.

A common misframe: "engagement" is treated as evidence of motivation, and motivation is treated as evidence of learning. Self-determination theory rejects this chain. Engagement that comes from manufactured loss aversion or variable-ratio reward is controlled motivation, not autonomous motivation, and the longitudinal evidence on the difference is unkind to the engagement-optimized products: when the manufactured cue is removed, the behavior collapses, because nothing internal was ever built.

The autonomy criterion does practical design work. It rules **out** persuasion mechanics that exploit limited self-regulation, surveillance the learner cannot inspect or decline, recommendation systems whose objective is platform-time, and consent flows the learner cannot meaningfully exercise. It rules **in** learner-visible records of what the system tracks, granular control over notifications and recommendations, pacing controls and self-directed pathways, and real exit ramps that do not penalize the learner.

## Worked Case Analysis: The Streaks-and-Leaderboards Redesign

**Scenario.** A learning platform for high-school students introduces three features: daily streaks, leaderboards visible to the whole class, and AI-generated "peer" comments under each student's highlights. After one term, engagement metrics (daily active users, session length) improve 40 percent. The product team wants to ship the features to all schools. You are the instructional designer asked to sign off.

**Step 1: Classify each feature against the surveillance tests and dark-pattern table.**

| Feature | Dark pattern present? | SDT need threatened | Surveillance concern |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Daily streaks | Variable-ratio + loss aversion | Autonomy (controlled motivation) | Low (no new data stream) |
| Class-visible leaderboard | Loss aversion + social pressure | Autonomy + relatedness (rivalry, shaming) | Medium (rank is a derived profile shown to peers) |
| AI-generated peer comments | Manufactured social presence | Relatedness (fake connection) + autonomy | Medium (implies modeling of the learner) |

**Step 2: Predict the long-run outcome using SDT.** The 40 percent engagement lift is controlled motivation. SDT predicts that once the streak or leaderboard cue is removed (summer break, a platform change, simple habituation), autonomous motivation and persistence will be *lower* than baseline, because the features substituted external pressure for internal interest. Engagement up does not imply learning up.

**Step 3: Stakeholder and tradeoff table.**

| Stakeholder | What they gain from shipping | What they lose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Product team | Headline 40% engagement metric | Long-run retention risk; regulatory exposure |
| Teacher | Short-term participation | Trust if shaming surfaces; managing leaderboard fallout |
| High-performing student | Status from leaderboard | Anxiety to defend rank; extrinsic reframing of learning |
| Struggling student | Little | Public shaming, loss-aversion stress, withdrawal |
| Parent | Visible activity | Unconsented social profiling of their child |

**Step 4: Decision.** Do not ship as designed. The struggling student bears concentrated harm for a metric that SDT predicts will not convert to learning. **Redesign:** keep streaks but make them private and self-set (autonomy-supportive goal-setting rather than imposed loss aversion); replace the class-visible leaderboard with private personal-best progress; remove AI-generated peer comments entirely, because fake relatedness is the least defensible mechanic of the three. Re-measure on a learning outcome, not engagement.

This is the move the autonomy anchor forces: a feature is not justified by the metric it lifts; it is justified by whether it builds something the learner keeps.

## Counterarguments and Limitations

A serious analyst should hold the opposing case in view.

**"Some friction is good, and gamification demonstrably helps."** True in part. Well-designed gamification that supports competence (clear goals, informative feedback, optional challenge) is consistent with SDT. The line is not "no game mechanics" but "no mechanics that substitute control for volition." A self-set streak is fine; a guilt-inducing streak-loss notification is not.

**"Engagement is a real prerequisite; a tool no one opens teaches nothing."** Also true. The autonomy critique does not say ignore engagement; it says do not treat engagement as the terminal goal or as a proxy for learning. Engagement is necessary, not sufficient, and must be earned with interest rather than manufactured with pressure.

**"The legal floor permits all of this."** Mostly correct today, and that is the limitation of a purely legal analysis. FERPA, COPPA, and IDEA set a floor, not a ceiling; most dark patterns are legal precisely because regulation lags design. The ethical bar is higher than the legal one, which is why a workflow that stops at "is it legal?" will ship harmful defaults.

**"Surveillance vs. monitoring is a spectrum, not a binary."** Correct, and the three tests are diagnostic, not a bright line. Their value is in forcing the specific questions (How deep is the aggregation? Can the learner see it? Can they decline it?) rather than in producing a single verdict.

## A Pre-Deployment Workflow for Educational AI

Combining this module with the FERPA/COPPA/IDEA module from earlier:

1. **Map data flows.** What is collected, by whom, retained how long, accessible to which roles?
2. **Test for monitoring vs surveillance.** Aggregation depth, learner visibility, opt-out reality.
3. **Audit engagement mechanics.** Are variable-ratio rewards, loss aversion, manufactured social pressure used? On minors?
4. **Check learner autonomy.** Do learners have visibility into and control over what the system does about them?
5. **Verify legal floor.** FERPA, COPPA, IDEA, GDPR (if applicable), DSA dark-pattern restrictions.
6. **Verify procedural due process.** Algorithmic decisions affecting placement, services, or credentialing must satisfy IDEA-class oversight.
7. **Document the audit trail.** A specific decision must be explainable to a parent or learner a year later.

Educational AI is high-stakes. The workflow is intentionally heavy because the subjects are minors, the context is coercive, and the trajectories are durable.

## Key Takeaways

- **Surveillance is not monitoring.** Use three tests: aggregation depth, asymmetry of access, inability to opt out. Choose bounded, learner-visible, declinable.
- **Five dark patterns recur in learner-facing AI:** variable-ratio rewards, loss aversion, manufactured social pressure, engagement-optimized recommendation, dark consent flows. Each exploits a developmental vulnerability and is mostly legal but ethically contested.
- **Engagement is not motivation, and motivation is not learning.** SDT predicts controlled motivation collapses when the manufactured cue is removed.
- **Autonomy is the working anchor.** It rules out manipulation and surveillance; it rules in visibility, granular control, pacing, and real exit ramps.
- **The legal floor is a floor.** A workflow that stops at "is it legal?" will ship harmful defaults; the ethical bar is higher.
- **Justify features by what the learner keeps,** not by the metric they lift.
