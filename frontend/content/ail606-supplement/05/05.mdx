# Observation Protocols

![Observation Protocols textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/ail606-supplement/05-05-observation-protocols.png)

## Why This Section Matters

What you write down during a session becomes your only memory of it, and a verdict scribbled in the moment ("the page is confusing") destroys the evidence it claims to capture. The discipline of observation is the discipline of recording behavior, the things a camera would catch, separately from inference, the conclusions you draw later. Collapse the two at log time and you have replaced data with opinion before analysis even begins. This section is about building an observation protocol that keeps the raw record clean enough that a second analyst could replay the session and arrive at the same entries, which is the difference between findings and impressions.

In AIL 606 the moderated usability test script with observation-to-revision trace must contain a protocol, not just a hope to "pay attention." The protocol decides what gets counted, how it is anchored in time and task, and where the moderator's judgments are allowed to live. A weak protocol lets one observer's bias masquerade as the participant's experience.

## Learning Targets

- Separate observation (what was seen, said, or done) from inference (what it might mean) at the moment of recording.
- Anchor each entry to a timestamp and a task step so behavior can be tallied across participants.
- Capture think-aloud and behavioral signals without leading or prompting the participant.
- Defer severity and interpretation to analysis, keeping the log replayable and bias-resistant.

## Opening Case

A moderator watches a participant struggle on a checkout screen and logs: "Confused by the cart page, clearly did not understand it, the page is bad." Three sentences, three verdicts, and not one piece of evidence. When the team meets to prioritize fixes, this note can be argued with but not checked, because nobody can see what the participant actually did.

The problem is that the moderator recorded a conclusion and threw away the behavior that might have justified it. Whether the participant hovered, asked a question, clicked the wrong control, or backed out is gone. The note feels like data because it is specific and confident, but it is unrepeatable: a second observer watching the same recording could log something entirely different and neither could be shown wrong.

## Core Concept

An observation protocol enforces a boundary between two kinds of statements that beginners constantly merge.

| Layer | Example | Belongs in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Observation | "Hovered between two buttons, said 'which one saves it?'" | The live log |
| Inference | "Probably confused by ambiguous labels" | Analysis, later |
| Verdict | "The page is bad" | Nowhere in the raw record |

The reason to keep observation pure is repeatability. A behavioral entry ("moused over Continue and Update Cart three times") can be verified against the recording and tallied across participants; a verdict cannot. This is also a **[cognitive load theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_load)** issue for the moderator: interpreting and judging in real time consumes attention that should be spent watching, so the protocol offloads judgment to a later, calmer pass and frees the moderator to capture more behavior accurately.

Two further mechanics make a protocol trustworthy. First, anchoring: every entry carries a timestamp and the task step it occurred in, so the same event can be located and counted later, turning scattered notes into patterns. Second, non-leading capture: a think-aloud protocol asks the participant to keep talking but never asks "did you find that confusing?", because a leading prompt manufactures the data it pretends to observe. **UDL learner variability** adds a final demand: the protocol must record how different learners behave, not just whether the median learner succeeded, since the edge cases are where the design's real failures live and where a verdict-style note is most likely to erase them.

## Worked Example: Revising an Observation Note So It Records Behavior, Not Verdict

A moderator is logging what happened while a participant attempted a checkout task in a learning-store prototype.

**Before (interpretation logged as data):**

> Note: Participant was confused by the cart page and clearly did not understand the design. The page is bad.

Walk through why this note is not usable observation data:

1. **Separate observation from inference.** "Confused" and "did not understand" are the observer's conclusions, not what was seen or heard.
2. **Record the observable behavior.** What did the participant actually do, say, or look at, and for how long?
3. **Timestamp and anchor to the task step.** Tie each note to a specific screen and moment so it can be counted across participants.
4. **Keep the verdict out of the log; move it to analysis.** The protocol captures behavior; severity judgments come later, after patterns appear.

**After (behavior-anchored protocol entry):**

> 02:14, cart page: Participant moused over "Continue" and "Update Cart" three times, said "Wait, which one saves it?", clicked "Update Cart," then clicked browser Back. Task step: proceed to checkout. (Inference noted separately for analysis: possible label ambiguity.)

The after version is defensible because another analyst could replay the session and verify the entry, and the same behavior can be tallied across participants. The before version logged a verdict, which is unrepeatable and lets one observer's bias stand in for evidence.

Log what a camera would catch; save what it means for analysis. If an entry contains a word you could not point to in a recording (confused, frustrated, intuitive, bad), it is an inference and belongs in a separate column, not in the behavioral log.

Before locking the protocol into your moderated usability test script with observation-to-revision trace, check three things:

1. **Behavior check:** Is each log entry something an observer could verify on replay, free of verdicts?
2. **Anchor check:** Does every entry carry a timestamp and a task step so it can be counted across participants?
3. **Leading check:** Do your prompts keep the participant talking without suggesting what they should feel?

If any check fails, the log will record the moderator's interpretation rather than the participant's behavior.

## Common Misreadings

The most common error is treating a confident note as a clean note; specificity ("clearly confused") is not the same as observability, and confident verdicts are the hardest to dislodge later precisely because they sound like data. A second error is leading capture, where the moderator's reassurance or hint ("most people use the top button") rewrites the behavior being observed. A third is logging only failures and skipping the silent, smooth moments, which removes the baseline that makes a failure interpretable.

The test of a good entry is whether a stranger could replay the recording and write the same line.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Trace one moment from a verdict to a protocol entry. A participant testing a quiz-feedback screen pauses, then closes the module. A weak log says "gave up, found it discouraging." Replayability is gone. The protocol entry instead records the behavior with its anchor: "07:41, feedback screen after item 3: read on-screen text for about eight seconds, said nothing, clicked the X to close." The interpretation ("possible discouragement after negative feedback") goes in the inference column, where it can be tested against the other participants rather than asserted.

Now read across participants, which is what the anchoring was for. If four of six paused at the same timestamp on the same screen and three closed the module, a pattern emerges that no single verdict could establish, and **[cognitive load theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_load)** offers a testable explanation: the feedback may have read as an error rather than as formative guidance, adding a moment of demand at exactly the wrong point. The walkthrough's lesson is that clean, anchored behavioral entries are what let separate moments become a countable finding; verdicts stay forever stuck as one person's opinion.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read a session log, keep one column for verifiable behavior and one for interpretation, and move every entry into the column it actually belongs in. A log that cannot be split this way has fused observation and inference, and its findings inherit whatever bias the moderator brought to the room.

A strong reader of an observation protocol can answer four questions:

1. Could a second analyst replay the session and reproduce each entry?
2. Is every entry anchored to a time and a task step so it can be counted?
3. Where did a verdict slip into the behavioral log?
4. Did any prompt lead the participant toward the behavior it recorded?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The protocol is strong when a reviewer could hand your log and the recording to a stranger and have them confirm each behavioral entry without argument. Treat the observation-inference boundary as the load-bearing element; a single verdict in the behavioral column contaminates the count it sits in. Use the annotation tools to flag any entry containing an unobservable word, and ask BigAL to compare your entries against the behavior check rather than to make the notes more readable.

A useful closing line reads: "I rewrote the note from 'confused, page is bad' to a timestamped record of the hovering and the question because the original was an unverifiable verdict; I rejected the suggestion to add my severity rating inline, since severity belongs in analysis after patterns appear, not in the raw log."

## Transfer Case

Move the same observation discipline to classroom research, where the protocol governs field notes instead of session logs. The verdict trap returns as the evaluative shorthand ("the students were engaged"), which records the observer's impression and erases the behavior that might support or contradict it. The structure transfers (log observable behavior, anchor it, defer judgment), but the setting is noisier and the temptation to summarize is stronger. A reader who accepts "engaged" as data has made the same move as the moderator who wrote "confused": a feeling logged where a behavior should be.
