## Core Concept

The core idea in this section is that **Module 5 Usability Plan Studio** should be treated as a relationship among purpose, constraint, evidence, and revision. Purpose names what the learner or audience needs to accomplish. Constraint names what limits the decision. Evidence names the source that makes the decision defensible. Revision shows what changed after the evidence was considered.

Three course anchors shape the reasoning here:

- **Mayer multimedia learning** helps you decide what counts as a meaningful learning or performance demand.
- **cognitive load theory** helps you notice when a design or technology choice creates risk, burden, or unsupported assumptions.
- **UDL learner variability** helps you check whether the artifact works for varied learners, audiences, or use contexts.

The anchors should do work in your artifact. If Mayer multimedia learning or cognitive load theory does not change a decision, it is only a citation rather than evidence of judgment.

## Worked Example: Auditing a Full Usability Plan for Internal Coherence

This studio assembles the whole chapter into one plan. The failure mode here is not a single weak part; it is a plan whose parts do not line up. Audit a draft plan section by section.

**Before (parts that do not align):**

> Hypothesis: The redesigned navigation will feel more modern. Participants: 5 expert designers. Tasks: "Click Settings, then Profile, then Save." Metric: satisfaction rating. Plan: if satisfaction is high, ship it.

Walk the coherence audit, step by step:

1. **Hypothesis to metric.** "Feel more modern" is untestable and satisfaction cannot confirm a navigation-findability claim. Mismatch (chapter 05.01, 05.04).
2. **Participants to population.** Expert designers do not represent the real audience. Mismatch (05.02).
3. **Tasks to hypothesis.** The tasks spell out the click path, so findability is never tested. Mismatch (05.03).
4. **Decision rule to evidence.** "Ship if satisfaction is high" lets an attitude metric drive a launch the data cannot justify (05.06).

**After (an aligned plan):**

> Hypothesis: With the redesigned navigation, at least 8 of 10 target users will reach Profile settings unaided, versus a 4 of 10 baseline. Participants: 8 real instructors screened by LMS experience, spanning novice to frequent. Tasks: goal-based ("Update your display name so students see it"). Metrics: task completion and time on task (usability) plus a brief findability error count; satisfaction logged but not decisive. Decision rule: revise the navigation if fewer than 8 of 10 succeed, then re-test.

The after version is defensible because every part traces to the same goal: the metric can test the hypothesis, the sample matches the population, the tasks expose findability, and the decision rule follows the evidence. The studio's job is to catch the before-version mismatches before a single session runs.

## Decision Rule

Use the learning goal first, the media choice second, and the software feature last. A feature is justified only when it reduces unnecessary processing, strengthens useful processing, or makes learner action easier to observe.

Use the following check when the artifact looks finished but the reasoning may still be hidden:

1. **Purpose check:** What should the audience be able to do after reading or using the artifact?
2. **Evidence check:** Which exact source, observation, annotation, rubric line, data pattern, or policy boundary justifies the revision?
3. **Revision check:** What changed in the artifact, and what suggestion did you reject or modify?

If you cannot answer all three questions, the artifact may be complete, but it is not yet ready as evidence of learning.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Read this section as a small professional decision cycle, not as a list of terms. First, locate the work product: **moderated usability test script with observation-to-revision trace**. Then ask what problem the artifact is supposed to solve inside usability and learning evidence. A strong reader can explain the artifact in plain language before naming any theory: who will use it, what decision it supports, and what would count as evidence that it improved.

Next, connect the artifact to the course anchors: **Mayer multimedia learning**, **cognitive load theory**, **UDL learner variability**. The point is not to cite every anchor. The point is to let one anchor change the artifact. If the anchor does not change wording, layout, evidence, timing, or feedback, it has not yet become design reasoning.

Finally, inspect the revision trail. A weak submission says the artifact was improved. A strong submission shows the earlier version, the issue noticed, the support requested, the decision accepted or rejected, and the reason the final version is better for the intended learner or audience.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: **claim** on the left, **evidence** on the right. The claim is the decision you think the section is teaching. The evidence is the exact phrase, example, annotation, support move, or artifact revision that makes the claim inspectable.
