# Module 5 Usability Plan Studio

![Module 5 Usability Plan Studio textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/ail606-supplement/05-08-module-5-usability-plan-studio.png)

## Why This Section Matters

The pieces of a usability plan can each be excellent and the plan can still be worthless, because the failure mode of a whole plan is not a weak part but parts that do not line up. A precise hypothesis tested with the wrong participants, scored by a mismatched metric, on a task that leaks the answer, decided by a rule that ignores the data: every component is plausible alone, yet the chain is broken. This capstone studio is about internal coherence, the property that makes a plan trace end to end so the metric can actually test the hypothesis, the sample can represent the population, the task can expose the behavior, and the decision rule can follow the evidence. Coherence is what a reviewer checks last and what a designer should design first.

In AIL 606 the moderated usability test script with observation-to-revision trace is assembled here into one plan, and the studio's job is to catch the seams where chapter 05.01 through 05.07 fail to connect, before a single session burns the budget on a misaligned design.

## Learning Targets

- Audit a full usability plan for alignment across hypothesis, sample, task, metric, observation, and decision rule.
- Trace each component back to the single learning goal the plan exists to serve.
- Locate the seam where two well-formed parts contradict each other.
- Revise the plan so every part puts the same hypothesis at risk.

## Opening Case

A draft plan reads, in one breath: hypothesis, the navigation will feel more modern; participants, five expert designers; tasks, click Settings then Profile then Save; metric, satisfaction rating; rule, ship if satisfaction is high. Each clause sounds like something a usability plan should contain, and a quick reader nods along.

A coherence reader does not. The hypothesis is untestable, so the satisfaction metric could never confirm it; the experts cannot represent the real audience; the task spells out the click path, so findability is never tested; and the decision rule lets an attitude number drive a launch the data cannot justify. Nothing is missing, yet nothing connects. The plan would run smoothly and teach nothing, which is the most expensive kind of failure because it looks like success.

## Core Concept

Coherence is a chain, and a chain is only as strong as its weakest link. Auditing means checking each adjacent pair of components for alignment, not each component in isolation.

| Link | Alignment question | Section |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hypothesis to metric | Can this metric disconfirm this prediction? | 05.01, 05.04 |
| Population to sample | Do these participants represent the target? | 05.02 |
| Hypothesis to task | Does the task expose the predicted behavior? | 05.03 |
| Behavior to observation | Will the protocol capture it verifiably? | 05.05 |
| Evidence to decision | Does the rule follow what the data can show? | 05.06, 05.07 |

The deep idea is that each component constrains its neighbors. A findability hypothesis demands a goal-based task and a completion metric, and it forbids a satisfaction-only metric and a click-path task; choosing one component silently rules out options for the others. This is why coherence cannot be added at the end by polishing parts. **Mayer multimedia learning** supplies the fixed point that holds the chain together: the learning goal, to which every link must trace, so a part that serves no goal is a part that breaks alignment no matter how well-formed it is.

The same alignment logic recruits the rest of the module's theory. **Cognitive load theory** governs the hypothesis-to-task and behavior-to-observation links, because a task that overloads or a protocol that judges in real time corrupts the very behavior the hypothesis predicts. **UDL learner variability** governs the population-to-sample and evidence-to-decision links, because a sample that misses a subgroup and a decision rule that ignores disaggregated results will both ship a design that fails learners the plan never represented. Coherence, in other words, is where every earlier section's lesson becomes a single integrated check.

## Worked Example: Auditing a Full Usability Plan for Internal Coherence

This studio assembles the whole chapter into one plan. The failure mode here is not a single weak part; it is a plan whose parts do not line up. Audit a draft plan section by section.

**Before (parts that do not align):**

> Hypothesis: The redesigned navigation will feel more modern. Participants: 5 expert designers. Tasks: "Click Settings, then Profile, then Save." Metric: satisfaction rating. Plan: if satisfaction is high, ship it.

Walk the coherence audit, step by step:

1. **Hypothesis to metric.** "Feel more modern" is untestable and satisfaction cannot confirm a navigation-findability claim. Mismatch (chapter 05.01, 05.04).
2. **Participants to population.** Expert designers do not represent the real audience. Mismatch (05.02).
3. **Tasks to hypothesis.** The tasks spell out the click path, so findability is never tested. Mismatch (05.03).
4. **Decision rule to evidence.** "Ship if satisfaction is high" lets an attitude metric drive a launch the data cannot justify (05.06).

**After (an aligned plan):**

> Hypothesis: With the redesigned navigation, at least 8 of 10 target users will reach Profile settings unaided, versus a 4 of 10 baseline. Participants: 8 real instructors screened by LMS experience, spanning novice to frequent. Tasks: goal-based ("Update your display name so students see it"). Metrics: task completion and time on task (usability) plus a brief findability error count; satisfaction logged but not decisive. Decision rule: revise the navigation if fewer than 8 of 10 succeed, then re-test.

The after version is defensible because every part traces to the same goal: the metric can test the hypothesis, the sample matches the population, the tasks expose findability, and the decision rule follows the evidence. The studio's job is to catch the before-version mismatches before a single session runs.

## Decision Rule

Audit the links, not the parts; a plan ships only when every adjacent pair aligns and every component traces to one learning goal. If you can describe each component in isolation but cannot draw a line from each to the same goal, the plan is a collection, not a plan.

Before finalizing the moderated usability test script with observation-to-revision trace, check three things:

1. **Alignment check:** Does each adjacent pair (hypothesis-metric, population-sample, hypothesis-task, evidence-decision) actually connect?
2. **Goal-trace check:** Does every component point back to the same learning goal?
3. **Seam check:** Is there any well-formed part that quietly contradicts its neighbor?

If any check fails, the plan will run cleanly and answer no question worth asking.

## Common Misreadings

The frequent error is auditing components in isolation, approving each because it is individually competent and missing that two competent parts contradict each other. A second error is treating coherence as a finishing pass, when the constraints between components mean alignment has to be designed in from the hypothesis outward. A third is assuming a complete plan is a coherent one; completeness only means every slot is filled, not that the slots agree.

A plan with no missing parts can still have no working chain.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Trace one seam from a plausible draft to a fix. A team's plan predicts that an interactive simulation raises transfer accuracy, recruits a representative novice-to-expert sample, writes goal-based tasks, and logs careful behavioral observations: four strong links. Then the metric is time on task, and the seam snaps. Time is a usability measure (05.04); it cannot disconfirm a transfer-learning hypothesis (05.01), so the plan's strongest claim has no instrument that could ever test it. Every neighbor is fine, but this one link is broken, and the whole plan fails through it.

The fix is local but the diagnosis was global. Swap in a pre/post transfer measure, and the hypothesis-to-metric link aligns; nothing else needs to move. Then re-walk the chain with **UDL learner variability**, checking that the decision rule reads the disaggregated transfer results rather than the mean, so the plan will not ship a simulation that lifts experts and strands novices. The walkthrough's lesson is that coherence auditing finds the one seam that quality-checking each part would miss, because the defect lives between the components, not inside any of them.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read a complete plan, keep one column for each component and one for the neighbor it must agree with, then test each pair rather than each box. The defects you are hunting do not live inside the boxes, which usually look fine; they live on the lines between them, which is exactly where a part-by-part review never looks.

A strong reader of a full plan can answer four questions:

1. Can the metric actually disconfirm the hypothesis?
2. Does the sample represent the population the hypothesis is about?
3. Does the task expose the behavior the hypothesis predicts?
4. Does the decision rule follow only what the evidence can support?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The plan is strong when a reviewer can start at any component, follow the chain to the learning goal and back, and find no link where two parts pull in different directions. Treat the seams as the load-bearing elements; a flawless hypothesis wired to a mismatched metric is a broken plan, not a strong plan with one weak spot. Use the annotation tools to mark every adjacency you cannot defend, and ask BigAL to compare your plan against the alignment check rather than to improve any single section.

A useful closing line reads: "I changed the metric from time on task to a pre/post transfer measure because the original could not disconfirm the learning hypothesis it was paired with; I rejected the suggestion to keep satisfaction as the decision metric, since an attitude rating cannot justify shipping a transfer claim."

## Transfer Case

Move the same coherence audit to a grant proposal or a research design, where the components are a research question, a sample, an instrument, and an analysis plan. The failure mode is identical: a sharp question undone by an instrument that cannot measure it, or an analysis that cannot answer it, each part defensible alone. The structure transfers (check the links, trace every part to the question), but the cost of a broken seam rises, because a misaligned study wastes a funded year rather than a test session. A reader who reviews such a proposal section by section, approving each, has repeated the exact mistake the studio exists to prevent.
