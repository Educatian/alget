# Learning Metrics and Usability Metrics

![Learning Metrics and Usability Metrics textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/ail606-supplement/05-04-learning-metrics-and-usability-metrics.png)

## Why This Section Matters

A satisfied learner is not necessarily a learning learner, and an efficient interface is not necessarily an effective lesson. The single most common overclaim in instructional design is letting one comfortable number (usually satisfaction) speak for a claim it cannot support. This section is about construct validity in measurement: matching each claim you want to make to a metric that can actually bear it. Usability metrics tell you whether people can operate the thing; learning metrics tell you whether they walked away able to do something they could not do before. They answer different questions, and a plan that confuses them will report success on the easy question while staying silent on the hard one.

In AIL 606 the moderated usability test script with observation-to-revision trace must keep these two measurement families distinct and pair each claim with the metric that fits it. Mixing them is not a rounding error; it is a category mistake that lets a smooth, well-liked module pass without any evidence that anyone learned.

## Learning Targets

- Classify a metric as usability (operability) or learning (capability gain) by the construct it measures.
- Pair each claim with a metric that has the validity to support it, never a proxy.
- Read satisfaction as an attitude signal, not as evidence of learning or of effectiveness.
- Combine completion, time, error, and outcome data into a picture no single number could give.

## Opening Case

A team tests a statistics-tutorial prototype and reports that nine of ten learners rated it four or five out of five. The headline writes itself: the module works and learners are learning. The slide deck is built before anyone notices the gap.

The gap is that satisfaction measured how the experience felt, and nothing else. It is silent on whether anyone can now interpret a confidence interval, which is the actual learning goal. The team has a usability-adjacent attitude metric standing in for a learning outcome it never measured. The number is real, but it is answering a question no one asked, and the unasked question (did they learn?) remains open.

## Core Concept

Metrics divide into two families because they measure two different constructs. Usability metrics measure operation; learning metrics measure capability change. Keeping them straight is the whole skill.

| Family | Construct | Typical metrics | Cannot tell you |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Usability | Can people operate it? | task completion, time on task, error rate, satisfaction | Whether anyone learned |
| Learning | Did capability change? | pre/post gain, transfer-item accuracy, retention | Whether the interface was pleasant |

Match each metric to the construct family it actually measures, so a claim can never quietly borrow validity from the wrong family.

Satisfaction is the trap because it feels like a verdict on everything; in fact it is an attitude reading, and attitudes correlate weakly with learning. Learners often rate a hard, effortful lesson lower precisely because it made them think, which **[cognitive load theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_load)** explains as the difference between extraneous load (bad, lowers both satisfaction and learning) and germane load (the productive effort of building schemas, which can lower satisfaction while raising learning). A module that feels easy may have stripped out the very effort that produces durable knowledge.

This is why **[Mayer multimedia learning](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781139164603)** insists that the outcome metric target a meaningful, transferable performance rather than a vanity count. Time on a page is operability data; accuracy on a novel transfer item is learning data. **UDL learner variability** adds the final caution: a single mean can hide a split where the design helped advanced learners and stranded novices, so disaggregating outcomes by subgroup is part of measuring learning honestly, not an optional extra.

## Worked Example: Separating a Usability Metric From a Learning Metric

A designer reports results from testing a statistics-tutorial prototype.

**Before (one metric standing in for everything):**

> Result: 9 of 10 participants rated the module 4 or 5 out of 5 on satisfaction. The module works and learners are learning.

Walk through why this single metric cannot carry both claims:

1. **Classify each metric by what it measures.** Satisfaction is a usability/attitude metric. It measures how the experience felt, not whether anyone learned.
2. **Name the learning claim separately.** "Learners are learning" is a learning-outcome claim and needs its own outcome measure (for example, pre/post performance on the target skill).
3. **Pair each claim with the right metric.** Usability claim to usability metric (task completion, time on task, error rate, satisfaction); learning claim to a learning metric (pre/post gain, transfer item accuracy).
4. **State each result against its own metric.**

**After (matched claims and metrics):**

> Usability: 8 of 10 completed the practice task unaided; mean time on task 2.4 minutes; 2 satisfaction ratings below 4 flagged the feedback panel. Learning: mean pre/post gain on the 5-item interpretation quiz was +1.8 items (3.1 to 4.9), with 7 of 10 improving.

The after version is defensible because each claim is backed by a metric that can actually measure it, and a high satisfaction score is no longer mistaken for a learning result. The before version committed a construct mismatch: it let an attitude rating overclaim a learning outcome.

A learning claim lives or dies on the pre/post gain, and a single mean can hide a subgroup the design failed. ## Decision Rule

Name the claim first, then choose the metric that has the validity to support that exact claim; never let a metric you happen to have collected define the claim you make. If your strongest evidence is satisfaction, your strongest defensible claim is about how the experience felt, not about whether it worked or taught.

Before locking metrics into your moderated usability test script with observation-to-revision trace, check three things:

1. **Construct check:** Does each metric measure the construct the claim is about (operation versus learning)?
2. **Proxy check:** Are you using satisfaction or completion as a stand-in for a learning outcome you never measured?
3. **Disaggregation check:** Could a single mean be hiding a subgroup the design failed?

If any check fails, you are about to report a number that answers a different question than the one you are claiming.

## Common Misreadings

The classic error is treating satisfaction as the master metric, so a well-liked module is declared effective without any outcome data. A second error is reading low satisfaction on a demanding lesson as a design failure, when it may be the signature of productive germane load; the cure for that is checking the learning metric, not softening the lesson. A third is reporting a flattering mean while a subgroup quietly failed, which a single number is structurally unable to reveal.

A metric is an answer to one specific question; using it for another question is how plans overclaim.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Trace one claim from ambition to the metric that earns it. A team wants to assert that an interactive simulation teaches Ohm's law better than the static text it replaces. "Better" is a learning claim, so satisfaction and time on task are off the table; they can describe the experience but not the gain. The right instrument is a transfer measure: items that require applying the relationship to a circuit the learners never saw, scored pre and post, which is the **[Mayer multimedia learning](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781139164603)** standard for meaningful outcomes rather than recall of the worked examples themselves.

Now read the result through **UDL learner variability**. Suppose the mean gain is healthy but driven entirely by learners with strong prior math; novices flatline. A disaggregated table turns that hidden split into a finding: the simulation helps the prepared and overloads the unprepared, which **[cognitive load theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_load)** would predict if the simulation's interface added extraneous demand that experts could absorb and novices could not. The walkthrough's lesson is that the learning claim and the usability claim must be reported side by side, because the usability metric (where novices stalled) often explains the learning metric (why novices did not gain).

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read a results section, keep one column for each claim and one for the metric offered to support it, then mark every pairing where the metric measures a different construct than the claim. Those mismatches are where the report overclaims, and they are almost always a satisfaction or completion number doing a learning number's job.

A strong reader of a metrics plan can answer four questions:

1. For each claim, is the metric measuring operation or capability?
2. Where is a proxy standing in for a learning outcome that was never measured?
3. What does the satisfaction number actually license you to say?
4. Could the headline mean be concealing a subgroup the design failed?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The measurement plan is strong when a reviewer can lay each claim beside its metric and confirm the metric could, on its own, disconfirm the claim. Treat the claim-to-metric pairing as the load-bearing element; a strong satisfaction result attached to a learning claim is not weak evidence but no evidence for that claim at all. Use the annotation tools to flag any claim whose only support is an attitude or completion number, and ask BigAL to compare your pairings against the construct check rather than to add more metrics.

A useful closing line reads: "I separated the usability and learning claims because a single satisfaction rating was carrying both; I rejected the suggestion to report only the overall mean gain, since disaggregating by prior knowledge revealed that novices did not improve."

## Transfer Case

Move the same construct-matching discipline to a program-evaluation context, where the tempting proxy is not satisfaction but attendance or completion rate. A course with high completion has demonstrated that people finished it, not that they learned, exactly as high satisfaction demonstrated enjoyment, not learning. The structure transfers (name the claim, demand a metric with matching validity), but the seductive proxy changes with the setting. A reader who accepts completion as evidence of impact has made the same category mistake as the team that accepted satisfaction as evidence of learning.
