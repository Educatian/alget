## Core Concept

The core idea in this section is that **From Rationale to Testable Hypotheses** should be treated as a relationship among purpose, constraint, evidence, and revision. Purpose names what the learner or audience needs to accomplish. Constraint names what limits the decision. Evidence names the source that makes the decision defensible. Revision shows what changed after the evidence was considered.

Three course anchors shape the reasoning here:

- **Mayer multimedia learning** helps you decide what counts as a meaningful learning or performance demand.
- **cognitive load theory** helps you notice when a design or technology choice creates risk, burden, or unsupported assumptions.
- **UDL learner variability** helps you check whether the artifact works for varied learners, audiences, or use contexts.

Use these anchors as lenses, not as names to drop into a reflection. A useful lens changes what you notice, what you question, and what you revise.

## Worked Example: Turning a Rationale Into a Testable Hypothesis

A designer has redesigned the onboarding screen of a mobile tutoring prototype. The first usability-test plan states only a rationale.

**Before (rationale only):**

> Rationale: We simplified the onboarding screen so it is easier to use. Plan: Show it to learners and confirm it is better.

This cannot be tested. "Easier" names a feeling, not a measurable behavior, so a moderated session cannot confirm or disconfirm it. Walk through the conversion in numbered steps:

1. **Name the design claim behind the rationale.** The claim is that reducing the onboarding screen from 7 fields to 3 will lower first-task abandonment (a **cognitive load theory** prediction about extraneous load).
2. **Choose an observable outcome.** First-task abandonment is observable: did the participant reach the first tutoring task without quitting?
3. **State a direction and a threshold.** Predict the change moves the outcome in a specific direction by a stated amount.
4. **Write the falsifiable hypothesis.**

**After (testable hypothesis):**

> H1: With the 3-field onboarding screen, at least 8 of 10 first-time participants will reach the first tutoring task without abandoning, versus the 4 of 10 baseline observed on the 7-field screen.

The after version is defensible because a moderated session can disconfirm it: if only 5 of 10 succeed, the hypothesis fails and the rationale must be revised, not the data. The change is anchored in **cognitive load theory** (fewer fields, less extraneous load) and leaves an auditable threshold.

## Decision Rule

Use the learning goal first, the media choice second, and the software feature last. A feature is justified only when it reduces unnecessary processing, strengthens useful processing, or makes learner action easier to observe.

Before submitting the moderated usability test script with observation-to-revision trace, slow down and check whether the trace can answer three questions:

1. **Purpose check:** What should the audience be able to do after reading or using the artifact?
2. **Evidence check:** Which exact source, observation, annotation, rubric line, data pattern, or policy boundary justifies the revision?
3. **Revision check:** What changed in the artifact, and what suggestion did you reject or modify?

If you cannot answer all three questions, the artifact may be complete, but it is not yet ready as evidence of learning.

## Common Misreadings

A common mistake is to treat multimedia quality as visual polish. A cleaner screen can still increase extraneous load if the learner action is unclear.

The trace should show what cognitive demand was noticed, what support was requested, and which change improved the learner's path.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Read this section as a small professional decision cycle, not as a list of terms. First, locate the work product: **moderated usability test script with observation-to-revision trace**. Then ask what problem the artifact is supposed to solve inside usability and learning evidence. A strong reader can explain the artifact in plain language before naming any theory: who will use it, what decision it supports, and what would count as evidence that it improved.

Next, connect the artifact to the course anchors: **Mayer multimedia learning**, **cognitive load theory**, **UDL learner variability**. The point is not to cite every anchor. The point is to let one anchor change the artifact. If the anchor does not change wording, layout, evidence, timing, or feedback, it has not yet become design reasoning.

Finally, inspect the revision trail. A weak submission says the artifact was improved. A strong submission shows the earlier version, the issue noticed, the support requested, the decision accepted or rejected, and the reason the final version is better for the intended learner or audience.
