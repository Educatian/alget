# From Rationale to Testable Hypotheses

![From Rationale to Testable Hypotheses textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/ail606-supplement/05-01-from-rationale-to-testable-hypotheses.png)

## Why This Section Matters

Most usability plans fail before a single participant arrives, and they fail at the very first line: the statement of what the test is supposed to find out. Designers who write "we want to confirm the redesign is better" have not written a plan; they have written a hope. A hope cannot be disconfirmed, so the session that follows will appear to succeed no matter what happens. This section teaches the move that converts a design rationale (the reasoning for why you built something a certain way) into a testable hypothesis (a prediction a session can actually contradict). That conversion is the hinge of the whole module: every later choice about participants, tasks, metrics, and analysis only makes sense once you know exactly which prediction you are trying to put at risk.

In AIL 606 the deliverable is a moderated usability test script with observation-to-revision trace, and the hypothesis is its spine. Get it right and the rest of the script almost writes itself. Get it wrong and you will collect data that cannot answer your question.

## Learning Targets

- Convert a design rationale into a falsifiable, directional hypothesis with an observable outcome and a stated threshold.
- Distinguish a prediction the session can disconfirm from a goal it can only restate.
- Ground each hypothesis in a named mechanism (for example, an extraneous-load reduction) so the test checks a theory, not a preference.
- Decide when a result should revise the design and when it should revise the rationale.

## Opening Case

A team has cut a course-registration flow from five screens to three and writes: "Rationale: fewer steps feel less overwhelming, so completion will improve." A reviewer asks one question: "If you run ten learners and the result comes back the wrong way, what number would you have to see?" The team has no answer, because "improve" has no baseline and no target. The rationale is sound design reasoning, but it is not yet a hypothesis. Nothing the session produces could ever count as evidence against it.

The fix is not more polish on the prototype. It is a precise prediction: a baseline completion rate, a direction, and a threshold that a session of a known size could plausibly miss. Until that prediction exists, the moderated script is collecting impressions, not evidence.

## Core Concept

A hypothesis in formative usability work has four parts, and a weak hypothesis is almost always missing one of them.

| Part | Question it answers | Failure when missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mechanism | Why should the change move anything? | "Improvement" with no theory; you cannot interpret a null result |
| Observable outcome | What behavior will you actually measure? | Feelings ("easier") that no session can score |
| Direction | Which way should the outcome move? | A two-sided shrug that confirms itself |
| Threshold | How much movement counts, given your sample? | Any result reads as success |

The mechanism is what links the hypothesis to theory rather than taste. When you predict that trimming a form lowers abandonment, the mechanism is **cognitive load theory**: each extra field adds extraneous processing that competes with the learner's actual goal. **Mayer multimedia learning** sharpens the outcome side by forcing you to name a meaningful performance, not a vanity number; a click is not learning. **UDL learner variability** keeps the threshold honest, because a result that holds for the median learner can still fail learners at the edges of the range, and a single average can hide that.

A practical test of a good hypothesis: write down, before testing, the exact result that would make you abandon the change. If no such result exists, you have a rationale dressed up as a prediction.

## Worked Example: Turning a Rationale Into a Testable Hypothesis

A designer has redesigned the onboarding screen of a mobile tutoring prototype. The first usability-test plan states only a rationale.

**Before (rationale only):**

> Rationale: We simplified the onboarding screen so it is easier to use. Plan: Show it to learners and confirm it is better.

This cannot be tested. "Easier" names a feeling, not a measurable behavior, so a moderated session cannot confirm or disconfirm it. Walk through the conversion in numbered steps:

1. **Name the design claim behind the rationale.** The claim is that reducing the onboarding screen from 7 fields to 3 will lower first-task abandonment (a **cognitive load theory** prediction about extraneous load).
2. **Choose an observable outcome.** First-task abandonment is observable: did the participant reach the first tutoring task without quitting?
3. **State a direction and a threshold.** Predict the change moves the outcome in a specific direction by a stated amount.
4. **Write the falsifiable hypothesis.**

**After (testable hypothesis):**

> H1: With the 3-field onboarding screen, at least 8 of 10 first-time participants will reach the first tutoring task without abandoning, versus the 4 of 10 baseline observed on the 7-field screen.

The after version is defensible because a moderated session can disconfirm it: if only 5 of 10 succeed, the hypothesis fails and the rationale must be revised, not the data. The change is anchored in **cognitive load theory** (fewer fields, less extraneous load) and leaves an auditable threshold.

Walk the rationale-to-hypothesis conversion as predictions you commit to before any data arrives. Each step locks in one part that a weak hypothesis leaves blank.

## Decision Rule

When a result comes back, decide what it revises before you decide what to build. A confirmed hypothesis revises the prototype toward the next risk; a disconfirmed one revises the rationale, because the mechanism you trusted did not hold here. The mistake to avoid is revising the data (explaining away a miss) instead of the reasoning.

Before locking the hypothesis into your moderated usability test script with observation-to-revision trace, run three checks:

1. **Falsifiability check:** Can you state the exact result that would prove you wrong?
2. **Mechanism check:** Which named theory predicts the direction, so a null result still teaches you something?
3. **Calibration check:** Is the threshold reachable but not guaranteed by a sample of your planned size?

If any check fails, the line is a goal, not a hypothesis, and the test will be unable to surprise you.

## Common Misreadings

The most common error is treating the redesign's polish as the hypothesis. "The new screen looks cleaner" predicts nothing about behavior; a cleaner screen can still raise abandonment if the action it asks for is unclear. A second error is the self-confirming wording ("learners will respond to the change"), which is true no matter which way they respond.

A hypothesis earns its place in the script only when it names a number you could fail to reach.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Trace one rationale from idea to inspectable prediction. Suppose a team argues that adding a worked-example walkthrough before a practice set will raise practice accuracy. The rationale is the **Mayer multimedia learning** worked-example effect; that is your mechanism. The observable outcome is accuracy on the first three practice items, not time spent, because time can rise or fall for reasons unrelated to learning. The direction is upward, and the threshold has to respect your sample: with eight participants, "a 20-percentage-point gain over the no-walkthrough baseline" is a claim eight sessions can actually contradict, while "significantly better" is not.

Now stress-test it against **UDL learner variability**. If the gain is concentrated in high-prior-knowledge participants and disappears for novices, the median number hid an expertise-reversal pattern. A walkthrough strong reading would predict not just the average shift but who is expected to move, so a flat result among novices is recognized as a finding rather than noise.

A threshold is only honest if your sample could plausibly miss it. Manipulate the baseline, the predicted gain, and the sample size below to see how many successes your hypothesis demands and how big a swing a single participant produces, which is the calibration check made concrete.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read a draft plan, keep one column for the prediction and one for the kill condition: the specific outcome that would force a redesign of the rationale. The kill condition is what separates a hypothesis from a wish, and a reader who cannot find it in your plan has found your weakest point.

A strong reader of a hypothesis can answer four questions:

1. What behavior, exactly, will the session score?
2. What baseline is the prediction measured against?
3. Which theory supplies the direction, and what would a null result mean?
4. What threshold, given the sample, would count as failure?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The hypothesis is strong when a skeptical reviewer can read it, predict the data table you intend to fill, and name the result that would sink it, all without asking you a question. Treat the wording of the prediction as load-bearing: a vague verb ("improve," "enhance") quietly removes the threshold and makes the test unfalsifiable. Use the annotation tools to flag any outcome you cannot define as a countable behavior, and ask BigAL to compare your wording against the falsifiability check rather than to make the sentence sound more academic.

A useful closing line for the artifact reads: "I revised H1 from 'onboarding will feel easier' to 'at least 8 of 10 reach the first task' because the original had no outcome a session could score; I rejected the suggestion to add a satisfaction target, since satisfaction cannot disconfirm a findability claim."

## Transfer Case

Move the same hypothesis-writing discipline to a high-stakes setting: a compliance course where a failed task is a legal exposure, not a lost click. Here the threshold cannot be a modest majority; the mechanism (load reduction) is the same, but the acceptable failure rate drops toward zero, and the kill condition tightens accordingly. The structure transfers (mechanism, outcome, direction, threshold) but the numbers do not, and a reader who simply copies "8 of 10" into the new context has missed that the constraint, not the form, is what changed.
