# Analyzing Feedback Without Overclaiming

![Analyzing Feedback Without Overclaiming textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/ail606-supplement/05-06-analyzing-feedback-without-overclaiming.png)

## Why This Section Matters

Formative usability tests are built to surface problems cheaply, not to prove things conclusively, and the most damaging mistake at the analysis stage is forgetting that bargain. Five participants can reveal that something is worth fixing; they cannot prove that a feature "fails" or that a population "hates" it. This section is about calibration: scaling the strength of a claim to the strength of the evidence, so a small qualitative signal becomes a candidate problem to verify rather than a verdict to act on. Overclaiming is seductive because a sharp conclusion feels more useful than a hedged one, but a confident claim built on thin data sends a team to redesign the wrong thing.

In AIL 606 the storyboard frame sequence with narration, signaling, and reader-action notes is revised on the basis of what testing actually showed, and the analysis must keep claim strength honest. A finding that says more than the data can support is not a strong finding; it is a liability dressed as a result.

## Learning Targets

- Scale claim strength to sample size and evidence type, distinguishing a candidate issue from a confirmed defect.
- Replace evaluative language (hate, fails, proves) with counts of what was actually observed.
- Separate a frequent signal from a severe one, and name which the evidence supports.
- State the verification step a finding needs before it justifies a costly design change.

## Opening Case

After a five-person test of an onboarding storyboard, a designer writes: "Users hate the progress bar. We proved it fails and should remove it." The sentence is decisive, quotable, and wrong on every count. "Users" generalizes from five people; "hate" and "fails" replace observation with emotion; "proved" claims certainty a formative test cannot deliver; and "remove it" is a product decision riding on all three errors.

What actually happened was smaller and more useful: two participants commented that the progress-bar labels were ambiguous, one ignored it, and nobody failed the task because of it. That is a candidate issue worth a clearer label and a re-test, not a defect worth deletion. The overclaim did not just exaggerate; it pointed the next iteration at the wrong response.

## Core Concept

Calibration means choosing the verb that matches the evidence. The same observation can support a weak or a strong claim, and the analyst's job is to pick the honest one.

| Evidence | Defensible claim | Overclaim to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 of 5 commented, 0 failed | "Candidate label issue; verify" | "Users hate it; it fails" |
| Same issue across varied participants | "Likely problem worth fixing" | "Proven defect" |
| One vivid complaint | "A signal to watch" | "The main problem" |

Two distinctions keep claims calibrated. The first is observation versus emotion: "hate" and "fails" are interpretations, while "two negative comments, zero task failures" is what occurred; only the latter can be defended. The second is frequency versus severity: an issue many people hit lightly is different from an issue one person hit hard, and conflating them ("everyone struggled") hides which response the design needs. **UDL learner variability** sharpens this, because a problem that appears only in one subgroup may be severe for them and invisible in the aggregate, so the count must say who, not just how many.

There is also a theory reason to slow down. **Cognitive load theory** reminds you that a single complaint about effort might reflect productive germane load (the learner thinking hard) rather than a flaw, so a comment is a prompt to investigate, not a confirmed problem. And **Mayer multimedia learning** keeps the analysis tied to whether the storyboard supports the intended learning action, not to whether participants found it pleasant; a well-liked frame that fails the learning goal is still a problem the comments may have missed entirely.

## Worked Example: Rewriting a Finding So It Does Not Overclaim

A designer summarizes results after a 5-participant test of an onboarding storyboard.

**Before (overclaimed finding):**

> Finding: Users hate the progress bar. We proved the progress bar fails and should remove it from the product.

Walk through how to scale the claim to the evidence:

1. **Count what actually happened.** 2 of 5 participants commented negatively on the progress bar; 1 ignored it; 2 used it without comment.
2. **Distinguish observation from preference.** "Hate" and "fails" are not what was observed; what was observed is two negative comments and no completion failures.
3. **Match the claim strength to the sample.** Five participants in a formative test can surface candidate problems, not population-level proof.
4. **State the finding with its uncertainty and a next step.**

**After (calibrated finding):**

> Finding: In this 5-participant formative session, 2 participants found the progress-bar labels ambiguous; no participant failed the task because of it. This is a candidate issue, not a confirmed defect. Next step: add a clearer label and re-test with a larger, more varied sample before deciding to remove it.

The after version is defensible because the claim does not exceed what five sessions can show, and it names a verification step. The before version overgeneralized a small qualitative signal into proof and a product decision, the central failure this section guards against.

## Decision Rule

State only what the count supports, then name the verification a stronger claim would require before you act on it. If a finding uses "proves," "all," "hate," or any verdict, replace it with the number behind it and ask whether that number licenses the action you are about to take.

Before locking the analysis into your storyboard frame sequence with narration, signaling, and reader-action notes, check three things:

1. **Calibration check:** Does the claim's strength match the sample size and evidence type?
2. **Language check:** Have you replaced emotion words with counts of observed behavior?
3. **Verification check:** For any costly change, have you named the re-test that would confirm the issue first?

If any check fails, the finding asserts more than five sessions can defend.

## Common Misreadings

The frequent error is treating a vivid anecdote as the headline; one memorable complaint is easy to overweight precisely because it is memorable, not because it is representative. A second error is sliding from "a problem appeared" to "the feature failed," which jumps from a signal to a verdict in one word. A third is removing a feature on formative evidence alone, when the honest response to a candidate issue is to revise and re-test, not to delete.

A formative test earns the right to say "worth checking," rarely the right to say "proven."

## Textbook Walkthrough

Trace one signal from raw comments to a calibrated finding. In a six-person storyboard test, three participants hesitate at a frame where narration and on-screen text say slightly different things. The tempting write-up is "the narration confuses everyone." The calibrated version counts and locates: "Three of six hesitated at frame 4, where audio and caption differed; none abandoned." That hesitation has a named mechanism in **Mayer multimedia learning**, the redundancy effect, so the finding is not just a complaint but a testable hypothesis about why the frame underperformed.

Now decide the response proportional to the evidence. Because no one failed and the cause is identifiable, the honest move is to align the narration and caption in the storyboard and re-test, not to declare the frame broken. **UDL learner variability** adds a check: were the three who hesitated the multilingual participants, for whom mismatched audio and text is a heavier load? If so, the finding tightens to who is affected and why, which is more useful, and more defensible, than the sweeping "everyone was confused" the raw comments first suggested.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read a findings list, keep one column for the claim as written and one for the count that backs it, then flag every claim whose verb outruns its number. The gap between the verb and the count is the overclaim, and it is usually where a team is about to spend effort it cannot justify.

A strong reader of an analysis can answer four questions:

1. What exact count or behavior supports this claim?
2. Is the verb (candidate, likely, proven) calibrated to that evidence?
3. Is the issue frequent, severe, or both, and for whom?
4. What verification would a costlier response require before acting?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The analysis is strong when a reviewer can trace every claim back to a number and agree the claim says neither more nor less than that number allows. Treat the verb in each finding as the load-bearing element; "proves" and "fails" quietly upgrade a candidate signal into a mandate. Use the annotation tools to flag any finding stated in emotion words, and ask BigAL to compare your claims against the calibration check rather than to make the findings sound more conclusive.

A useful closing line reads: "I rewrote 'users hate the progress bar' as 'two of five found the labels ambiguous, none failed, verify before removing' because the original overclaimed a five-person signal as proof; I rejected the suggestion to cut the feature now, since formative evidence supports a re-test, not deletion."

## Transfer Case

Move the same calibration discipline to interpreting student course evaluations, where a handful of strong comments tempt the same overreach. Three pointed complaints about an assignment are a candidate issue worth examining, not proof that "students hate the course," exactly as two progress-bar comments were a signal, not a verdict. The structure transfers (count it, calibrate the verb, name the verification), but the emotional pull of a sharp negative comment is even stronger in evaluations. A reader who lets the loudest comment set policy has made the same mistake as the designer who deleted a feature on five sessions.
