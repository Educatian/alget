# Participant Profiles and Sampling

![Participant Profiles and Sampling textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/ail606-supplement/05-02-participant-profiles-and-sampling.png)

## Why This Section Matters

A usability finding is only as trustworthy as the people who produced it. You can run a flawless session, log every behavior, and still draw a false conclusion if the participants do not stand in for the learners the design is actually for. This is the validity problem at the heart of sampling: who you recruit silently determines which problems can even appear. Recruit experts and the friction novices feel never surfaces; recruit only the eager and you miss the reluctant learner the course most needs to reach. This section treats the participant profile as a design decision with consequences, not as a logistics chore solved by "whoever is available."

In AIL 606 the moderated usability test script with observation-to-revision trace must say not just what you tested but who you tested it on, and why those people make a found problem likely to transfer to the real audience. A clean session with the wrong sample is worse than a messy one with the right sample, because it overclaims with confidence.

## Learning Targets

- Define a target population by the trait that drives the design's usability risk, not by convenience.
- Build a screener that recruits for that trait and excludes participants who would mask the problem.
- Use small-sample logic to choose a defensible number of participants per relevant subgroup.
- Span the range of learner variability so a finding is not an artifact of one narrow cluster.

## Opening Case

A designer needs feedback on a self-paced course built for warehouse staff with little prior experience using a learning management system. The fastest people to recruit are five colleagues from the design team. They are sharp, available, and fluent in e-learning, so the session runs smoothly and everyone completes every task. The plan looks validated.

It is not. The colleagues never stumbled where warehouse staff will stumble, because expertise erased exactly the friction the test was meant to find. The smooth session is not evidence that the design works; it is evidence that the wrong people tried it. The sample, chosen for availability, guaranteed a false positive before anyone clicked a button.

## Core Concept

Sampling in formative usability is purposive, not statistical. You are not trying to estimate a population parameter; you are trying to surface design problems, and the math that governs that is the discovery curve. With problems that affect a reasonable share of users, a handful of well-chosen participants exposes most of them, while the next handful mostly repeats what you already saw. The classic rule of thumb (roughly five users per homogeneous group surfaces the bulk of that group's problems) is a budgeting heuristic, not a guarantee, and it collapses the moment your users are not homogeneous.

That caveat is where **UDL learner variability** does real work. If your audience spans novices and frequent users, or differs on a trait that changes how people interact (language, assistive technology, prior schema), then "five users" is five users per meaningfully different group, not five users total. Recruit for the trait that drives risk:

- **Risk-driving trait first.** Screen on the variable most likely to cause failure (prior LMS use, reading level, device), because that is the axis along which problems cluster.
- **Exclude the maskers.** Participants whose expertise or insider knowledge would hide the problem are not "extra data"; they are contamination.
- **Span the range.** Cover the edges of the variability, not just the comfortable middle, since edge learners reveal problems the median never hits.

**Cognitive load theory** explains why the wrong sample misleads: experts have schemas that absorb extraneous load invisibly, so a screen that overloads a novice feels effortless to them. The expert's success is not transferable evidence.

## Worked Example: Revising a Participant Profile and Sampling Plan

A designer testing a corporate-compliance e-learning prototype drafts a recruitment plan.

**Before (convenience recruitment):**

> Plan: Recruit 5 colleagues from our design team who are available this week. They know e-learning, so they will give good feedback.

Walk through why this sample threatens the evidence, step by step:

1. **Name the target population.** The course is for warehouse staff with low prior LMS experience, not designers.
2. **Check representativeness.** Design-team colleagues are expert users; they will not stumble where the real population stumbles, so usability problems stay hidden.
3. **Define a screening criterion.** Recruit by the trait that drives the design risk: prior LMS experience, not availability.
4. **Set a sample that covers known variability.** Cover the range of the real population (a **UDL learner variability** move), not a single convenient cluster.

**After (criterion-based, varied sample):**

> Plan: Recruit 6 warehouse staff screened by prior LMS use: 2 first-time users, 2 occasional users, 2 frequent users. None work in design or training. Screener question: "In the past year, how many online courses have you completed at work?"

The after version is defensible because the sample is chosen for the trait that drives usability risk and deliberately spans learner variability, so a problem found in the session plausibly transfers to the real audience. The before version would have produced a clean session that overclaims, because experts hid the real friction.

The four moves above are not interchangeable; they have a logical order, because you cannot screen for a trait before you have named it, and you cannot size the sample before you know which subgroups must appear. Put the recruitment-design steps in the order a defensible plan follows.

## Decision Rule

Recruit for the trait that breaks the design, then cover its range; let availability decide only among people who already pass the screener. If you find yourself justifying a participant by "they were free" rather than "they match the risk profile," stop and rewrite the screener.

Before locking the sample into your moderated usability test script with observation-to-revision trace, check three things:

1. **Representativeness check:** Does each participant share the trait that drives the usability risk?
2. **Coverage check:** Do your participants span the variability that matters, or cluster in one comfortable subgroup?
3. **Contamination check:** Would any participant's expertise hide a problem the real audience would hit?

If any check fails, the session may run smoothly and still produce evidence that does not transfer.

## Common Misreadings

The frequent error is reading "five users is enough" as a universal license, when it holds only within a single homogeneous group; an audience with three meaningfully different segments needs participants in each. A second error is equating a diverse-looking roster with a valid sample: diversity on traits that do not affect the interaction (favorite color, department) adds nothing, while a missing axis that does affect it (no screen-reader users for an accessibility claim) quietly voids the finding.

A sample is a claim about who your evidence covers; recruit so that claim is true.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Trace one recruitment decision from population to roster. A team is testing a tutoring app aimed at first-generation college students. The risk-driving trait is not age or major; it is unfamiliarity with academic-jargon navigation labels, which **cognitive load theory** predicts will cost these learners extraneous effort that experienced students never spend. So the screener targets that trait directly: it recruits students who have not previously used a campus tutoring portal, and it excludes peer tutors who know the vocabulary.

Now apply **UDL learner variability**. Within first-generation students there is still a range: some are confident readers, some are multilingual and parse English jargon more slowly. A walkthrough plan covers both ends rather than filling all six slots with confident readers, because a label that confuses the slower reader is precisely the finding worth keeping. The roster, read back, is a sentence: "Six first-generation students, none prior portal users, spanning confident to slower readers." That sentence is what makes any problem they hit credible for the real audience.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read a recruitment plan, keep one column for who is included and one for who is excluded, and for each entry ask which problems that choice makes visible or invisible. The exclusions are usually more revealing than the inclusions; a plan that excludes the very learners who would struggle has engineered its own clean result.

A strong reader of a sampling plan can answer four questions:

1. What single trait most threatens this design, and does the screener recruit for it?
2. Which subgroups must appear for the finding to transfer?
3. Who, if recruited, would hide the problem rather than reveal it?
4. How many participants per group, and what discovery logic justifies that number?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The sampling section is strong when a reviewer can read the screener alone and predict which learners will and will not appear, then agree those are the right people for the claim you intend to make. Treat the screener question as the artifact's load-bearing element; a vague criterion ("some experience") lets the wrong people through and reintroduces the contamination you were guarding against. Use the annotation tools to flag any slot justified by availability, and ask BigAL to compare your roster against the coverage check rather than to suggest more participants.

A useful closing line reads: "I changed the sample from five available colleagues to six warehouse staff screened by prior LMS use because the colleagues' expertise would have hidden the navigation problem; I rejected the suggestion to add two designers as a comparison group, since their data could not transfer to the real audience."

## Transfer Case

Move the same sampling logic to a classroom field test where you cannot screen freely and must take the students enrolled. The discipline does not disappear; it shifts from recruitment to interpretation. You now document which subgroups your fixed roster happens to cover and which it misses, and you scope every claim to the segments actually present. A reader who treats "the whole class participated" as automatic representativeness has missed that an intact class is itself a convenience sample, with its own blind spots to name.
