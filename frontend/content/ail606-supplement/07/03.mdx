## Why This Section Matters

A prototype that logs learner behavior is collecting personal data, whether or not the designer thinks of it that way. The default temptation is to capture everything ("we might want it later"), but that default inverts the ethical and legal order: data collection should follow from a question, not precede it. This section teaches data minimization as a design discipline. The principle, central to FERPA's protection of education records and to the GDPR notion of purpose limitation, is that you collect the smallest amount of the least identifiable data needed to answer a stated question, and you bind its use and retention to what learners agreed to.

For a learning designer, telemetry is not an IT afterthought. The fields you choose to log, the granularity, the identifiers, and the retention window are design decisions with consequences for re-identification risk, learner trust, and what claims you are later allowed to make. Getting the boundary right is part of designing the artifact, not a checkbox after it ships.

## Learning Targets

- Apply data minimization: derive the logging plan from a stated question rather than from technical capability.
- Distinguish direct identifiers, indirect identifiers, and pseudonymous keys, and explain how combinations enable re-identification.
- Set purpose limitation and a retention window that match the consent learners actually gave.
- Reject "collect now, decide later" requests and state the harm they create.

## Opening Case

A team is excited about the analytics dashboard their prototype could produce, so the first logging plan is: capture the full clickstream, keystroke timing, screen recordings, and each learner's name and email, stored indefinitely "so we can analyze it later." Nothing here is tied to a question. Names and email maximize identifiability; screen recordings can capture incidental third parties; indefinite storage means the data outlives any purpose and any consent. The plan feels thorough, but thoroughness in collection is precisely the risk. The work of this section is to replace "log everything" with "log what one question needs, and no more."

## Core Concept

Telemetry design turns on four boundaries, each answering a different question:

- **Purpose:** Which single, stated design question does this field help answer? A field with no question behind it has no justification.
- **Minimization:** What is the smallest data that answers the question at the lowest identifiability? Often a counter or a timestamp suffices where a recording was proposed.
- **Identifiability:** Is the key a direct identifier (name, email), an indirect identifier (a quasi-identifier like timestamp + class + device that can be triangulated), or a pseudonymous random id? Re-identification usually happens not through one field but through the *combination* of several, so minimizing fields lowers risk multiplicatively, not additively.
- **Retention and use:** How long is the data kept, and is its use bound to the consent given? Indefinite retention and scope creep ("we collected it for X, now we use it for Y") are the two most common boundary violations.

The course anchors connect privacy to learning quality, not just compliance. **Cognitive load theory** is relevant because intrusive data capture (a visible recorder, a consent wall that reappears) can itself add extraneous load and alter the very behavior you are trying to measure. **UDL learner variability** matters because identifiability harms are unevenly distributed: a small cohort, a learner from a marginalized group, or a class small enough that "the only student who took it on a phone" is identifiable, all bear more re-identification risk from the same fields. **Mayer's principles** remind you that telemetry should serve a learning question; data with no link to a learning decision is collection for its own sake.

## Worked Example

Trace one logging plan from over-collection to a defensible boundary.

**Before (logging plan, first draft):**

> Log everything the prototype can capture: full clickstream, keystroke timing, screen recording, and each learner's name and email, stored indefinitely so we can analyze it later.

1. No field is tied to a stated question, so none can be justified.
2. Name plus email are direct identifiers; combined with timestamps they make every record trivially re-identifiable.
3. Indefinite retention sets no purpose limit, so the data outlives any consent.

**After (minimized logging plan):**

> Question: does the new hint button reduce time-to-correct on item 4? Log only item-4 response time and hint-click events, keyed to a random pseudonymous id, retained for the study term and used only to improve the lesson, as stated in the consent form.

The revised plan collects exactly two fields the question needs, drops direct identifiers in favor of a random key, and binds use and retention to the consent learners gave. A reviewer can see the boundary and check it against the consent text.

One rejected request: a teammate asked to also store email "for follow-up." I declined because no current question requires it; adding email would reintroduce a direct identifier and widen re-identification risk with no design justification. If a follow-up study is later approved, it can seek its own consent rather than borrowing this one.

## Decision Rule

Derive the plan from the question, then test each proposed field against the four boundaries:

1. **Purpose check:** Which stated question does this field answer? No question, no field.
2. **Minimization check:** Is there a less identifiable field that answers the same question (a count instead of a recording, a pseudonymous key instead of a name)?
3. **Combination check:** Could this field, joined with the others, re-identify a learner in the smallest plausible cohort? If so, coarsen or drop it.
4. **Retention-and-use check:** Does the retention window end when the purpose ends, and is use confined to the consent given?

A field that fails any check is not a "nice to have"; it is an uncompensated risk to the learner.

## Common Misreadings

One misreading is that anonymization is a switch you flip after collection. Removing names does not anonymize a dataset if quasi-identifiers remain; a record keyed by class, date, and device can still single out one learner. A second misreading treats consent as a blanket permission. Consent is scoped: data gathered "to improve this lesson" cannot be quietly repurposed for a marketing model or a different study. Boundary work is about matching collection, identifiability, retention, and use to the specific purpose and the specific consent.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the boundaries to a discussion-board prototype. A first plan logs every post's full text, author name, IP address, and timestamp, kept forever, to study engagement. Walk the checks. Purpose: the question is "does a prompt scaffold raise the rate of evidence-based replies?" That needs a coded reply-type per post, not the full verbatim text plus identifiers. Minimization: replace stored full text with a derived code (evidence-based: yes/no) computed at collection time; drop IP entirely. Combination: author name plus timestamp in a 12-person seminar re-identifies everyone, so swap names for a random per-study id. Retention: keep coded counts for the study term, then delete. The revised plan answers the engagement question while collecting a fraction of the data and almost none of the identifiability, which is exactly the trade data minimization is designed to produce.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

For each logged field, write two things: the question it serves and the worst-case re-identification it enables in the smallest cohort you can imagine. A strong reader notices that the danger usually lives in the join, not the field: response-time alone is harmless, but response-time plus device plus a four-person class is not. The skill is to read a logging plan adversarially, as an attacker who wants to single out one learner, and to keep only the fields that survive that reading while still answering the question.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The telemetry plan is strong when every field traces to a stated question, when identifiers are minimized to the lowest level the question allows, and when the README records the retention window and the consent scope alongside the field list. A reviewer should be able to see, for at least one field you dropped, the re-identification risk that justified dropping it. Treat the dashboard's visual appeal as the last concern; the load-bearing layer is the field-by-field justification against the four boundaries.

## Transfer Case

Move the plan into a stricter jurisdiction or a smaller cohort, the two constraints that most often break a telemetry design. In a five-student pilot, fields that were safe in a 200-student deployment (timestamp, device, completion order) may now re-identify everyone, so the same question forces a coarser plan: bucket timestamps, drop device, report only aggregates. The purpose stays stable while the identifiability boundary tightens. Testing your plan against the smallest, most regulated context is how you confirm you understand minimization as a relationship between data and cohort, not as a fixed list of "safe" fields.
