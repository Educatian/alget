# Turning Designs Into Study Questions

![Turning Designs Into Study Questions textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/ail606-supplement/07-01-turning-designs-into-study-questions.png)

## Why This Section Matters

Most design decisions arrive disguised as preferences. A storyboard frame "feels clearer" with a signaling arrow; narration "seems better" before the visual; a control "should" sit on the right. None of those statements can be confirmed or disconfirmed, which means none of them can guide a revision you could defend to a committee, a client, or a journal reviewer. Research translation begins the moment you convert a design preference into a study question: a statement narrow enough that a small amount of evidence could change your mind.

This section teaches the conversion itself. You will take a feature of a storyboard frame sequence (narration timing, a signaling cue, a reader-action prompt) and rewrite the design hope as a falsifiable question that names a manipulation, a measurable learner behavior, and a comparison. The skill matters because a learning designer who cannot state what would prove them wrong is not designing with evidence; they are decorating.

## Learning Targets

- Distinguish a design preference (untestable) from a design hypothesis (testable) using the manipulation/measure/comparison test.
- Rewrite a vague storyboard note as a study question with a named independent variable, an observable dependent variable, and a baseline.
- Choose a single primary measure and justify excluding plausible-but-irrelevant outcomes.
- Predict in advance which result would send you back to revise the frame.

## Opening Case

A designer hands you frame 3 of a statistics lesson. The narration explains a residual plot; a new red arrow now points at the simulation's "Run" control. The note reads: "Added an arrow so the lesson is clearer." The screen is attractive and the arrow is well placed. Yet the note hides three unanswered questions: clearer for whom, clearer in what observable way, and clearer compared with what? Until those are answered, "clearer" is a feeling the designer has, not a property the frame possesses. Your job is to turn that feeling into something a five-learner tryout could actually test.

## Core Concept

A study question is the operationalized form of a design claim. It has four moving parts, and a useful question makes all four explicit:

- **Independent variable (the manipulation):** the single design element you change, stated precisely enough to build two versions. "An arrow" is vague; "a red signaling arrow on frame 3, appearing after the narration ends" is buildable.
- **Dependent variable (the observable):** the learner behavior you can see and record. Replace internal states ("understands," "feels confident") with actions ("selects the correct label," "time to first click on Run").
- **Comparison (the baseline):** what the manipulation is measured against, usually a no-treatment or alternative-treatment version. Without a comparison, any result is uninterpretable.
- **Population and condition:** who is tested and under what constraint, because the same arrow may help novices and bore experts.

The course anchors do specific work here. **Mayer's signaling principle** predicts that cueing essential material reduces extraneous search, which tells you the arrow's plausible mechanism and therefore the right measure (search time, not enjoyment). **Cognitive load theory** warns that an added cue is not free: if it competes with the narration for attention, it can raise extraneous load rather than lower it, so your question should be able to detect harm, not only benefit. **UDL learner variability** reminds you that a single primary measure can mask divergent effects, so you specify the population whose behavior the question is really about.

A practical heuristic: if you cannot describe the two screens a developer would build to test your question, the question is still a preference.

## Worked Example

Follow one storyboard note as it becomes a question a tryout can answer.

**Before (design note on the storyboard):**

> Frame 3 adds a signaling arrow to point learners toward the simulation control. I think this will make the lesson clearer.

1. "Clearer" names no behavior, so no observation could confirm or refute it.
2. There is no comparison, so even a positive reaction would be uninterpretable.
3. The mechanism is unnamed, so we cannot pick the right measure.

**After (study question tied to the same frame):**

> Among first-time learners, does placing a red signaling arrow on frame 3 (appearing after the narration ends) reduce the median time to start the first simulation action, compared with an identical frame without the arrow?

This version names the manipulation (red arrow, frame 3, post-narration), the observable (median time-to-first-action), the comparison (identical no-arrow frame), and the population (first-time learners). A developer could build both screens; a reviewer can see exactly what result would matter. The prespecified decision rule is: if the arrow version does not reduce median time-to-first-action by at least a noticeable margin, the arrow is not earning its place and frame 3 reverts.

One rejected addition: a teammate proposed also measuring overall lesson enjoyment. I excluded it because enjoyment cannot tell me whether *the arrow* reduced search time; a more enjoyable lesson with a slower start would falsely credit the cue. Keeping one primary measure tied to the hypothesized mechanism is what makes the answer actionable.

## Decision Rule

A storyboard note is ready to become a study question only when you can fill all four slots: manipulation, observable, comparison, population. Apply three checks before you treat any note as testable:

1. **Buildability check:** Can you describe the two versions a developer would create? If not, the manipulation is underspecified.
2. **Falsifiability check:** Can you state, in advance, the result that would make you revise or remove the feature? If no result would change your mind, you have a preference, not a hypothesis.
3. **Mechanism check:** Does the chosen measure track the reason the feature should work? Enjoyment does not test search; recall does not test motivation.

If any check fails, the note is not yet evidence-ready, however polished the frame looks.

## Common Misreadings

The frequent error is to treat a more detailed design note as a study question. Adding adjectives ("a clean, prominent, well-placed arrow") increases specificity about the *artifact* without adding a measure or a comparison, so the claim remains untestable. A second error is question inflation: asking whether the redesign "improves learning" when a single-frame cue change can only plausibly move a narrow, proximal behavior. Scope the question to what the manipulation can actually cause.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the conversion to a fresh case. A storyboard note says: "Move the narration to start before the diagram appears so learners are prepared." First name the manipulation: narration onset relative to diagram onset (before versus simultaneous). Next pick an observable tied to a mechanism: because the temporal contiguity principle predicts that separating related words and pictures in time raises integration cost, the right measure is whether learners correctly match diagram parts to spoken labels, not whether they "feel prepared." Add a comparison (narration-first frame versus simultaneous frame) and a population (learners new to the diagram). The note "start narration earlier" becomes: "For learners new to the diagram, does narration-before-diagram lower the proportion of mislabeled parts, compared with simultaneous presentation?" That is now buildable, falsifiable, and mechanism-aligned.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

Keep a two-column note: in the left column, the design preference as written; in the right column, the four slots you would need to fill to test it. A strong reader can look at any storyboard annotation and immediately spot which slot is missing (usually the comparison or the observable) and propose the minimal rewrite that supplies it. By the end of this section, you should be able to read a stack of design notes and triage them: which are already testable, which need one slot, and which are pure taste that no tryout could ever adjudicate.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The artifact is strong when each design note on the storyboard is paired with a study question whose four slots are filled and whose prespecified decision rule is stated. Visual polish is the last layer; the load-bearing layer is the manipulation-measure-comparison chain. If a note cannot be converted, that is itself a finding: mark it as a preference to resolve by judgment rather than by data, so you do not waste a tryout on a question no result could settle.

## Transfer Case

Move the same skill to a context with no tryout budget at all. When you cannot run even a five-person test, the conversion still pays off: a precise study question lets you reason from prior evidence. Because Mayer's signaling experiments already show cueing reduces search for novices, a well-scoped question ("would an arrow reduce search for *these* novices on *this* frame?") can be answered provisionally from the literature, with the prediction logged so a later tryout can confirm or overturn it. The preference-to-question discipline is what makes borrowed evidence usable instead of ornamental.
