## Core Concept

The core idea in this section is that **Small-N Testing and Design Claims** should be treated as a relationship among purpose, constraint, evidence, and revision. Purpose names what the learner or audience needs to accomplish. Constraint names what limits the decision. Evidence names the source that makes the decision defensible. Revision shows what changed after the evidence was considered.

Three course anchors shape the reasoning here:

- **Mayer multimedia learning** helps you decide what counts as a meaningful learning or performance demand.
- **cognitive load theory** helps you notice when a design or technology choice creates risk, burden, or unsupported assumptions.
- **UDL learner variability** helps you check whether the artifact works for varied learners, audiences, or use contexts.

Read the anchors as competing checks on quality. One may highlight the learning demand, another may expose a risk, and another may help you test transfer.

## Worked Example

Trace one finding from an overreaching claim to a claim a five-person test can actually carry.

**Before (claim drafted after five moderated sessions):**

> Our usability test proves the redesigned lesson improves learning, since testers did better and liked it.

1. Annotation: five sessions cannot support an efficacy or population claim, and "did better" has no baseline or measure.
2. Decision point: what did we actually observe, and what design change does it justify?
3. Revision move: scope the claim to observed friction and pair it with a revision.

**After (observation-to-revision trace entry):**

| Observation | Frequency | Revision | Recheck |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Clicked Back instead of Next at frame 2 | 4 of 5 | Relabel button to "Continue" and move it right | Watch frame-2 path next round |
| Hovered glossary popup with no use | 1 of 5 | None yet; low-frequency signal | Flag to watch |

The after version scopes each claim to what five moderated sessions can bear: it names the observed behavior, its frequency, the design change it triggered, and a recheck. I rejected adding a statement that the lesson "improves learning," because no outcome comparison was run and the sample cannot support it.

## Decision Rule

Use the learning goal first, the media choice second, and the software feature last. A feature is justified only when it reduces unnecessary processing, strengthens useful processing, or makes learner action easier to observe.

A strong submission should survive three quick tests:

1. **Purpose check:** What should the audience be able to do after reading or using the artifact?
2. **Evidence check:** Which exact source, observation, annotation, rubric line, data pattern, or policy boundary justifies the revision?
3. **Revision check:** What changed in the artifact, and what suggestion did you reject or modify?

If you cannot answer all three questions, the artifact may be complete, but it is not yet ready as evidence of learning.

## Common Misreadings

Students sometimes describe the final prototype but skip the design decision that made the prototype worth revising.

In this course, quality means that design reasoning can be traced from learning goal to media choice to revision.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Read this section as a small professional decision cycle, not as a list of terms. First, locate the work product: **moderated usability test script with observation-to-revision trace**. Then ask what problem the artifact is supposed to solve inside research translation. A strong reader can explain the artifact in plain language before naming any theory: who will use it, what decision it supports, and what would count as evidence that it improved.

Next, connect the artifact to the course anchors: **Mayer multimedia learning**, **cognitive load theory**, **UDL learner variability**. The point is not to cite every anchor. The point is to let one anchor change the artifact. If the anchor does not change wording, layout, evidence, timing, or feedback, it has not yet become design reasoning.

Finally, inspect the revision trail. A weak submission says the artifact was improved. A strong submission shows the earlier version, the issue noticed, the support requested, the decision accepted or rejected, and the reason the final version is better for the intended learner or audience.
