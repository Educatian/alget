# Ethical Reporting of AI Use

![Ethical Reporting of AI Use textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/ail606-supplement/07-07-ethical-reporting-of-ai-use.png)

## Why This Section Matters

When a learning designer uses a generative model to draft an item, summarize a source, or propose a layout, the question is not whether to disclose but how to disclose well. A good disclosure does three things at once: it scopes the AI's contribution to specific, bounded decisions; it makes the human verification visible; and it keeps accountability with the person, not the tool. The failure modes are predictable: a vague "AI was used" that names nothing checkable, and an accountability-shedding claim that the output is "objective" because a machine produced it. This section teaches the bounded, honest disclosure that publication venues, granting agencies, and course policies increasingly require.

This matters because disclosure is where intellectual honesty becomes operational. A reviewer cannot judge the provenance of your work from a slogan; they need to know which decision the AI touched and how you checked it. A learning designer who can write that disclosure precisely is demonstrating the same judgment the rest of the chapter teaches: evidence over assertion, accountability over hand-waving.

## Learning Targets

- Distinguish a vague disclosure ("AI was used") from a bounded one that names the decision, the AI contribution, and the verification.
- Reject accountability-shedding language (the output is "objective" because AI made it) and keep responsibility with the human.
- Document accept/reject calls on AI suggestions, with the reason for each.
- Decide the right granularity of disclosure: enough for a reviewer to judge provenance, not a full transcript.

## Opening Case

A draft report ends with: "AI was used in this project. All analysis was AI-generated and is therefore objective." Two sentences, two failures. "Was used" names nothing a reviewer could verify, so it discloses in form but not in substance. "Therefore objective" tries to launder accountability through the tool, as if a model's involvement could relieve the designer of responsibility for choices the designer in fact made. The disclosure reads as a shield rather than a record. This section replaces it with a statement a reviewer can actually use to judge provenance.

## Core Concept

An ethical AI-use disclosure is built from four elements, each closing a specific gap:

- **Scope:** which bounded decision the AI supported (e.g., drafting alternative item stems for screen 4), not a blanket "the project."
- **Contribution:** what the AI actually produced, stated concretely enough that a reader can picture it (three candidate stems, a summary, a layout option).
- **Accept/reject calls:** which suggestions you kept, which you changed, which you discarded, and the reason for each, because the editorial judgment is yours and is the point of interest.
- **Verification and accountability:** how you checked the output and an explicit statement that you remain responsible for the result. Accountability does not transfer to a tool.

The unifying principle is that disclosure exists so others can judge provenance, which means it must be specific and honest rather than reassuring. A disclosure that makes the work sound more authoritative ("objective," "validated by AI") is doing the opposite of its job.

The course anchors inform even this. **Mayer's coherence principle** argues for a disclosure that is complete but not bloated; a full chat log adds extraneous material that obscures the decisions a reviewer needs. **Cognitive load theory** supports a structured disclosure (decision, contribution, calls, verification) that a reader can parse quickly rather than a narrative they must mine. **UDL learner variability** is relevant when AI assists with content for diverse learners: a disclosure should note where you checked that AI-drafted material did not encode bias or assume a single learner profile, since that is a verification a reviewer will rightly want.

## Worked Example

Trace one AI-use disclosure from a vague, accountability-shedding line to an honest, bounded statement.

**Before (disclosure excerpt, first draft):**

> AI was used in this project. All analysis was AI-generated and is therefore objective.

1. "Was used" names nothing checkable, so it discloses in form only.
2. "Therefore objective" tries to hand accountability to the tool, which a designer cannot do.
3. There is no scope, no contribution detail, and no verification.

**After (bounded disclosure excerpt):**

> AI-use note: To revise the screen-4 item stems, I prompted a generative model for three alternative stems. I accepted two after checking them against the rubric, rewrote one for reading level, and discarded one that tested a construct outside the lesson. I verified all final wording and remain responsible for the items.

The revised version is honest and bounded: it names the decision (screen-4 stems), what the AI produced (three stems), the accept/reject/rewrite calls with reasons, and the human verification. Accountability stays with the designer.

One rejected claim: that the output was "objective." I removed it because the items reflect choices I made (which stems to keep, how to rewrite for reading level, which construct to exclude) and I must answer for them. The disclosure now lets a reviewer judge provenance instead of trusting a slogan.

## Decision Rule

Before any AI-use statement ships, test it against four questions:

1. **Scope check:** Does it name the specific bounded decision the AI touched, rather than "the project"? Vague scope means no real disclosure.
2. **Contribution check:** Could a reader picture what the AI actually produced? If not, add the concrete detail.
3. **Calls check:** Does it record what you accepted, changed, and rejected, with reasons? The editorial judgment is the part worth disclosing.
4. **Accountability check:** Does the statement keep responsibility with you, with no claim that the tool's involvement confers objectivity or relieves you of judgment?

A disclosure that fails the accountability check is not merely incomplete; it misrepresents who is responsible, which is an ethical fault, not a stylistic one.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading treats disclosure as a confession to be minimized: say "AI was used" and move on. Minimal disclosure protects no one, because it gives a reviewer nothing to evaluate. The second misreading treats disclosure as a credential: cite the AI to make the work sound more rigorous ("validated by AI," "objective"). That inverts the purpose; disclosure is for scrutiny, not endorsement, and accountability-shedding language is a red flag a reviewer is trained to catch. Honest disclosure is neither apologetic nor self-promoting; it is a precise record of what the tool did and what you decided.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the standard to AI assistance with a literature summary. A first-draft disclosure says: "AI summarized the sources." Walk the checks. Scope: name which sources and for what purpose (the AI drafted one-sentence summaries of five readings to seed the related-work section). Contribution: state what it produced (five candidate summaries). Calls: record that you verified each summary against the original source, corrected two that misstated a finding, and discarded one for a source the AI could not actually access (a hallucination you caught). Accountability: state that you confirmed every cited claim against the primary text and are responsible for the related-work section. The revised disclosure does the real work: it tells a reviewer exactly where the AI touched the manuscript, surfaces that you caught a fabrication, and leaves no doubt about who stands behind the citations.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

Read a disclosure as a skeptical reviewer asking "what can I actually check here?" A strong reader spots the empty disclosure (no scope, no verification) and the dangerous one (accountability handed to the tool) and can rewrite either into a bounded, checkable statement. The tell of a good disclosure is that it makes the work *easier to scrutinize*, not harder: it points the reviewer to the exact decisions where AI was involved and the exact checks the human performed. A disclosure that makes you trust the work less, but understand it more, is doing its job.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The disclosure excerpt is strong when it scopes the AI contribution to a bounded decision, records the accept/reject calls with reasons, names the verification, and keeps accountability with the author, all without padding it into a transcript. The README should make it possible for a reviewer to locate, for at least one decision, exactly what the AI produced and how it was checked. Persuasive polish is beside the point here; the load-bearing quality is honesty a reviewer can verify.

## Transfer Case

Carry bounded disclosure into a high-stakes venue with a formal AI policy, such as a journal that requires authors to specify model, prompts, and the role AI played. The transfer is to scale the same four elements without changing their logic: scope becomes a per-section accounting, verification becomes a documented procedure, and accountability becomes an explicit author statement that AI is not credited as an author and that the humans are responsible. The discipline transfers because the ethic is constant, AI is a tool whose contributions you scope, check, and own, while the formality of the venue only changes how much detail that ethic must be expressed in.
