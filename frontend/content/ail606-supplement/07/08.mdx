# Research Translation Studio

![Research Translation Studio textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/ail606-supplement/07-08-research-translation-studio.png)

## Why This Section Matters

The previous seven sections each isolated one move: stating a study question, aligning evidence to claims, bounding telemetry, scoping small-N findings, coding reflection, packaging for reuse, disclosing AI use. The studio asks for the hardest thing: putting them together into a single, unbroken argument. Research translation is the practice of carrying a research finding all the way through to a defensible, evidenced, disclosed design decision, with no broken link in the middle. A finding that is cited but never changes a design, or a design that is built but never tested, is a chain with a gap, and the gap is where credibility leaks out.

This matters because integration is where most design reports fail even when each part is competent. A storyboard can be beautiful, a finding can be real, and a disclosure can be honest, yet the report still fails if the finding never touched the storyboard or the storyboard was never tested. The studio is the rehearsal for producing work whose every claim traces backward to a source and forward to evidence.

## Learning Targets

- Assemble a complete translation chain: finding to principle to design decision to evidence to disclosed revision.
- Locate the broken link in an argument where a finding is cited but never changes a decision, or a decision is made but never tested.
- Reject decorative citations that do not alter any design choice.
- Show that each design decision traces backward to a source and forward to evidence.

## Opening Case

A studio summary reads: "Mayer's redundancy principle is important, so I built a polished storyboard with narration and on-screen text. It looks great." Read it as a chain and the break is obvious: the principle is named, but the screens still duplicate narration and on-screen text, which is exactly what the redundancy principle warns against. The finding was cited and then ignored; it changed nothing. There is also no evidence the design worked, so the forward link is missing too. The studio's job is to repair both: make the finding actually alter a decision, and tie that decision to evidence.

## Core Concept

A research-translation argument is a chain whose links must all hold:

| Link | What it asserts | The break that voids it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Finding | A research result relevant to a design choice | Cited but never used |
| Principle | The design rule the finding implies | Named without application |
| Design decision | The specific change made to the artifact | Made without grounding or without testing |
| Evidence | The observation that tests the decision | Decision asserted, never checked |
| Disclosed revision | The change, with AI assistance disclosed and verified | Provenance hidden |

An argument is only as strong as its weakest link. The two most common breaks are the *back* break (a finding decorates the report but alters no decision) and the *forward* break (a decision is made but never tested). Integration means walking the whole chain and confirming that each link both follows from the one before and supports the one after.

The course anchors are the raw material the chain translates. **Mayer's redundancy principle** is a finding that becomes a principle (remove duplicated on-screen text where narration carries content) and then a concrete decision (drop captions on specific frames). **Cognitive load theory** explains *why* the decision should help (it lowers extraneous load), which is what lets the evidence be interpreted rather than merely collected. **UDL learner variability** keeps the chain honest about scope: a decision that helps the tested learners may need rechecking for others, so the evidence link names whose behavior was observed.

## Worked Example

The studio asks you to integrate the whole chapter into one argument. Trace a studio package from a broken chain to a complete research-translation argument.

**Before (studio summary, first pass):**

> Mayer's redundancy principle is important, so I built a polished storyboard with narration and on-screen text. It looks great.

1. The finding is cited but never changed a decision; the screens still duplicate narration and text.
2. There is no evidence the design worked, so the forward link is missing.
3. "It looks great" is polish, not a tested outcome.

**After (integrated translation argument):**

> Finding: redundancy of identical narration and on-screen text raises extraneous load (Mayer).
> Principle: remove duplicate on-screen text where narration carries the content.
> Design decision: on frames 2 to 5, drop the duplicated caption and keep only key labels.
> Evidence: in a five-person moderated test, 4 of 5 learners reported less re-reading and started the next action sooner.
> Disclosed revision: I used an AI tool to draft the revised captions and verified each against the rubric myself.

The revised version is a complete chain: each design decision traces back to a finding and forward to evidence, and the AI support is disclosed. The small-N evidence is scoped honestly (a 4-of-5 friction reduction, not an efficacy claim).

One rejected addition: a second, unrelated theory citation. I left it out because it would not change any decision in this package and would only decorate the argument, reintroducing the very back break the studio exists to eliminate.

## Decision Rule

Audit the whole chain before submitting, link by link:

1. **Back-link check:** Does every cited finding actually change a design decision? If a citation alters nothing, remove it or use it.
2. **Forward-link check:** Does every design decision connect to evidence that tests it? An untested decision is a hope, not a result.
3. **Continuity check:** Can you trace any single decision both backward to its source and forward to its evidence without a gap?
4. **Disclosure check:** Is the AI assistance in any link scoped, verified, and owned?

A chain that fails any check has a leak, and one leak undermines the credibility of the links that do hold.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading equates more citations with a stronger argument. Extra citations that change no decision are decoration; they dilute the chain rather than reinforce it. The second misreading equates a polished, complete-looking package with a sound one. A package can have every section filled in and still contain a back break (a finding that did nothing) or a forward break (a decision never tested). Integration is judged by the continuity of the chain, not by the number of parts present or the quality of their finish.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Apply the integration audit to a second package, this one about feedback timing. The student cites a finding that immediate feedback can aid skill acquisition, then writes that the prototype "gives good feedback." Audit the chain. Back link: the finding implies a principle (deliver feedback immediately after the response on practice items), but the prototype delays feedback to the end, so the finding was cited and then contradicted; repair it by moving feedback to immediately after each item. Forward link: there is no evidence, so add a small moderated check of whether learners use the immediate feedback before the next item. Disclosure: if AI drafted the feedback text, scope and verify it. Now the chain runs from finding to principle to a decision that matches the principle to evidence that tests it to a disclosed revision. The walkthrough shows that integration is repair work: finding each break and closing it, not adding more material.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

Read any design argument as a chain and put your finger on each link in turn, asking "does this follow from the last, and does it support the next?" A strong reader does not admire the storyboard or count the citations; they hunt for the break, which is almost always a finding that changed nothing or a decision that was never tested. The skill is to see the whole argument as a single load path and to know that the report's strength is set by its weakest link, not by the average quality of its sections.

## Transfer Case

Carry the integration discipline into a full thesis or design-case publication, where the chain must hold across many findings and many decisions at once. The studio's single chain becomes a web: each design decision still traces back to a source and forward to evidence, but now a reviewer can sample any decision in the document and expect both links to hold. The transfer is that the audit scales without changing: instead of checking one chain you spot-check many, and a single back break or untested decision anywhere weakens the whole. Producing work that survives that sampling is the proof that research translation has become a habit rather than a checklist.
