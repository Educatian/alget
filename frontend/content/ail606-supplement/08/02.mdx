## Core Concept

The core idea in this section is that **Feedback Templates for Design Drafts** should be treated as a relationship among purpose, constraint, evidence, and revision. Purpose names what the learner or audience needs to accomplish. Constraint names what limits the decision. Evidence names the source that makes the decision defensible. Revision shows what changed after the evidence was considered.

Three course anchors shape the reasoning here:

- **Mayer multimedia learning** helps you decide what counts as a meaningful learning or performance demand.
- **cognitive load theory** helps you notice when a design or technology choice creates risk, burden, or unsupported assumptions.
- **UDL learner variability** helps you check whether the artifact works for varied learners, audiences, or use contexts.

The anchors should do work in your artifact. If Mayer multimedia learning or cognitive load theory does not change a decision, it is only a citation rather than evidence of judgment.

## Worked Example

Walk through one concrete revision of a feedback template an instructor uses to comment on student design drafts. The artifact is the comment template itself, not a single student's draft.

**Before (the praise-and-vague template).** The original three-line template read: (1) "Great start!" (2) "This is a bit confusing, can you clarify?" (3) "Tighten this up before final." When this template was used on 12 drafts, students revised cosmetics (fonts, headings) but left the design reasoning untouched, because no line pointed to a decision, an evidence source, or a next action.

1. **Diagnose the template against criteria, not tone.** A useful feedback template should name the criterion at stake, locate it in the draft, and request an observable next move. The old line "tighten this up" fails all three. By **cognitive load theory**, vague feedback adds search load: the student must guess what "tighten" means before they can act.
2. **Rewrite each line to criterion -> location -> action.** Line 2 becomes: "Frame 4's narration states the goal but not the learner action; add one sentence naming what the learner does on this screen." Now the student knows the criterion (alignment of narration to learner action), the location (Frame 4), and the move (add one action sentence).
3. **Add a single actionable next step, not three.** The revised template caps instructor comments at one priority revision per draft so the feedback does not itself overload the learner.

**After (the template as criterion-anchored feedback).** Each line follows criterion -> location -> action, with exactly one prioritized revision. Re-used on the next 12 drafts, 10 students changed design reasoning (narration-to-action alignment) rather than only formatting. The change is justified by **cognitive load theory** and is auditable: a reviewer can compare the old line "tighten this up" to the rewritten criterion-located-action line and the shift in what students actually revised.

## Decision Rule

Use the learning goal first, the media choice second, and the software feature last. A feature is justified only when it reduces unnecessary processing, strengthens useful processing, or makes learner action easier to observe.

Use the following check when the artifact looks finished but the reasoning may still be hidden:

1. **Purpose check:** What should the audience be able to do after reading or using the artifact?
2. **Evidence check:** Which exact source, observation, annotation, rubric line, data pattern, or policy boundary justifies the revision?
3. **Revision check:** What changed in the artifact, and what suggestion did you reject or modify?

If you cannot answer all three questions, the artifact may be complete, but it is not yet ready as evidence of learning.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Read this section as a small professional decision cycle, not as a list of terms. First, locate the work product: **storyboard frame sequence with narration, signaling, and reader-action notes**. Then ask what problem the artifact is supposed to solve inside course readiness and facilitation. A strong reader can explain the artifact in plain language before naming any theory: who will use it, what decision it supports, and what would count as evidence that it improved.

Next, connect the artifact to the course anchors: **Mayer multimedia learning**, **cognitive load theory**, **UDL learner variability**. The point is not to cite every anchor. The point is to let one anchor change the artifact. If the anchor does not change wording, layout, evidence, timing, or feedback, it has not yet become design reasoning.

Finally, inspect the revision trail. A weak submission says the artifact was improved. A strong submission shows the earlier version, the issue noticed, the support requested, the decision accepted or rejected, and the reason the final version is better for the intended learner or audience.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: **claim** on the left, **evidence** on the right. The claim is the decision you think the section is teaching. The evidence is the exact phrase, example, annotation, support move, or artifact revision that makes the claim inspectable. By the end of AIL 606 08.02, your notes should make it possible to reconstruct your reasoning without asking you to explain it again.

A strong reader of Feedback Templates for Design Drafts can answer four questions:

1. What is the real decision hidden inside this topic?
2. Which constraint makes the decision difficult?
3. What evidence would persuade a skeptical reviewer?
4. What would you revise if the audience, learner, classroom, or tool changed?
