# Feedback Templates for Design Drafts

![Feedback Templates for Design Drafts textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/ail606-supplement/08-02-feedback-templates-for-design-drafts.png)

## Why This Section Matters

Feedback is the single most studied lever in education, and also the most reliably wasted. Hattie and Timperley's synthesis found that feedback's effect on learning is enormous but highly variable: praise, grades, and vague exhortations ("tighten this up") show near-zero or even negative effects, while feedback that tells a learner where the gap is and what move closes it is among the strongest interventions known. A feedback template is the instrument that decides, at scale, which kind your students receive.

This matters because at scale you do not give feedback; your template does. An instructor commenting on thirty drafts will reuse the same moves whether or not they are written down. A template makes those moves explicit and editable, which means it is a design artifact you can diagnose and improve, rather than a habit you repeat unexamined. The question of this section is not "did I respond" but "does my response structurally cause a revision that changes design reasoning."

## Opening Case

An instructor sets aside a Saturday and writes warm, generous comments on every storyboard draft: "Great energy here!" "Love the visuals." "Maybe clarify a few spots." The students feel encouraged. The next round of drafts comes back with new fonts and tidier headings, and the same broken narration-to-action links the instructor was actually worried about. A full day of feedback produced cosmetic revision and nothing else.

The case exposes a hard truth: feedback that feels supportive is not the same as feedback that redirects. Praise is processed as information about the self, not about the task, so it rarely points to a move. As you read, separate two questions that templates routinely conflate: does the comment make the student feel seen, and does it tell the student where the gap is and what to do about it.

## Core Concept

A feedback template is effective when every line answers Hattie and Timperley's three feedback questions for the learner: feed up (where am I going, what is the criterion), feed back (how am I doing against it), and feed forward (what is the next move). A template that does only feed back ("this is confusing") leaves the learner to infer the criterion and invent the move, which is precisely the search cost that produces cosmetic revision.

Effective feedback also operates at the right level. The same synthesis distinguishes four levels, and they are not equally useful:

- **Task level:** correctness of the specific work ("Frame 4 has no narration"). Useful but does not generalize.
- **Process level:** the strategy or relationship behind the task ("your narration names the goal but not the learner action; the relationship to check is whether each frame states what the learner does"). This is where design reasoning improves.
- **Self-regulation level:** prompts that build the learner's own monitoring ("which frame are you least sure aligns, and how would you check?").
- **Self level:** praise or criticism of the person ("great work!"). Lowest yield; often counterproductive.

The design move for a draft-feedback template is to push comments up from task and self toward process and self-regulation, where they change how the learner reasons, not just what they fix this once.

| Template line type | Feedback level | Likely revision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Tighten this up" | Task, underspecified | Guessing; cosmetic |
| "Great start!" | Self (praise) | None to design reasoning |
| "Frame 4 names the goal but not the learner action; add one action sentence" | Task to process, with location and move | Targeted alignment fix |
| "Which frame's narration are you least confident aligns?" | Self-regulation | Learner-led monitoring |

A second principle bounds the template: actionability has a ceiling. A draft buried under twelve comments cannot be revised; the learner faces the same overload the work was meant to reduce. Prioritizing one process-level revision per draft is itself a load decision, not a generosity decision.

## Worked Example

Walk through one concrete revision of a feedback template an instructor uses to comment on student design drafts. The artifact is the comment template itself, not a single student's draft.

**Before (the praise-and-vague template).** The original three-line template read: (1) "Great start!" (2) "This is a bit confusing, can you clarify?" (3) "Tighten this up before final." When this template was used on 12 drafts, students revised cosmetics (fonts, headings) but left the design reasoning untouched, because no line pointed to a decision, an evidence source, or a next action.

1. **Diagnose the template against criteria, not tone.** A useful feedback template should name the criterion at stake, locate it in the draft, and request an observable next move. The old line "tighten this up" fails all three. By **cognitive load theory**, vague feedback adds search load: the student must guess what "tighten" means before they can act.
2. **Rewrite each line to criterion -> location -> action.** Line 2 becomes: "Frame 4's narration states the goal but not the learner action; add one sentence naming what the learner does on this screen." Now the student knows the criterion (alignment of narration to learner action), the location (Frame 4), and the move (add one action sentence).
3. **Add a single actionable next step, not three.** The revised template caps instructor comments at one priority revision per draft so the feedback does not itself overload the learner.

**After (the template as criterion-anchored feedback).** Each line follows criterion -> location -> action, with exactly one prioritized revision. Re-used on the next 12 drafts, 10 students changed design reasoning (narration-to-action alignment) rather than only formatting. The change is justified by **cognitive load theory** and is auditable: a reviewer can compare the old line "tighten this up" to the rewritten criterion-located-action line and the shift in what students actually revised.

## Decision Rule

Write each comment to the criterion-location-action structure, and reserve praise for when it is true and specific, never as the carrier of the actual feedback. A line that does not name a criterion, point to a place, and request a move is decoration.

Test every template line against three questions before it ships:

1. **Criterion check:** Does the line name the standard at stake, or does it assume the learner already knows what "good" means here? "Confusing" names a feeling; "the narration does not state the learner action" names a criterion.
2. **Location check:** Can the learner find the exact frame, line, or screen the comment refers to without rereading the whole draft? Unlocated feedback adds a search tax before any revision can begin.
3. **Move check:** Does the line request one observable next action, and is it the single highest-leverage revision rather than one of twelve? If the template emits more than one priority per draft, it is overloading the very learner it means to help.

A template whose lines cannot pass these three is producing feelings, not revisions.

## Common Misreadings

The most seductive misread is that warmth is the active ingredient. A kind tone helps a learner accept feedback, but it carries no information about the task; a template built on encouragement produces students who feel good and revise nothing of substance. Warmth is a delivery condition, not the payload.

A second misread is that more comments mean more help. The opposite is usually true. Past a small number, each additional comment competes for the learner's limited working memory and dilutes the one revision that mattered. A template that caps itself at one prioritized process-level move is not being stingy; it is respecting the actionability ceiling.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Diagnose and rebuild a real template. A program's peer-review form has three prompts: "What did you like? What was confusing? What would you change?" Run it through the levels. "What did you like" sits at the self level and harvests praise. "What was confusing" sits at the task level but is unlocated and gives the author no criterion. "What would you change" invites a list, blowing past the actionability ceiling.

Rebuild prompt by prompt. Replace "what did you like" with a feed-up anchor: "Name the one design criterion this draft handles best, and point to the frame that shows it." Replace "what was confusing" with a located process prompt: "Find one frame where the narration states a goal but not a learner action, and quote it." Replace "what would you change" with a single feed-forward move: "Request exactly one revision that would most improve narration-to-action alignment." The rebuilt form now produces criterion-anchored, located, single-move feedback, the profile the synthesis predicts will change reasoning rather than fonts.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

A strong reader of this section can take any feedback comment and immediately classify its level (self, task, process, self-regulation) and predict what kind of revision it will provoke. That classification is the diagnostic skill; once you can see a comment's level, you can see why it did or did not work.

Read every template and rubric in your own teaching against these four questions:

1. For each line, which of the three feedback questions does it answer: feed up, feed back, or feed forward? Lines that answer none are decoration.
2. At what level does the comment operate, and is that the level most likely to change design reasoning?
3. How many priority moves does this draft's feedback actually emit, and is that under the actionability ceiling?
4. Does any line build the learner's own monitoring, or does every line require the instructor to keep diagnosing forever?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A strong feedback-template artifact shows the before line, the level it operated at, and the rewritten line with its new level labeled, plus the change in what students actually revised. A reviewer should be able to see that "tighten this up" (task, underspecified) became "Frame 4 names the goal but not the learner action; add one action sentence" (task to process, located), and that the revision shifted from formatting to alignment.

A strong rationale sounds like this: "I rewrote each line to criterion-location-action and capped the form at one prioritized process-level move, because the old praise-and-vague form produced cosmetic edits. I rejected the suggestion to add a fourth encouraging line, because adding self-level praise would not raise the feedback's level and the extra line eats into the actionability budget."

## Transfer Case

Move the template from instructor-to-student feedback into automated, AI-generated draft feedback delivered inside the LMS. The criterion-location-action structure transfers, but a new constraint appears: an AI commenter will happily emit twelve located comments, blowing the actionability ceiling that a tired human naturally respected. The faithful transfer is to encode the ceiling explicitly (instruct the model to return exactly one prioritized process-level move and one self-regulation prompt) and to forbid self-level praise, which models generate by default. Copying the human template's structure without porting its limits would produce technically correct, located feedback that still overwhelms the learner.
