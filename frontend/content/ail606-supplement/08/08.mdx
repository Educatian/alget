## Core Concept

The core idea in this section is that **Course Readiness Checklist** should be treated as a relationship among purpose, constraint, evidence, and revision. Purpose names what the learner or audience needs to accomplish. Constraint names what limits the decision. Evidence names the source that makes the decision defensible. Revision shows what changed after the evidence was considered.

Three course anchors shape the reasoning here:

- **Mayer multimedia learning** helps you decide what counts as a meaningful learning or performance demand.
- **cognitive load theory** helps you notice when a design or technology choice creates risk, burden, or unsupported assumptions.
- **UDL learner variability** helps you check whether the artifact works for varied learners, audiences, or use contexts.

The anchors should do work in your artifact. If Mayer multimedia learning or cognitive load theory does not change a decision, it is only a citation rather than evidence of judgment.

## Worked Example

Walk through one concrete readiness review of a course before it opens, using a checklist tied to the capstone defense evidence board students will build.

**Before (the it-looks-done checklist).** The original checklist had four boxes: "Syllabus posted, Modules visible, Gradebook set up, Links work." All four were checked, so the course looked ready. A deeper readiness pass against actual student tasks found three gaps the surface checklist could not catch. (a) The capstone evidence board was due in Module 8 but no earlier activity ever asked students to state a limitation, so the "limitations" panel had no scaffold. (b) Two module links opened but pointed to last term's dropbox. (c) The AI-use policy was in the syllabus but not referenced anywhere in the assignment that required disclosure, so students would not connect them.

1. **Check readiness against the student's path, not the instructor's setup.** "Links work" is a setup fact; "a student can complete the capstone with the scaffolds they were given" is a readiness fact. The surface checklist verified the former and missed the latter.
2. **Trace each capstone panel back to a building activity.** The evidence board has three panels (evidence, limitations, transfer). Each panel needs a prior activity that practiced it. The limitations panel had none, so a Module 6 reflection prompt is added that asks for one named limitation. By **cognitive load theory**, distributing the sub-skills across the term keeps the capstone from front-loading three new demands at once.
3. **Close the link and policy gaps.** The two stale links are repointed to the current term, and the assignment text now links directly to the AI-use clause it depends on.

**After (the checklist as a readiness audit).** The checklist now verifies path-level readiness: 3 of 3 capstone panels traced to a building activity (was 2 of 3), 0 stale links (was 2), and the AI policy referenced at point of use. The change is auditable: a reviewer can compare the four-box surface list to the panel-to-activity trace and see which readiness gap each addition closed.

## Decision Rule

Use the learning goal first, the media choice second, and the software feature last. A feature is justified only when it reduces unnecessary processing, strengthens useful processing, or makes learner action easier to observe.

Use the following check when the artifact looks finished but the reasoning may still be hidden:

1. **Purpose check:** What should the audience be able to do after reading or using the artifact?
2. **Evidence check:** Which exact source, observation, annotation, rubric line, data pattern, or policy boundary justifies the revision?
3. **Revision check:** What changed in the artifact, and what suggestion did you reject or modify?

If you cannot answer all three questions, the artifact may be complete, but it is not yet ready as evidence of learning.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Read this section as a small professional decision cycle, not as a list of terms. First, locate the work product: **capstone defense evidence board with limitations and transfer claims**. Then ask what problem the artifact is supposed to solve inside course readiness and facilitation. A strong reader can explain the artifact in plain language before naming any theory: who will use it, what decision it supports, and what would count as evidence that it improved.

Next, connect the artifact to the course anchors: **Mayer multimedia learning**, **cognitive load theory**, **UDL learner variability**. The point is not to cite every anchor. The point is to let one anchor change the artifact. If the anchor does not change wording, layout, evidence, timing, or feedback, it has not yet become design reasoning.

Finally, inspect the revision trail. A weak submission says the artifact was improved. A strong submission shows the earlier version, the issue noticed, the support requested, the decision accepted or rejected, and the reason the final version is better for the intended learner or audience.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: **claim** on the left, **evidence** on the right. The claim is the decision you think the section is teaching. The evidence is the exact phrase, example, annotation, support move, or artifact revision that makes the claim inspectable. By the end of AIL 606 08.08, your notes should make it possible to reconstruct your reasoning without asking you to explain it again.

A strong reader of Course Readiness Checklist can answer four questions:

1. What is the real decision hidden inside this topic?
2. Which constraint makes the decision difficult?
3. What evidence would persuade a skeptical reviewer?
4. What would you revise if the audience, learner, classroom, or tool changed?
