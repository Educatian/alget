# Prototype Showcase Logistics

![Prototype Showcase Logistics textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/ail606-supplement/08-06-prototype-showcase-logistics.png)

## Why This Section Matters

A prototype showcase looks like an event-planning problem (tables, time, traffic) but it is really an assessment-design problem. The logistics you choose decide whether visitors leave having understood why each prototype made the design choices it did, or having only registered that the screens looked nice. The artifact at the center, a README traceability matrix linking each theoretical decision to the screen change it produced, only does its job if the format of the showcase prompts anyone to look at it.

This matters because the default format, an open house where visitors wander, optimizes for foot traffic and conversation volume, not for evidence exchange. Presenters repeat surface overviews, popular stations mob while others sit empty, and the reasoning that the whole capstone was meant to demonstrate never surfaces. Designing showcase logistics is the work of structuring attention so the deep evidence, the theory-to-screen rationale, is what visitors actually encounter.

## Opening Case

A program runs its end-of-term showcase as an open house: eight prototypes on tables, visitors free to roam, presenters answering whatever comes up. Within ten minutes three stations have crowds of six and two have nobody. Presenters, facing a churn of new arrivals, repeat the same thirty-second overview every couple of minutes and never reach the design reasoning. Visitors drift out having seen pretty screens and asked "so what is it?" The traceability matrices, painstakingly built, sit untouched on every table.

The case shows that logistics is not neutral plumbing; the format silently selected for milling over understanding. As you read, treat the showcase as an information environment whose structure determines what gets attended to, and ask what arrangement would make the theory-to-screen reasoning, not the visuals, the thing visitors take away.

## Core Concept

Designing showcase logistics means deciding the format, timing, and prompts of a public demonstration so that the intended evidence is what audiences exchange. The central artifact is a traceability matrix: a table whose rows pair a design decision with the theory that justified it and the specific screen change that resulted, the same logic used in requirements engineering to prove that nothing important was dropped between intent and implementation. In a showcase the matrix is the deep content; logistics decide whether it gets read.

Two design principles govern the format:

- **Define the unit of success as evidence exchanged, not visitors counted.** Foot traffic is easy to maximize and tells you nothing about understanding. The right metric is whether each visitor can recount at least one prototype's theory-to-screen link and whether presenters field substantive questions. The format should optimize for that, which usually means trading open milling for structure.
- **Manage the audience's cognitive load with a focusing prompt.** A roaming visitor faces a blank-slate problem ("what do I even ask?") that defaults to the lowest-effort question, "what is it?" A single posted prompt, "Ask me: which screen changed because of which theory?", removes that load and routes attention to the matrix.

Two logistical levers turn the principles into a runnable plan: timed rotations (groups move every few minutes so every station gets visitors and presenters give their overview once per rotation rather than continuously) and staggered starts (groups begin at different stations so no prototype is mobbed while another is empty).

| Open-house default | Failure it produces | Structured alternative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Visitors roam freely | Stations mob or sit empty | Timed rotations with staggered starts |
| No conversation prompt | Default question is "what is it?" | Posted "ask me about theory-to-screen" card |
| Success = attendance | Milling, no understanding | Success = evidence each visitor can recount |

## Worked Example

Walk through one concrete logistics decision for a prototype showcase: how visitors move through eight student prototype stations in a 50-minute session and how each station presents its README traceability matrix.

**Before (the open-house plan).** The first plan was "all eight prototypes open on tables, visitors wander, presenters answer questions." In the pilot, three popular stations had crowds of six while two stations had no visitors for 20 minutes, presenters repeated the same overview every two minutes (so no one heard the design reasoning), and visitors left without seeing any README traceability matrix because nothing prompted them to ask about it.

1. **Name the real goal of the showcase.** The goal is not foot traffic; it is that each visitor can see at least a few prototypes' theory-to-screen reasoning, and each presenter gets substantive questions. The open-house format optimized for milling, not for evidence exchange.
2. **Convert to timed rotations with a focusing prompt.** Visitors are grouped and rotate every 6 minutes (eight stations, two rounds covers the room). Each station posts one card: "Ask me: which screen changed because of which theory?" so the README matrix becomes the conversation, not an afterthought. By **cognitive load theory**, a single focusing prompt removes the "what do I even ask" load that left visitors silent.
3. **Stagger start stations to balance load.** Groups start at different stations so no prototype is mobbed; presenter overview is given once per rotation, not continuously.

**After (the showcase as an evidence exchange).** Timed 6-minute rotations, staggered starts, and a per-station "ask me about theory-to-screen" prompt. In the re-run every prototype received visitors in both rounds and presenters reported questions about specific README matrix rows rather than generic "cool, what is it?" The change is auditable: a reviewer can compare the open-house plan to the rotation schedule and the shift in the kind of questions presenters logged.

## Decision Rule

Design the format around the evidence you want exchanged, then add only the logistics that protect it. A showcase feature earns its place if it raises the chance a visitor encounters the theory-to-screen reasoning; if it only adds spectacle or throughput, cut it.

Test any showcase plan against three questions:

1. **Evidence-encounter check:** What in this plan makes a visitor look at the traceability matrix rather than just the screen? If nothing prompts it, the deep content will go unseen no matter how good it is.
2. **Attention-load check:** Does the format hand visitors a focusing question, or does it leave them to invent one and default to "what is it?" An unprompted audience asks the shallowest available question.
3. **Coverage check:** Does the timing guarantee every station gets substantive visits, or will popularity concentrate attention and starve the rest? Rotations and staggered starts exist to answer this.

A plan that fails these is an event; a plan that passes them is an assessment that happens to be public.

## Common Misreadings

The most common misread is that a busy room is a successful showcase. Crowding and chatter feel like success but are exactly what an open house over-produces; high attendance with zero recountable reasoning is a failed assessment dressed as a hit event. Energy is not understanding.

A second misread is that a great traceability matrix sells itself. Even an excellent matrix is passive; without a format that points visitors at it, it competes with the visually louder prototype screen and loses. The quality of the artifact does not substitute for logistics that route attention to it.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Convert the failed open house into an evidence exchange. Begin from the metric: success is that each visitor can recount one prototype's theory-to-screen link, not that the room was full. That reframing immediately rules out "just add more tables."

Next, fix the attention default. Post one card at each station, "Ask me: which screen changed because of which theory?", so the traceability matrix becomes the conversation rather than an afterthought. This is a cognitive-load move; it removes the blank-slate problem that produced "so what is it?"

Then fix coverage with timing. Group visitors and rotate every six minutes, eight stations across two rounds, with groups starting at different stations so nothing mobs or empties. Presenters now give their overview once per rotation and spend the rest of each visit on substantive questions. The walkthrough is complete when a post-event check shows presenters logging questions about specific matrix rows rather than generic curiosity, which is the observable signal that logistics moved attention to the evidence.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

A strong reader of this section evaluates a showcase by what visitors leave understanding, not by how lively it looked. The diagnostic move is to ask, for any proposed format, what question a visitor will actually ask and whether the answer touches the design reasoning.

Read any showcase plan against these four questions:

1. What concrete prompt or arrangement points a visitor at the traceability matrix rather than the screen?
2. What is the default question an unprompted visitor will ask, and how does the format upgrade it?
3. Does the timing guarantee even coverage, or will attention concentrate on a few stations?
4. What observable signal (the kind of questions presenters log) would tell you the format worked?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A strong showcase artifact shows the before format, the failure it produced (mobbing, empty stations, surface questions), and the revised plan with its rotation schedule, focusing prompt, and success metric. A reviewer should be able to see that the change shifted presenter-logged questions from "what is it?" toward specific traceability-matrix rows.

A strong rationale sounds like this: "I replaced the open house with six-minute staggered rotations and a per-station focusing card, because the open format optimized for milling and starved half the stations. I rejected the suggestion to give prizes for the most-visited prototype, because rewarding foot traffic doubles down on the wrong metric and pulls attention further from the reasoning."

## Transfer Case

Move the showcase fully online, asynchronous, with visitors browsing recorded walkthroughs on their own time. Timed rotations and staggered starts are meaningless when there is no shared room, so those levers do not transfer. The constants are the metric (evidence recounted) and the attention principle (a focusing prompt), but the new constraint is that nothing forces a visitor past the screen recording into the matrix. The faithful transfer is to embed the focusing question into the artifact itself (open each walkthrough by answering "which screen changed because of which theory?") and to capture evidence-encounter through a short prompt visitors complete. Copying the in-person rotation schedule into an online setting would be planning traffic flow for a room that no longer exists.
