# AI Policy Language for Students

![AI Policy Language for Students textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/ail606-supplement/08-04-ai-policy-language-for-students.png)

## Why This Section Matters

An AI-use policy is not a warning label; it is an instrument that shapes behavior. The words you choose determine whether students conceal their tool use or disclose it, whether assistive-technology users feel criminalized, and whether you retain any visibility into how AI shaped a learner's design judgment. Generative AI made the old binary, allowed or forbidden, unworkable, because the same tool that drafts an essay also reads alt text aloud and critiques a learner's own draft. A policy that cannot distinguish those uses governs nothing it intends to.

This matters because the wrong policy produces the opposite of its goal. A flat prohibition does not eliminate AI use; it drives it underground, where the instructor loses the ability to assess where the student's judgment ends and the tool's begins. The design problem in this section is to write language that makes the boundary of student authorship visible rather than hidden, which is the only form of integrity an instructor can actually observe and teach.

## Learning Targets

- Contrast deterrence and transparency models of academic integrity and explain why generative AI makes deterrence unenforceable.
- Write an AI-use clause with a bounded permission, a one-line disclosure format with a worked example, and an accessibility carve-out.
- Apply the distinguishability, disclosure-cost, and accessibility checks to evaluate a draft AI-use policy.
- Predict the behavioral effect (concealment, exclusion, or transparency) a policy clause will produce.

## Opening Case

An instructor, alarmed by a viral story about AI cheating, adds one line to the syllabus: "Use of AI tools such as ChatGPT is prohibited; violations are academic dishonesty." The term unfolds in two quiet failures. A student who relies on an AI screen-reader for alt text does not know whether her assistive tool counts, and stops using it during coursework rather than risk an accusation. Three other students use AI to brainstorm, decide it is safer not to mention it, and hand in work whose origins the instructor can no longer see. The ban did not produce integrity; it produced silence and exclusion.

The case reveals the central mechanism: a prohibition cannot distinguish a learner who generated a whole storyboard from one who used AI to critique their own narration, so it treats both as the same crime and pushes both into hiding. As you read, watch how replacing prohibition with bounded permission plus disclosure changes not the students' honesty but the instructor's visibility into it.

## Core Concept

Good AI-use policy language rests on a shift from a deterrence model to a transparency model of integrity. Deterrence asks "how do I stop the prohibited act," and it works only when the act is detectable; with generative AI it is not reliably detectable, so deterrence collapses into either false accusations or undetected use. Transparency asks "how do I keep the learner's judgment visible," and it works precisely because the learner, not a detector, supplies the disclosure.

A workable policy has three structural parts:

- **Bounded permission.** Name the supported uses (brainstorming options, critiquing one's own draft, generating alternatives to react to) and the prohibited one (submitting AI-generated design work as your own). A boundary the student can apply beats a blanket the student must guess around.
- **Disclosure with a worked example.** Require a one-line disclosure naming the tool, the prompt intent, and what was accepted or rejected, and show one filled-in example. Without a model, students invent formats under pressure, raising the very load that suppresses disclosure.
- **Alignment with the learning goal.** The policy should protect the construct being assessed (design judgment), not all AI contact. Banning an assistive reader protects nothing and excludes someone.

A subtle but decisive principle: policy language is also an accessibility decision. Assistive technologies increasingly embed AI, so any blanket ban silently penalizes disabled students. A policy that forgets this is not just harsh; it is likely noncompliant.

## Worked Example

Walk through one concrete revision of the AI-use clause an instructor places in a design course syllabus. The artifact is the policy paragraph students read before they touch any AI tool.

**Before (the prohibition clause).** The original clause read: "Use of AI tools such as ChatGPT is prohibited. Violations are academic dishonesty." When this policy ran for one term, two failures appeared: a student who used a screen-reader's AI alt-text generator did not know whether that counted, and three students used AI for brainstorming and hid it, so the instructor lost any view of how AI shaped their drafts. A flat ban produced concealment, not integrity.

1. **Decide what the policy is actually protecting.** The goal is not zero AI; it is that the learner's design judgment remains theirs and is visible. A ban cannot distinguish a learner who generated a whole storyboard from one who used AI to critique their own narration.
2. **Replace prohibition with bounded permission plus disclosure.** The clause becomes: "You may use AI for bounded support (brainstorming options, critiquing your own draft, generating alternatives to react to). You may not submit AI-generated design work as your own. For any AI-assisted decision, add a one-line disclosure naming the tool, the prompt intent, and what you accepted or rejected."
3. **Show the disclosure format so it lowers, not raises, student load.** A single worked disclosure line is included: "Used Claude to generate three signaling alternatives for Frame 2; accepted one, rejected two because they added redundant on-screen text." By **[cognitive load theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_load)**, a concrete template removes the load of inventing a disclosure format under pressure.

**After (the policy as a transparency contract).** The revised clause permits bounded use, forbids submitting generated work as one's own, and requires a one-line disclosure with an example. In the next term, disclosures appeared on 11 of 12 AI-assisted drafts and the instructor could see and assess the boundary of student judgment. The change is auditable: a reviewer can compare "prohibited / dishonesty" to the bounded-permission clause and the disclosures it produced.

## Decision Rule

Write the policy to protect the learning construct, not to ban a category of tool. Every clause should be testable against one question: does this rule keep the student's design judgment visible, or does it merely forbid something a detector cannot reliably catch?

Run any AI-use clause through three checks before you adopt it:

1. **Distinguishability check:** Can the policy tell apart a learner who generated the whole artifact from one who used AI to critique their own draft? A rule that treats both identically will either over-punish or go unenforced.
2. **Disclosure-cost check:** How hard is it for an honest student to comply? If there is no worked example and no fixed format, the cost of disclosing is high and disclosure rates fall, even among willing students.
3. **Accessibility check:** Does the clause sweep in AI-embedded assistive technology a disabled student depends on? If a screen-reader's AI alt text could violate the policy, the policy is excluding learners, not protecting integrity.

A clause that fails any of these will produce concealment, noncompliance, or exclusion, regardless of how firm it sounds.

## Common Misreadings

The most common misread is that a firmer ban produces more integrity. Firmness and enforceability are different properties; a ban on an undetectable act is loud but empty, and its main effect is to relocate the behavior to where the instructor cannot see it. Strength of wording is not the same as control over outcomes.

A second misread is that disclosure is a punishment students will resist. The opposite holds when the cost of disclosing is low: given a one-line format and a worked example, most students disclose, because the policy reframes AI use as a normal, bounded part of design work rather than a confession. Resistance comes from ambiguity and high disclosure cost, not from disclosure itself.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Rewrite a real clause end to end. A department's policy reads: "AI-generated content is not permitted in any assignment." Diagnose it first. It fails distinguishability (alt text and full ghostwriting are both "AI-generated content"), it offers no disclosure path, and it has no accessibility carve-out.

Now rebuild around the construct. State the bounded permission: "You may use AI to brainstorm options, critique your own draft, and generate alternatives to react to. You may not submit AI-generated design work as your own." Then lower the disclosure cost with a fixed line and an example: "For any AI-assisted decision, add one line naming the tool, your prompt intent, and what you accepted or rejected. Example: Used Claude to generate three signaling alternatives for Frame 2; accepted one, rejected two for adding redundant on-screen text." Finally, add the accessibility sentence: "Assistive technologies you rely on for access are always permitted and need no disclosure."

The rebuilt clause is enforceable where the old one was not, because it asks students to make a boundary visible rather than to avoid an undetectable act. The walkthrough is complete when the policy could survive both a disability-services review and a real student trying to comply in good faith.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

A strong reader of this section evaluates a policy by its predicted behavioral effect, not its tone. Read a clause and ask what an honest, anxious, or assistive-technology-dependent student would actually do upon reading it. The policy's real meaning is the behavior it produces, not the firmness it projects.

Read any AI-use policy against these four questions:

1. Does it operate on a deterrence model (stop an undetectable act) or a transparency model (keep judgment visible)?
2. Can it distinguish bounded support from wholesale generation, or does it collapse them into one prohibition?
3. What is the cost to an honest student of complying, and does a worked example lower it?
4. Does it protect or penalize a student whose assistive technology embeds AI?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A strong policy artifact shows the before clause, the specific failure it produced (concealment, exclusion, or unenforceability), and the rewritten clause with its bounded permission, disclosure example, and accessibility carve-out. A reviewer should be able to see that the change converts an undetectable prohibition into a visible boundary, and should be able to predict higher disclosure rates as a result.

A strong rationale sounds like this: "I replaced 'AI is prohibited' with bounded permission plus a one-line disclosure and example, because the ban produced concealment and excluded an assistive-technology user. I rejected the suggestion to require a paragraph-long reflection for each AI use, because that raises disclosure cost enough to suppress the very transparency the policy depends on."

## Transfer Case

Move the policy from a graduate design seminar to a first-year required course with a strict program-wide privacy rule that forbids students from pasting their own data into third-party tools. The transparency goal is unchanged, but a new constraint dominates: even bounded, disclosed use can violate data privacy if the prompt contains protected information. The faithful transfer is to keep bounded permission and disclosure but add a data-handling boundary ("disclose the tool, but never paste classmate data, identifiable records, or unpublished institutional content"), and to provide a sanctioned, privacy-reviewed tool option. Copying the seminar's permissive clause without the data rule would satisfy integrity while breaching privacy, trading one compliance failure for another.
