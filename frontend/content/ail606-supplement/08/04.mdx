## Core Concept

The core idea in this section is that **AI Policy Language for Students** should be treated as a relationship among purpose, constraint, evidence, and revision. Purpose names what the learner or audience needs to accomplish. Constraint names what limits the decision. Evidence names the source that makes the decision defensible. Revision shows what changed after the evidence was considered.

Three course anchors shape the reasoning here:

- **Mayer multimedia learning** helps you decide what counts as a meaningful learning or performance demand.
- **cognitive load theory** helps you notice when a design or technology choice creates risk, burden, or unsupported assumptions.
- **UDL learner variability** helps you check whether the artifact works for varied learners, audiences, or use contexts.

Use these anchors as lenses, not as names to drop into a reflection. A useful lens changes what you notice, what you question, and what you revise.

## Worked Example

Walk through one concrete revision of the AI-use clause an instructor places in a design course syllabus. The artifact is the policy paragraph students read before they touch any AI tool.

**Before (the prohibition clause).** The original clause read: "Use of AI tools such as ChatGPT is prohibited. Violations are academic dishonesty." When this policy ran for one term, two failures appeared: a student who used a screen-reader's AI alt-text generator did not know whether that counted, and three students used AI for brainstorming and hid it, so the instructor lost any view of how AI shaped their drafts. A flat ban produced concealment, not integrity.

1. **Decide what the policy is actually protecting.** The goal is not zero AI; it is that the learner's design judgment remains theirs and is visible. A ban cannot distinguish a learner who generated a whole storyboard from one who used AI to critique their own narration.
2. **Replace prohibition with bounded permission plus disclosure.** The clause becomes: "You may use AI for bounded support (brainstorming options, critiquing your own draft, generating alternatives to react to). You may not submit AI-generated design work as your own. For any AI-assisted decision, add a one-line disclosure naming the tool, the prompt intent, and what you accepted or rejected."
3. **Show the disclosure format so it lowers, not raises, student load.** A single worked disclosure line is included: "Used Claude to generate three signaling alternatives for Frame 2; accepted one, rejected two because they added redundant on-screen text." By **cognitive load theory**, a concrete template removes the load of inventing a disclosure format under pressure.

**After (the policy as a transparency contract).** The revised clause permits bounded use, forbids submitting generated work as one's own, and requires a one-line disclosure with an example. In the next term, disclosures appeared on 11 of 12 AI-assisted drafts and the instructor could see and assess the boundary of student judgment. The change is auditable: a reviewer can compare "prohibited / dishonesty" to the bounded-permission clause and the disclosures it produced.

## Decision Rule

Use the learning goal first, the media choice second, and the software feature last. A feature is justified only when it reduces unnecessary processing, strengthens useful processing, or makes learner action easier to observe.

Before submitting the AI-USE disclosure excerpt tied to one bounded design-support decision, slow down and check whether the trace can answer three questions:

1. **Purpose check:** What should the audience be able to do after reading or using the artifact?
2. **Evidence check:** Which exact source, observation, annotation, rubric line, data pattern, or policy boundary justifies the revision?
3. **Revision check:** What changed in the artifact, and what suggestion did you reject or modify?

If you cannot answer all three questions, the artifact may be complete, but it is not yet ready as evidence of learning.

## Common Misreadings

A common mistake is to treat multimedia quality as visual polish. A cleaner screen can still increase extraneous load if the learner action is unclear.

The trace should show what cognitive demand was noticed, what support was requested, and which change improved the learner's path.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Read this section as a small professional decision cycle, not as a list of terms. First, locate the work product: **AI-USE disclosure excerpt tied to one bounded design-support decision**. Then ask what problem the artifact is supposed to solve inside course readiness and facilitation. A strong reader can explain the artifact in plain language before naming any theory: who will use it, what decision it supports, and what would count as evidence that it improved.

Next, connect the artifact to the course anchors: **Mayer multimedia learning**, **cognitive load theory**, **UDL learner variability**. The point is not to cite every anchor. The point is to let one anchor change the artifact. If the anchor does not change wording, layout, evidence, timing, or feedback, it has not yet become design reasoning.

Finally, inspect the revision trail. A weak submission says the artifact was improved. A strong submission shows the earlier version, the issue noticed, the support requested, the decision accepted or rejected, and the reason the final version is better for the intended learner or audience.
