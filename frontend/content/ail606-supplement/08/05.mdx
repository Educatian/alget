# Accessibility Review Workflow

![Accessibility Review Workflow textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/ail606-supplement/08-05-accessibility-review-workflow.png)

## Why This Section Matters

Accessibility is not a finishing pass you run on a polished screen; it is a property of whether a learner can perceive, operate, and understand the material at all. A storyboard frame that looks immaculate can be entirely closed to a screen-reader user, unreadable to someone with low vision, or unusable by a learner who cannot distinguish red from green. An accessibility review workflow is the disciplined procedure that replaces "looks clean" with a checkable list of who is currently excluded and why.

This matters because accessibility failures are invisible to the person who built the screen. The designer who can see the diagram, read the gray text, and notice the red highlight has no native signal that any of those choices excludes someone. A workflow anchored to explicit criteria is what makes the invisible exclusion visible, and turns a vague sense that "we should be accessible" into specific, fixable defects tied to specific learners.

## Opening Case

A designer presents a finished prototype screen at a critique: clean typography, a labeled diagram, instructions in elegant light-gray text, and a red frame marking the next step. The room nods; it looks done. Then someone runs it through a screen reader, which announces only "image" where the diagram should be, and checks the gray text, which measures 2.9 to 1 against the background, well below the 4.5 to 1 floor. The "next" cue is color alone, invisible to a color-blind learner. Three learners were excluded by a screen everyone in the room called finished.

The case makes the workflow's whole point: "looks clean" is a statement about the reviewer's own perception, not about access. As you read, notice how every finding in a real review is tied to a named criterion and a named excluded learner, which is what separates an accessibility review from an aesthetic reaction.

## Core Concept

An accessibility review workflow is a repeatable procedure that maps each element of a screen to the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) and produces a prioritized, checkable list of defects. WCAG organizes everything under four principles, abbreviated POUR: content must be Perceivable, Operable, Understandable, and Robust. Each principle decomposes into testable success criteria with identifiers, and those identifiers are what make a review auditable rather than impressionistic.

Three of those criteria recur in instructional media often enough to memorize:

- **1.1.1 Non-text content.** Every meaningful image needs a text alternative. A diagram without alt text is silence to a screen-reader user. The alt text should convey what the image means in context, not merely that an image exists.
- **1.4.3 Contrast (minimum).** Normal text needs a contrast ratio of at least 4.5 to 1 against its background (3 to 1 for large text). Light-gray-on-white routinely fails this and excludes low-vision learners.
- **1.4.1 Use of color.** Information conveyed by color must also be available another way. "The red frame is next" fails; "the frame labeled Next, with an arrow" passes.

Two principles govern how you act on findings. First, prioritize by depth of exclusion: a missing alt text fully locks out a screen-reader user, so it outranks a marginal contrast miss that merely strains a sighted reader. Second, born-accessible beats retrofit. Universal Design for Learning treats variability as the norm, so designing redundant cues from the start (color plus label plus icon) is cheaper and cleaner than bolting accommodations onto a finished screen.

## Worked Example

Walk through one concrete accessibility review of a single prototype screen, a narrated storyboard frame with an embedded diagram and a "Next" button.

**Before (the polished-but-inaccessible screen).** The screen looked finished: clean typography, a labeled diagram, instructions in light gray on white. A structured WCAG-anchored pass found three concrete blockers. (a) The diagram had no alt text, so a screen-reader user reached "image" with no content. (b) The instruction text measured a 2.9:1 contrast ratio against the background, below the 4.5:1 minimum for normal text. (c) The only cue for the required action was color ("the red frame is next"), unusable for a color-blind learner.

1. **Review against criteria, not impression.** "Looks clean" is not an accessibility finding. Each blocker is tied to a specific WCAG success criterion (1.1.1 non-text content, 1.4.3 contrast, 1.4.1 use of color), so the review produces a checkable list rather than a vibe.
2. **Fix in priority order by who is excluded.** The missing alt text fully excludes a screen-reader user, so it is fixed first: a one-sentence alt description naming what the diagram shows and why it matters. Contrast is raised to 4.6:1. The color-only cue gains a text label plus an arrow icon so the action is conveyed three ways.
3. **Confirm the fix does not add load for everyone else.** By **UDL learner variability**, the redundant cue (color plus label plus icon) supports varied learners without crowding the screen, because the existing layout had room.

**After (the screen as an accessible artifact).** Three named WCAG blockers closed: alt text added (1.1.1), contrast 2.9:1 raised to 4.6:1 (1.4.3), action cue no longer color-only (1.4.1). The change is auditable: a reviewer can map each fix to its success criterion and to the specific learner who was previously excluded, justified by **UDL learner variability**.

## Decision Rule

Review against named criteria, not against your own impression, and fix in the order of who is most fully excluded. An accessibility finding that cannot be tied to a WCAG criterion is an opinion; a fix that does not say which learner it unblocks has no priority.

Run every screen through three checks:

1. **Criterion check:** For each potential issue, which specific success criterion does it violate (1.1.1, 1.4.3, 1.4.1, or another)? If you cannot name one, you have a preference, not a defect.
2. **Exclusion-depth check:** Does this issue fully lock a learner out (no alt text, so the diagram simply does not exist for them) or merely strain them (slightly low contrast)? Full exclusions are fixed first.
3. **Redundancy check:** After the fix, is the same information available through at least two channels (color and label and icon), and does the added cue fit without crowding the screen?

A screen that passes all three is accessible by evidence; one that only "looks fine" has not been reviewed, only admired.

## Common Misreadings

The most common misread is that a clean, professional look implies accessibility. Visual polish and access are independent properties; a beautifully typeset screen can fail every relevant criterion, and a plain one can pass them all. Treating aesthetics as evidence of access is how excluded learners stay invisible.

A second misread is that an automated checker certifies accessibility. Automated tools catch contrast ratios and missing alt attributes, but they cannot judge whether alt text is meaningful, whether reading order makes sense, or whether a color cue has a real alternative. The workflow needs human judgment against the criteria, with the checker as one input, not the verdict.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Run the workflow on the critique screen from the opening. Start with Perceivable, because perception failures are total exclusions. The diagram triggers 1.1.1: add a one-sentence alt that says what the diagram shows and why it matters here, not "diagram of process." The gray instructions measure 2.9 to 1, failing 1.4.3; raise the text to 4.6 to 1.

Move to Operable and Understandable. The "next" action is signaled by the red frame alone, failing 1.4.1; add a text label and an arrow so the cue lives in three channels. Now check the fix against UDL: redundant cues must not crowd everyone else. Because the layout had whitespace, color plus label plus icon fits without raising load for sighted learners.

Close the loop with priority and audit. The alt text was fixed first because it fully excluded a screen-reader user, ahead of the contrast strain. Each fix is logged against its criterion (1.1.1, 1.4.3, 1.4.1) and the learner it unblocks. The walkthrough is complete when a reviewer can map every change to a criterion and a person, which is the difference between an accessible screen and one that merely got prettier.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

A strong reader of this section looks at any screen and mentally walks POUR, asking at each principle "who cannot perceive, operate, or understand this, and which criterion names that gap?" That habit converts a vague worry about inclusion into specific, addressable defects.

Read any prototype against these four questions:

1. For each non-text element, is there a meaningful text alternative, or only a placeholder (1.1.1)?
2. Does any text fall below the contrast floor for its size (1.4.3)?
3. Is any required information carried by color alone, with no second channel (1.4.1)?
4. When you fix the most-excluding issue first, does the redundant cue you add fit without raising load for other learners?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A strong accessibility artifact reports each defect by its WCAG criterion, the learner it excluded, the fix, and the post-fix measurement (for example, contrast raised from 2.9 to 1 to 4.6 to 1). A reviewer should be able to map every change to a criterion and a person, and to see that the fixes were ordered by depth of exclusion.

A strong rationale sounds like this: "I added alt text first (1.1.1) because the missing alternative fully excluded a screen-reader user, ahead of the contrast strain. I rejected the suggestion to convey the next step with a brighter red instead of adding a label, because brightening color does not satisfy 1.4.1; the information must exist in a non-color channel."

## Transfer Case

Move the workflow from a single static screen to an interactive simulation with drag-and-drop and timed feedback. The POUR criteria still hold, but Operable now dominates: drag-and-drop must have a keyboard-only alternative (criterion 2.1.1), and timed interactions must be adjustable for learners who need more time (criterion 2.2.1), constraints that a static screen never raised. The faithful transfer is to extend the review to the criteria the new interaction introduces rather than reusing the static checklist. Porting only the alt-text-and-contrast pass would certify a simulation that is unusable to anyone who cannot operate a mouse under time pressure.
