# Worked Examples and Fading

![Worked Examples and Fading textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/ail606-supplement/01-04-worked-examples-and-fading.png)

## Why This Section Matters

There is a counterintuitive result at the center of this section: for novices, studying a fully worked solution produces more learning than solving an equivalent problem, even though solving feels like more learning is happening. This is the worked-example effect, and it follows directly from cognitive load theory. A novice solving an unguided problem spends working memory on search and trial, which is extraneous to building a schema; a novice studying a worked example spends that capacity on the solution structure itself. The design move this section teaches, fading, is how you move a learner from studied examples to independent performance without ever dropping them into the unguided-search trap. Your storyboard sequence is where that fade is engineered, frame by frame.

## Learning Targets

- Explain the worked-example effect in terms of search load versus schema-building load.
- Engineer a fading sequence (worked, completion, open) matched to the learner's growing schema.
- Document a bounded AI/software support move with accepted and rejected suggestions.
- Use annotations, support requests, and revision notes to decide what help or design change should come next.

## Opening Case

A module asks novices to "write a measurable learning objective" across four open prompts. Learners stare, guess, and produce flailing attempts, because they have no schema for the parts of an objective. The instinct is to add more practice problems. But practice volume is not the constraint; the learners are searching a space they cannot yet navigate. The real decision is where to place a model, and how to withdraw it, so that effort lands on building the schema rather than on blind search.

## Core Concept

The worked-example effect rests on the distinction between two ways a novice can spend working memory. **Problem-solving search** (means-ends analysis: where am I, where is the goal, what reduces the gap?) consumes capacity without leaving a schema behind. **Studying a worked solution** lets the learner attend to the solution's structure and build the schema directly. For novices, search is largely extraneous load, so worked examples win.

But the advantage is conditional. The **expertise-reversal effect** says that once a learner has a schema, studying a worked example becomes redundant with what they already know, and now problem-solving is the better use of capacity. So the right design is not "always show examples" or "always make them practice." It is a **fading sequence** that tracks the learner's growth:

| Stage | Format | What the learner does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Worked example | Studies and self-explains a complete solution |
| 2 | Completion problem | Finishes a partly worked solution (some steps faded) |
| 3 | Completion problem | Finishes more (more steps faded) |
| 4 | Open problem | Solves unaided |

Two design refinements matter. **Backward fading** removes the last step first (the step closest to the goal, often the easiest to attempt), so the learner always works from a supported position. And a **self-explanation prompt** on the worked stages converts passive viewing into the germane processing that actually builds the schema.

## Worked Example

A thin claim hides the instructional move:

> I made the objective-writing sequence clearer.

A fading-grounded claim names the stages and the schema target:

> I replaced four open prompts with a four-stage fade: a fully worked objective with its four parts labeled, then two completion problems that withdraw parts one at a time, then an open problem. Support is removed as the schema forms, so effort lands on the parts of an objective rather than on guessing.

The improvement is that support is transferred one part at a time, so the learner moves from studying a model to independent performance rather than being dropped into open problems with no schema.

### Annotated Before/After: A Faded Sequence

Here is a storyboard sequence for teaching novices to write a measurable learning objective, revised to apply the worked-example effect with fading.

**Before.** Frames 1 through 4 each pose an open task: "Write a measurable objective for this scenario." No model is shown. Reader-action note: "submit your objective."

The diagnosis: novices have no schema for the parts of an objective (audience, behavior, condition, degree), so four unguided attempts mostly produce flailing and high extraneous effort.

**After (four faded frames).**

1. Frame 1, fully worked example: a complete objective is shown with each of its four parts labeled and a one-line rationale for each. Reader-action: study and self-explain.
2. Frame 2, completion problem: the audience and behavior are given; the learner supplies the condition and degree. (Two of four parts faded in.)
3. Frame 3, completion problem: only the behavior verb is given; the learner supplies the other three parts. (Three of four faded in.)
4. Frame 4, open problem: the learner writes a full objective from the scenario unaided.

**Rejected suggestion.** An AI assistant proposed making all four frames open problems "so learners get more practice." Rejected: for these novices it removes the modeled schema and recreates the original failure. Practice volume is not the constraint; guided schema-building is.

The improvement is that support is transferred one part at a time, so the learner moves from studying a model to independent performance rather than being dropped into open problems with no schema.

Match the support level to the schema, and fade only as the schema grows:

1. **Start worked for true novices.** If the learner has no schema, the first frame is a complete, self-explained model, not a problem.
2. **Fade one element at a time.** Each completion stage withdraws a single part so the learner is never in unsupported search.
3. **Reach open only when the schema can carry it.** The final unaided frame is the assessment of the fade, not its starting point.

If you cannot say which element each stage withdraws and why now, the sequence is a list of tasks, not a fade.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is "more practice is more learning," which for novices reintroduces the search load the worked example removed. The second is treating a worked example as a video to watch passively; without a self-explanation prompt, the germane processing never happens and the example teaches little.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Trace why the four-open-prompt version failed and why the fade fixes it. A novice at prompt 1 must invent the very category "parts of an objective" while also producing one, that is search load piled on intrinsic load, and capacity saturates. The fade reorders the spend: frame 1 hands the learner the four-part schema as a studied model, so capacity goes to understanding structure, not searching. By frame 2 the learner has the schema and can supply two parts; by frame 3, three; by frame 4 the schema carries an unaided attempt. The withdrawal is gradual and element-wise, so the learner is always working from support rather than from scratch. The rejected "all open" suggestion is exactly the original failure, which is why it is rejected on principle, not preference.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

Read every practice sequence and ask whether the learner has the schema the first task assumes. A strong reader can name, for each frame, the support level, the element being withdrawn, and the schema state that justifies the withdrawal. Keep a two-column note: frame on the left, support level and faded element on the right.

A strong reader of worked examples and fading can answer four questions:

1. Does the learner have a schema, or is the first frame a search trap?
2. Which element does each stage withdraw, and why now?
3. Where is the self-explanation prompt that makes a worked example generative?
4. At what expertise level should the worked stages be dropped entirely?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The sequence is strong when each frame states its support level (worked, completion, open), the element it withdraws, and the schema condition that licenses the withdrawal, with a self-explanation prompt on the worked stages. "Four practice frames" is weak; "worked then two completion frames withdrawing one part each then open, with self-explanation on frame 1" is strong. If a frame's support level is unstated, mark the gap and ask BigAL to compare a novice fade with an expert fade for the same skill rather than to define worked examples.

A useful final entry would read: "I built a four-stage fade that withdraws one objective part per frame and added self-explanation to frame 1. I rejected the all-open version because it recreates the search trap for novices."

## Transfer Case

Move the sequence to a graduate cohort who already writes objectives competently but inconsistently applies the degree criterion. A full four-stage fade would bore them and, by reversal, teach less than targeted problems. The right transfer is to drop frames 1 and 2 entirely and give two open problems that stress the weak element (degree), with feedback. The fading principle is intact; the starting support level moves up because the schema is already partly built. A designer who engineered the fade by schema state can re-anchor it; one who just sequenced four tasks cannot.
