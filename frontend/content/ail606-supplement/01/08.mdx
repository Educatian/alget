# Module 1 Synthesis Studio

![Module 1 Synthesis Studio textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/ail606-supplement/01-08-module-1-synthesis-studio.png)

## Why This Section Matters

The first seven sections handed you separate instruments: the cognitive architecture, Mayer's principles, load diagnosis, fading, UDL, tool selection, and rationale. On any real screen those instruments do not line up neatly; they collide. The redundancy principle wants you to delete text while the worked-example principle wants you to show more support. UDL wants flexible expression while cognitive load wants a constrained path. Synthesis is not summarizing the seven ideas in seven paragraphs. It is making them act on a *single* decision, naming where they conflict, and resolving the conflict for a stated learner. This studio is where you prove you can design under tension rather than recite frameworks side by side.

## Learning Targets

- Integrate multiple Module 1 principles into one design decision rather than listing them separately.
- Name a conflict between two principles and resolve it explicitly for a stated learner.
- Document a bounded AI/software support move with accepted and rejected suggestions.
- Use annotations, support requests, and revision notes to decide what help or design change should come next.

## Opening Case

A synthesis brief devotes one paragraph each to architecture, Mayer, load, and worked examples, then presents a screen at the end. Every paragraph is correct, and none of them touches the screen. Worse, two of the cited ideas, the worked-example pull toward more support and the redundancy pull toward less text, actually conflict on that very screen, and the brief never notices. This is a summary wearing the label "synthesis." The decision is not "did you cover the topics?" It is "did the topics collide on one decision, and did you resolve the collision?"

## Core Concept

Synthesis differs from summary along three axes that the studio scores.

| Axis | Summary | Synthesis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Structure | Principles listed in parallel | Principles converge on one decision |
| Conflict | Ignored or unnoticed | Named and resolved |
| Learner | Generic | A specific learner governs the resolution |

The hard part is **resolving principle conflict**. Two principles can both be valid and still point opposite ways on a frame; you cannot honor both by default, so you must **rank** them for the stated learner and state the warrant for the ranking. On a multi-step procedure for novices, the worked-example need (show a model) governs the *amount* of support, while the redundancy principle still governs its *form* (narration plus diagram, not duplicated text). That is a resolution, not a compromise: each principle controls the variable it legitimately owns. A synthesis that never names a conflict has almost certainly left principles sitting inertly side by side rather than designing with them.

Resolve the worked-example versus redundancy conflict on the shutdown screen by ownership, not by compromise.

## Worked Example

A thin synthesis lists ideas:

> The screen uses dual channels, Mayer's principles, cognitive load management, and worked examples.

A real synthesis converges them on one decision and resolves the tension:

> For novices learning a 7-step shutdown, I split the explanation across narration and diagram (architecture and modality), removed the duplicated on-screen text (redundancy), segmented the 7 steps into three learner-paced chunks (intrinsic load), and made chunk 1 worked, chunk 2 a completion problem, chunk 3 unaided (fading). The worked-example pull and the redundancy pull conflicted; I let worked-example govern the support amount and redundancy govern its form, so both are honored.

The improvement is that four principles converge on one screen, their conflict is named and resolved for a stated learner, and the rationale is auditable.

### Annotated Before/After: One Screen, Four Principles in Tension

Here is a synthesis brief for a single instructional screen teaching a multi-step lab safety procedure, revised so the whole module acts on one decision.

**Before.** The brief lists Module 1 topics in four separate paragraphs (architecture, Mayer principles, cognitive load, worked examples) and ends with the screen. No paragraph changes the screen, and two of the cited ideas actually conflict on it.

The diagnosis: this is a summary, not a synthesis. The principles sit side by side and never interact.

**After (integrated decision, tension resolved).** Goal: novices perform a 7-step shutdown procedure correctly and unaided by the end.

1. Cognitive architecture and Mayer: narration carries the step explanations while the diagram shows the equipment, splitting load across channels; the duplicated on-screen paragraph is removed (redundancy).
2. Cognitive load: the 7 steps have high element interactivity for novices, so the screen is segmented into three learner-paced chunks (intrinsic-load management).
3. Worked examples and fading: chunk 1 is a fully worked demonstration, chunk 2 a completion problem, chunk 3 an unaided attempt.
4. Named tension and resolution: the worked-example principle pushed toward showing more support, while the redundancy principle pushed toward removing on-screen text. For these novices, the worked-example need governs the support amount, but the support is delivered as narration plus diagram, not duplicated text, so both principles are honored rather than ignored.

**Rejected alternative recorded.** Presenting all 7 steps on one screen "so learners see the whole procedure." Rejected: it overloads working memory for novices and removes the faded sequence.

The improvement is that four principles converge on one screen, their conflict is named and resolved for a stated learner, and the rationale is auditable.

Drive every principle toward one decision and resolve conflicts on the page:

1. **Anchor to one decision.** Name the single screen or move the synthesis is about; principles that change nothing about it do not belong in the brief.
2. **Surface the conflict.** For each pair of principles you apply, ask whether they pull the same way; if not, the conflict must be named, not buried.
3. **Resolve by ownership for a stated learner.** Rank the conflicting principles by letting each govern the variable it legitimately owns (amount versus form, access versus pacing), and write the warrant.

If your brief reads as four correct paragraphs that never touch each other, you have summarized, not synthesized.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is that covering all the topics is synthesis; parallel correct paragraphs are a summary. The second is forcing a false harmony by pretending two principles agree when they conflict, which usually means quietly dropping one.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Watch the four principles collide and get resolved on the shutdown screen. Architecture and modality say split the explanation across narration and diagram; redundancy says do not also print the same words; cognitive load says segment seven high-interactivity steps for novices; fading says make the first chunk worked and the last unaided. So far they cooperate. The collision appears between worked-example (show more support) and redundancy (show less text). A summary would list both and move on. The synthesis asks which variable each principle owns: worked-example owns *how much* support a novice needs, redundancy owns *what form* that support takes. Let each govern its own variable and the conflict dissolves into a single coherent decision, support delivered generously but as narration-plus-diagram rather than duplicated text. The rejected all-on-one-screen alternative is named because a defensible synthesis records the path not taken. That is design under tension, and it is the skill Module 1 was building toward.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

Read your own synthesis and hunt for the conflict; if you cannot find one, you have probably summarized. A strong reader can point to the single decision the brief is about, name at least one principle pair that pulls in opposite directions, and state which principle governs which variable for the named learner. Keep a two-column note: principle pair on the left, the variable each owns and the resolution on the right.

A strong reader of the synthesis studio can answer four questions:

1. What single decision do all the principles in this brief act on?
2. Which two principles conflict on it?
3. Which variable does each conflicting principle legitimately govern?
4. How would the resolution change if the learner became an expert?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The synthesis is strong when multiple principles converge on one decision, at least one conflict is named and resolved by ownership for a stated learner, and a rejected alternative is recorded. Four parallel correct paragraphs fail; a single decision with a named, resolved tension passes. If the brief lists principles without a conflict, mark the gap and ask BigAL to find where two of your principles disagree on the screen rather than to restate each principle.

A useful final entry would read: "I converged architecture, redundancy, load, and fading on the shutdown screen and resolved the worked-example versus redundancy conflict by ownership (amount versus form) for novices. I rejected the all-on-one-screen alternative because it overloads working memory and removes the fade."

## Transfer Case

Re-run the synthesis for experienced technicians refreshing on the shutdown after a procedure change. The conflict resolution flips: by the expertise-reversal effect, the worked-example support that governed the novice screen now adds little, so the redundancy and segmenting pressures relax and a single concise reference screen may be the right call. The synthesis discipline is unchanged, anchor to one decision, name the conflict, resolve by ownership for the stated learner, but the resolution moves because the learner moved. A designer who synthesized by naming the governing variable and the learner can re-derive the expert version; one who listed four topics has nothing to re-run.
