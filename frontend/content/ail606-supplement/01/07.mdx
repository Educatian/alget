# Design Rationale as Evidence

![Design Rationale as Evidence textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/ail606-supplement/01-07-design-rationale-as-evidence.png)

## Why This Section Matters

A finished prototype shows what you decided; it does not show why, what you rejected, or what you traded away. Six months later, when a stakeholder asks "why is Screen 4 a branching scenario and not a quiz?", a polished screen cannot answer, but a design rationale can. This section treats rationale as the load-bearing evidence of design competence. Drawing on design-rationale traditions (the question-option-criteria approach and Toulmin's structure of an argument), it asks you to record not just the decision but the *argument* for it: the claim, the data that grounds it, the warrant connecting them, and the alternatives you weighed and discarded. Your rationale log is the artifact that makes your reasoning auditable and your design revisable by someone other than you.

## Learning Targets

- Distinguish a description of a design (what it contains) from a rationale (why, with alternatives weighed).
- Structure a rationale entry as claim, evidence, warrant, and rejected alternatives.
- Document a bounded AI/software support move with accepted and rejected suggestions.
- Use annotations, support requests, and revision notes to decide what help or design change should come next.

## Opening Case

A rationale log entry reads: "Screen 4 has a branching scenario with three choices, a progress bar, and a Submit button. The colors match our brand." Every word is true and none of it is rationale. It catalogs what the screen contains and says nothing about why a branching scenario rather than a quiz, what evidence motivated it, or what was considered and rejected. A reviewer learns the screen's contents but cannot evaluate, challenge, or improve a single decision. The case is not "describe the screen better." It is "turn the description into an argument a skeptic could attack."

## Core Concept

A design rationale is an argument, and arguments have parts. Borrowing Toulmin's structure, a strong rationale entry exposes:

- **Claim:** the decision (use a three-choice branching scenario on Screen 4).
- **Data/evidence:** the observation that motivated it (pilot testers passed a recall quiz but still made the target error in the follow-up task).
- **Warrant:** the principle that licenses moving from data to claim (decision practice with consequences builds judgment that recall does not).
- **Alternatives and rebuttal:** the options weighed and why they lost (a quiz tested recall, not judgment; a five-branch tree exceeded the build window without adding distinct decision types).

The discipline behind this is sometimes called **design rationale capture** in the question-option-criteria tradition: for each design question, record the options considered and the criteria that decided among them. The reason it matters is **knowledge transfer and revisability**. A description lets a reviewer admire the design; a rationale lets them re-decide it when the situation changes. The most common failure is the **gulf between description and rationale**: listing components feels like documentation but records no reasoning, so the design cannot be defended or safely revised.

## Worked Example

A thin entry describes contents:

> Screen 4 has a branching scenario, a progress bar, and a Submit button.

A rationale entry argues for the decision:

> Claim: use a three-choice branching scenario on Screen 4. Evidence: five of six pilot testers passed a multiple-choice version but still made the target error afterward. Warrant: the goal is judgment under consequence, which recall items do not exercise. Rejected: a quiz (tested recall, not judgment) and a five-branch tree (exceeded the two-week window without adding distinct decision types).

The improvement is that a reviewer can now reconstruct why Screen 4 looks as it does, see what was traded off, and challenge a specific decision.

### Annotated Before/After: From Description to Rationale

Here is one entry in a design-rationale log for an onboarding module, revised so it functions as evidence rather than description.

**Before (description).** "Screen 4 has a branching scenario with three choices, a progress bar, and a Submit button. The colors match our brand."

The diagnosis: this lists what the screen contains. A reviewer learns nothing about why these decisions were made or whether they were reasoned.

**After (rationale as evidence).**

1. Decision: use a three-choice branching scenario on Screen 4.
2. Motivation and evidence: pilot observation showed five of six testers passed a multiple-choice version while still making the target error in the follow-up task, so we needed decision practice with consequences, not recall.
3. Alternatives weighed: a multiple-choice item (rejected, it tested recall not judgment) and a five-branch tree (rejected, the extra branches raised authoring time past our 2-week window without adding distinct decision types).
4. Expected effect and check: testers should now encounter the consequence of a wrong choice; we will confirm by comparing follow-up-task error rates before and after.

**Rejected suggestion.** An AI writing aid offered to "polish the log into smoother prose." Rejected: smoother prose without the motivation, alternatives, and check would hide the very reasoning the log exists to expose.

The improvement is that a reviewer can now reconstruct why Screen 4 looks as it does, see what was traded off, and challenge a specific decision.

Write rationale as an argument with all four parts present, or it is description:

1. **State the claim and the evidence together.** A decision with no motivating observation is an assertion; an observation with no decision is a note.
2. **Make the warrant explicit.** Name the principle that licenses the move from evidence to claim, so a reviewer can challenge the principle, not just the taste.
3. **Record at least one rejected alternative and why it lost.** A decision with no discarded options was never really a decision.

If an entry has no rejected alternative and no warrant, it is a description wearing the costume of a rationale.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is that more detail equals rationale; a longer list of components is still a description. The second is letting an AI tool "polish" the log into smooth prose, which often deletes the rough but essential parts (the alternatives, the warrant, the planned check) because they read awkwardly.

 The third is treating rationale as a post-hoc justification written after the design is locked; captured after the fact, it tends to rationalize rather than record, and it cannot inform the decision it claims to explain.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Take the Screen 4 description and interrogate it like a skeptic. "Branching scenario, three choices" tells you the claim but hides the argument, so ask: what evidence motivated branching over a quiz? The pilot data (passed recall, still erred in practice) supplies it. Ask: what principle connects that data to the choice? The warrant that judgment-under-consequence is not built by recall. Ask: what else was on the table? A quiz and a five-branch tree, each rejected for a stated reason. Ask: how will we know it worked? A planned before-and-after comparison of follow-up error rates. Each question converts a piece of description into a piece of argument, and only when all four are present can a reviewer attack the design at a specific joint rather than shrug at a feature list. That is the difference between documentation that looks complete and evidence that is defensible.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

Read every design statement and ask "is this a claim, evidence, a warrant, or a rejected alternative, or is it just a description?" A strong reader can take a paragraph of design notes and label which Toulmin part each sentence supplies, then name what is missing. Keep a two-column note: the statement on the left, the argument part it provides (or "description, no argument") on the right.

A strong reader of design rationale as evidence can answer four questions:

1. What is the claim, and what evidence motivates it?
2. What warrant licenses moving from the evidence to the claim?
3. Which alternatives were weighed, and why did they lose?
4. How will we check whether the decision worked?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The log is strong when each entry contains a claim, motivating evidence, an explicit warrant, at least one rejected alternative with a reason, and a planned check. A component list fails; a four-part argument with a verification plan passes. If an entry has no rejected alternative or no warrant, mark the gap and ask BigAL to surface the discarded options and the licensing principle rather than to smooth the prose.

A useful final entry would read: "I argued for the branching scenario from pilot error data under a judgment-not-recall warrant, recorded the rejected quiz and five-branch alternatives, and set an error-rate check. I rejected the AI's prose-polishing because it would have erased the alternatives and the warrant."

## Transfer Case

Move the rationale into a high-stakes context: a regulated compliance course where an auditor will later ask why each instructional decision was made. Now the rationale is not just good practice; it is the legal record. The four-part structure does not change, but the standard for evidence and the planned check tighten, because "pilot observation" may need to become documented validation. The same description-versus-rationale gulf that hurt a low-stakes onboarding module becomes a liability here: a feature list cannot survive an audit, while a claim-evidence-warrant-alternatives entry can. A designer who captured rationale as argument can hand the auditor a defensible trail; one who only described screens has nothing to show.
