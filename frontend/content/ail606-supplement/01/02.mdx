# Mayer Principles as Design Constraints

![Mayer Principles as Design Constraints textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/ail606-supplement/01-02-mayer-principles-as-design-constraints.png)

## Why This Section Matters

Mayer's principles are usually taught as a checklist of good practices. That framing is a trap. A checklist invites you to apply every item every time, but the principles routinely pull in opposite directions, and several reverse depending on the learner's expertise. This section reframes them as *constraints*: conditional rules that license or forbid a specific design move given a stated learner and goal. The artifact, your diagnosis table, becomes the place where you write which principle governs a given frame, which it overrides, and on what condition. A designer who can name the boundary condition of a principle is doing instructional design; a designer who only recites the principle is decorating.

## Learning Targets

- State each principle you use as a conditional rule with its boundary condition (learner expertise, modality, pacing).
- Resolve conflicts between principles by naming which one governs and why.
- Document a bounded AI/software support move with accepted and rejected suggestions.
- Use annotations, support requests, and revision notes to decide what help or design change should come next.

## Opening Case

A tutorial slide arrives for review: a pump diagram, a 120-word paragraph that both narration and on-screen text repeat verbatim, a looping factory video in the background, and a corner legend naming the parts. Three of Mayer's principles fire at once here, and they do not all point the same way. Coherence says delete the video. Redundancy says delete the duplicated text. Spatial contiguity says move the legend onto the parts. The decision is not "apply Mayer." It is "which principles apply to *this* learner, in what order, and where do they conflict."

## Core Concept

Mayer's twelve principles cluster into three jobs, and each job answers a different design question.

- **Reduce extraneous processing:** coherence (cut the irrelevant), signaling (cue the relevant), redundancy (do not narrate and print the same words), spatial contiguity (put words near their picture), temporal contiguity (sync words and picture in time).
- **Manage essential processing:** segmenting (let the learner pace chunks), pre-training (teach component names first), modality (offload words to the auditory channel).
- **Foster generative processing:** multimedia (words plus pictures beat words alone), personalization (conversational voice), voice (human over machine narration).

Two features make these *constraints* rather than tips. First, **boundary conditions.** The modality and redundancy principles are strongest for novices and for fast-paced, system-controlled material; they weaken or reverse for experts and for learner-paced text (the [expertise-reversal effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expertise_reversal_effect)). Second, **conflict.** On the pump slide, the worked-example impulse to show more text collides with redundancy. You cannot satisfy both by default; you must rank them for the stated learner. Writing the rank, and the condition that justifies it, is the work the table records.

## Worked Example

A thin claim hides which principle did the work:

> I made the slide cleaner.

A constraint-grounded claim names the rule and its condition:

> For novice learners on a system-paced slide, the redundancy principle forbids identical narration plus on-screen text, so I deleted the printed paragraph and kept narration. I offered a captions toggle so the redundancy is opt-in for learners who need text, rather than forced on everyone.

The improvement is that each edit is licensed by a named principle and a stated learner condition, and the exception (the toggle) is justified, not ad hoc.

### Annotated Before/After: One Slide, Three Principles

Here is a single tutorial slide revised by applying Mayer's principles as constraints.

**Before.** A slide shows a pump diagram on the left and, on the right, a 120-word paragraph that both narration and on-screen text repeat word for word. A looping background video of a factory plays behind the slide. Labels for the inlet, piston, and outlet sit in a legend box in the bottom corner.

1. Coherence violation: the background factory video is interesting but extraneous; it competes for visual capacity. Decision: remove the background video.
2. Redundancy violation: narration and identical on-screen text duplicate the verbal stream for these novice learners. Decision: keep the narration, delete the 120-word printed paragraph.
3. Spatial-contiguity violation: the inlet, piston, and outlet labels live in a corner legend, away from the parts. Decision: move each of the three labels directly onto the diagram part it names.

**After.** Plain background; pump diagram centered with the three labels placed on the inlet, piston, and outlet; a 22-second narration carries the explanation; no duplicated paragraph.

**Rejected suggestion.** An AI tool proposed adding numbered captions under the diagram restating the narration "for accessibility." Rejected for novices on pedagogical grounds: it reintroduces the redundancy the revision just removed. A captions-on toggle is offered instead so learners who need text can opt in, rather than forcing it on everyone.

The point is not that the slide is cleaner. Each deletion or move is licensed by a named principle and a stated learner condition.

## Decision Rule

Treat each principle as an if-then with a boundary condition, then resolve conflicts explicitly:

1. **Name the condition:** Is the learner a novice or expert? Is the material learner-paced or system-paced? Modality and redundancy effects depend on these.
2. **Apply the governing principle:** Coherence and contiguity rarely backfire, so apply them first. Modality, redundancy, and segmenting are conditional; apply them only when the condition holds.
3. **Rank on conflict:** When two principles disagree on a frame, write which governs for this learner and the reason. An unranked conflict is an undecided design.

If you cannot state the boundary condition, you are using the principle as a slogan, not a constraint.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is "more principles applied equals better design." Applying personalization, signaling, and segmenting to a frame that only had a redundancy problem adds work without addressing the issue. The second is forgetting reversal: stripping on-screen text for an expert who reads faster than your narrator can speak inserts a new extraneous delay. The third is treating accessibility and redundancy as the same axis; the fix is learner-controlled options (a toggle), not forcing duplication on everyone.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Walk the pump slide through the constraints in order. Coherence is unconditional here: the factory video serves no objective, so it goes, no learner condition needed. Spatial contiguity is also robust: the corner legend forces back-and-forth scanning between word and part, a split-attention cost for any learner, so the labels move onto the parts. Now the conditional principle: redundancy. For these novices on a system-paced slide, identical narration plus print is duplication, so the printed paragraph goes and narration stays. But notice you had to check the condition first; for a self-paced reference where learners scan at their own speed, keeping the text could be the better call. The walkthrough shows the discipline: unconditional principles applied freely, conditional principles applied only after naming the learner, conflicts ranked on the page.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

Read each principle and immediately ask "under what condition does this reverse?" A strong reader never writes a principle without its boundary condition and never resolves a slide without naming which principle governed when two collided. Keep a two-column note: principle on the left, the learner condition that licenses or forbids it on the right.

A strong reader of Mayer's principles as constraints can answer four questions:

1. Which principles fire on this frame, and do any conflict?
2. What learner condition (expertise, pacing, modality) governs each conditional principle?
3. When two principles disagree, which governs for this learner, and why?
4. Which of my edits would reverse if the learner became an expert?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The table is strong when each design edit cites the principle that licensed it and the boundary condition under which that principle holds. "Removed text" is weak; "removed duplicated text per the redundancy principle, valid because these learners are novices on a system-paced slide" is strong. Treat polish as the last layer. If an edit names no principle, or names a principle with no condition, mark the gap and ask BigAL to compare the same edit under a novice versus an expert learner rather than to restate the principle.

A useful final entry would read: "I deleted the printed paragraph under redundancy for novices and rejected the AI's caption suggestion because it reintroduced the duplication; the captions toggle preserves access without forcing duplication on everyone."

## Transfer Case

Move the slide into a self-paced job aid for experienced technicians who consult it while servicing a pump. The [expertise-reversal effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expertise_reversal_effect) now bites: these readers scan faster than narration plays, so narration becomes the extraneous delay, and the printed text you deleted for novices is exactly what an expert wants to skim. The redundancy principle has not changed; its boundary condition has flipped because the learner and the pacing changed. A designer who wrote "narration is better" cannot explain the reversal; a designer who wrote "modality governs for novices on system-paced material" can predict it.
