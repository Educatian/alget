## Why This Section Matters

Every choice you make as a multimedia designer, where to put a label, whether to narrate or print a sentence, how fast a frame advances, is ultimately a bet about what the learner's mind can hold and process at that instant. If your mental model of that mind is vague, your design decisions become matters of taste. This section gives you the model that the rest of AIL 606 runs on: the human cognitive architecture proposed by Mayer and by Sweller, with its three memory stores and two sensory channels. The artifact you build against it, a cognitive load diagnosis table separating intrinsic, extraneous, and germane demand, is the instrument that turns "this looks busy" into "this frame pushes seven interacting elements through one channel for a novice."

## Learning Targets

- Map any multimedia frame onto the dual-channel, limited-capacity, active-processing architecture.
- Separate intrinsic, extraneous, and germane load and assign each a source in the artifact.
- Document a bounded AI/software support move with accepted and rejected suggestions.
- Use annotations, support requests, and revision notes to decide what help or design change should come next.

## Opening Case

A designer opens a finished water-cycle screen: a clean animation on the left, a tidy paragraph on the right, brand colors throughout. It looks professional. But "professional" is not a load claim. The screen says nothing about whether a novice can follow it, and the diagnosis table is still blank where it should read which channel each element enters and how many pieces must be juggled at once. The case is not "make it prettier." It is "decode what this screen demands of working memory, then revise the demand."

## Core Concept

Multimedia learning theory rests on three empirical assumptions about the human mind, and the whole diagnosis table is just these three assumptions made operational.

1. **Dual channels.** People process visual/pictorial material and auditory/verbal material through partially separate systems (Paivio's dual coding, Baddeley's working memory). Printed text is a special case: it enters through the eyes, so it loads the visual channel even though it is verbal in nature.
2. **Limited capacity.** Each channel can hold only a few elements at once. For genuinely novel material a common working estimate is roughly four to seven interacting elements before the learner saturates and comprehension collapses.
3. **Active processing.** Meaningful learning requires the learner to select relevant material, organize it into a coherent structure, and integrate it with prior knowledge in long-term memory. Passive exposure does not build a schema.

From these assumptions Sweller distinguishes three demands the table must keep separate:

| Load type | Source | Designer's job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Intrinsic | Element interactivity of the content for *this* learner | Manage by sequencing, pre-training, segmenting; never delete the concept |
| Extraneous | Poor presentation: split attention, redundancy, clutter | Reduce or remove |
| Germane | Effort the learner spends building the schema | Protect and provoke |

The single most consequential move in the table is correctly attributing demand. "Too hard" is not a diagnosis; it conflates intrinsic with extraneous and licenses no specific fix. When you write a row, you are forced to ask: is this difficulty in the *material* or in the *presentation*?

## Worked Example

The weak version below sounds plausible, but it does not yet give a reader enough evidence:

> I made the cognitive load diagnosis table with intrinsic/extraneous/germane load evidence clearer.

That claim is too thin because "clearer" does not identify the channel, the element count, or which of the three loads moved. A stronger entry would read:

> Frame 3 routed a 90-word paragraph and an animation through the same visual channel. I moved the paragraph to narration (auditory channel), which dropped the visual element count for a novice from roughly nine to four. The extraneous load fell; the intrinsic load (the water-cycle phases) was untouched.

The improvement is not longer wording. It names the channel, quantifies the element count, and states which load changed and why.

### Annotated Before/After: Routing the Two Channels

Here is one storyboard frame revised against the dual-channel and limited-capacity assumptions.

**Before (frame 3).** On-screen: an animation of the water cycle plays while a 90-word printed paragraph describing evaporation, condensation, and precipitation sits beside it. Reader-action note: "watch and read."

1. Diagnosis: the animation and the 90-word paragraph both enter the visual channel, so they compete for the same limited capacity (estimated 5 to 7 interacting elements for a novice).
2. Design decision: convert the printed paragraph to spoken narration (about 22 seconds) timed to the matching animation segment, and delete the on-screen paragraph.
3. Rejected suggestion: an AI tool proposed keeping the paragraph and also adding a caption track. Rejected, because that adds a third visual-channel element and worsens the same overload.

**After (frame 3).** On-screen: the same animation with brief signaling labels (3 words each) appearing as each phase begins; narration carries the explanation; no competing paragraph. Reader-action note: "predict the next phase before it animates."

The improvement is not that the frame is prettier. The verbal load now travels through the auditory channel while the visual channel carries only the animation plus minimal labels, keeping the count of interacting elements within working-memory capacity, and the prediction prompt provokes the germane processing that builds the schema.

## Decision Rule

Diagnose before you decorate. For any frame, run the load triage in this order:

1. **Channel audit:** List every element and the channel it enters. Two simultaneous demands in one channel is a split-attention or redundancy flag.
2. **Capacity estimate:** Count interacting elements for the *target* learner, not for yourself. Novices interact more elements than experts because they lack the schema that chunks them.
3. **Load attribution:** For each demand, is it intrinsic (in the content), extraneous (in the presentation), or germane (the productive struggle)? Cut extraneous, sequence intrinsic, protect germane.

If a revision lowers extraneous load but also strips a prediction or self-explanation prompt, you have traded away germane processing and probably made learning worse, not better.

## Common Misreadings

The most common error is treating the architecture as a style guide ("less text, more pictures"). It is a capacity model. A picture-heavy frame can still overload a single channel, and a text-only frame can be fine for an expert whose schema chunks the words. The second error is calling all difficulty "load to be removed." Germane load is difficulty you want; an interface that removes the learner's need to predict, explain, or organize has lowered effort and lowered learning at the same time.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Take the screen from the opening case and run it through the architecture row by row. First, the elements: animation (visual), paragraph (visual, because printed text enters the eyes), brand banner (visual, irrelevant). Three demands, all in one channel, one of them extraneous. Limited capacity says a novice cannot hold the animation and a 90-word paragraph simultaneously, so the two compete and selection fails. The architecture predicts the fix before you reach for a tool: offload the verbal stream to the unused auditory channel. Notice what the model did not tell you to do, namely make it prettier. It told you to rebalance the channels and protect the one demand (predicting the next phase) that drives active processing. That is the difference between a design grounded in theory and a design grounded in preference.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

Read this section with the diagnosis table open, not your stylesheet. For each frame you encounter, write three lines: which channel each element loads, your element-count estimate for a novice, and the load type of each demand. A strong reader of cognitive architecture can predict where a learner will saturate before testing it, and can say which of the three assumptions, dual channels, limited capacity, or active processing, a given design choice serves or violates.

A strong reader can answer four questions:

1. Which channel does each element load, and do any compete?
2. How many interacting elements does a novice face here, and where is the ceiling?
3. Which difficulty is intrinsic, which extraneous, which germane?
4. What would the element count become for an expert, and would the design still be justified?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The table is strong when every row attributes a demand to a channel and a load type, with an estimate a reviewer could dispute. "Frame 3 is confusing" is not a row. "Frame 3 routes two verbal-visual elements through one channel; novice count about nine; extraneous, fixable by narration" is a row. Treat brand polish as the last layer. If a row names a load but not its source, mark the gap and ask BigAL for a side-by-side of the same frame under a novice versus an expert capacity estimate, not a general definition of cognitive load.

A useful final entry would read: "I rebalanced frame 3 across channels because the visual count exceeded a novice's capacity. I rejected the caption-track suggestion because it reintroduced a third visual element and undid the rebalancing."

## Transfer Case

Now move the same screen to an expert audience: graduate hydrologists reviewing the model for accuracy. The intrinsic load collapses, because their schema chunks the whole water cycle into one element, so the 90-word paragraph is no longer competing with much. The narration you added for novices may now feel slow and become extraneous itself (the expertise reversal effect). The architecture has not changed; the capacity estimate has, because the learner changed. If your only justification was "narration is better than text," you cannot explain why the right answer flips. If your justification was a channel-and-capacity estimate for a named learner, you can.
