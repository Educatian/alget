## Why This Section Matters

The previous sections gave you the architecture and the principles. This section is about the clinical skill that uses them: diagnosis. A doctor who can only say "the patient feels unwell" cannot prescribe; a designer who can only say "the lesson is too hard" cannot revise. Diagnosis means decomposing a single felt difficulty into its three sources, intrinsic, extraneous, and germane, and attributing each to something in the artifact a reviewer could point at. The diagnosis table is the chart. Each row is a differential diagnosis: this much of the difficulty is in the content, this much in the presentation, this much is the productive struggle we must not remove.

## Learning Targets

- Decompose a single difficulty into intrinsic, extraneous, and germane components.
- Estimate element interactivity for a named learner and tie it to a revision.
- Document a bounded AI/software support move with accepted and rejected suggestions.
- Use annotations, support requests, and revision notes to decide what help or design change should come next.

## Opening Case

A pilot tester says a statistics tutorial on interaction effects is "too hard," and the first instinct is to cut content until it feels easy. But "too hard" is a symptom with at least three possible causes, and the wrong cut deletes the very concept being taught. The diagnostic question is not "how do I make this easier?" It is "of the difficulty this learner feels, how much is the inherent complexity of an interaction effect, how much is a confusing rainbow legend, and how much is the desirable effort of predicting a missing cell?"

## Core Concept

Total cognitive load is additive across the three sources and bounded by working-memory capacity. Informally,

$$ L_{total} = L_{intrinsic} + L_{extraneous} + L_{germane} \le C_{wm} $$

The design implication follows directly from the inequality. You cannot raise germane processing (the load that builds the schema) unless there is capacity to spare, and you free capacity mainly by cutting extraneous load, because intrinsic load is fixed by the content for a given learner. So the diagnostic order is forced: cut extraneous first, then sequence intrinsic, then spend the freed capacity on germane effort.

Manipulate the three loads against a fixed capacity and watch the spare capacity available for schema-building. Try cutting extraneous to zero, then see how much germane effort you can add before the total exceeds capacity.

Intrinsic load itself is governed by **element interactivity**, the number of elements that must be held and related simultaneously because they cannot be understood one at a time. A high element-interactivity task for a novice can be low for an expert, because the expert's schema chunks several elements into one. That is why every diagnosis row must name the learner.

| Source | Diagnostic question | Legitimate remedy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Intrinsic | Must these elements be processed together? For whom? | Pre-train, segment, sequence |
| Extraneous | Does the presentation add work unrelated to the goal? | Remove or restructure |
| Germane | Is this effort building the target schema? | Protect; do not optimize away |

## Worked Example

A first draft hides the real decision behind a vague claim:

> I made the cognitive load diagnosis table clearer.

A diagnostic entry separates the three sources and names a remedy for each:

> For a novice who knows main effects but not interactions, the intrinsic load is high (two factors plus their joint effect held at once). The rainbow legend adds extraneous scanning. The predict-the-cell prompt is germane. Remedy: pre-train on a single main effect (intrinsic), label lines on the plot and drop the legend (extraneous), keep the prediction (germane).

The improvement is that each entry names which load it is and what evidence places it there, so a reviewer can challenge a specific cell rather than the whole lesson.

### Annotated Before/After: A Three-Column Diagnosis

Here is one row of a cognitive-load diagnosis table for a graduate statistics tutorial on interaction effects, revised so each load is correctly sourced.

**Before (single column).** The table has one note: "This lesson is too hard. Make it easier." Target learner: unspecified.

The problem is that "too hard" merges three different loads, so no specific fix follows.

**After (three columns, with the source named).** Target learner: a novice who knows main effects but has not met interactions.

1. Intrinsic load (high): an interaction requires holding two factors and their joint effect at once, which is high element interactivity for this learner. Decision: add pre-training on reading a single main effect before the interaction plot appears.
2. Extraneous load (present): the original plot used a rainbow legend off to the side, forcing back-and-forth scanning. Decision: label the two lines directly on the plot (spatial contiguity) and drop the legend.
3. Germane load (to protect): the learner is asked to predict the missing cell before it is revealed. Decision: keep this self-explanation prompt; it is the processing that builds the schema.

**Rejected suggestion.** An AI tutor offered to "simplify by removing the second factor." Rejected: removing a factor deletes the very concept being taught. The defensible move manages intrinsic load through sequencing, not by cutting essential content.

The improvement is that each entry now names which load it is and what evidence places it there, so the next reviewer can challenge a specific cell rather than the whole lesson.

## Decision Rule

Diagnose in the order the additivity inequality dictates:

1. **Cut extraneous first.** It is free capacity with no cost to the content. Split attention, redundancy, and decorative clutter go here.
2. **Sequence intrinsic second.** If element interactivity is too high for the learner, lower it by pre-training and segmenting, never by deleting the concept.
3. **Spend the freed capacity on germane third.** Add or protect the prediction, self-explanation, or comparison that builds the schema.

A revision that lowers a load without saying which of the three it lowered is not a diagnosis; it is a guess.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is treating "easier" as the goal. The goal is the *right* total load for the learner, which sometimes means adding germane effort, not removing it. The second is letting a tool optimize the surface (recolor the plot, smooth the prose) while the intrinsic source is never named, so the lesson still fails for the same reason.

 The third is forgetting the learner: a row that does not name who the learner is cannot estimate element interactivity and therefore cannot diagnose intrinsic load at all.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Take the "too hard" complaint and run the differential. First ask the extraneous question: is anything in the presentation adding work unrelated to interactions? Yes, the rainbow legend forces eye travel between line and label, so that is extraneous and gets cut by moving labels onto the lines. Now ask the intrinsic question: is the remaining difficulty in the concept itself? Yes, an interaction is irreducibly two factors and their product, high element interactivity for a novice, so you sequence it with pre-training rather than deleting a factor. Finally ask the germane question: is any of the difficulty productive? The predict-the-cell prompt is, so you protect it. One symptom, three sources, three different remedies. That is what diagnosis buys you over "make it easier."

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

Read every difficulty claim as a symptom awaiting decomposition. A strong reader refuses to accept "hard" or "confusing" as a diagnosis and instead writes three lines, one per source, each tied to something in the artifact and to a named learner. Keep a two-column note: the felt difficulty on the left, its decomposed sources and remedies on the right.

A strong reader of cognitive load diagnosis can answer four questions:

1. Of this difficulty, how much is intrinsic, extraneous, and germane?
2. Who is the learner, and what does that do to the element-interactivity estimate?
3. Which remedy fits each source, and which remedies would destroy the concept?
4. Which difficulty here is productive and must be protected?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The table is strong when each row attributes a portion of the difficulty to a named source with artifact evidence and assigns a source-appropriate remedy. "Made it easier" fails; "extraneous scanning from the legend, fixed by on-plot labels; intrinsic interactivity managed by pre-training; germane prediction kept" passes. If a row names a load without evidence, mark the gap and ask BigAL to contrast a novice and an expert estimate of element interactivity for that content rather than to restate the definitions.

A useful final entry would read: "I cut the legend (extraneous), pre-trained the single main effect (intrinsic), and kept the cell-prediction prompt (germane). I rejected deleting the second factor because it removes the concept rather than managing its load."

## Transfer Case

Hand the same tutorial to a doctoral student who already reads interaction plots fluently. The intrinsic load drops sharply, because their schema chunks the two factors and the joint effect into one element, so pre-training that helped the novice now wastes their time and becomes extraneous. The prediction prompt may also be too easy to be germane and could be replaced with a harder transfer item. The three-source framework is unchanged; the estimates move because the learner moved. If your diagnosis named the learner, you can re-run it; if it only said "too hard," you cannot.
