# Learner Variability and UDL

![Learner Variability and UDL textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/ail606-supplement/01-05-learner-variability-and-udl.png)

## Why This Section Matters

Cognitive load theory and Mayer's principles often assume a single representative learner. Real cohorts are not single learners; they vary in prior knowledge, language, attention, sensory access, and motivation, and that variability is predictable, not random. Universal Design for Learning (UDL) starts from a sharp reframe: the disability is often in the *design*, not the learner. A single rigid path creates barriers that have nothing to do with the goal. This section teaches you to build those options in from the start rather than retrofit accommodations later, and to do it without diluting the construct you are actually teaching. Your storyboard becomes a design that anticipates the margins instead of defaulting to the median.

## Learning Targets

- Distinguish the firm learning goal from the (flexible) means of reaching and expressing it.
- Apply the three UDL principles to remove construct-irrelevant barriers without changing the construct.
- Document a bounded AI/software support move with accepted and rejected suggestions.
- Use annotations, support requests, and revision notes to decide what help or design change should come next.

## Opening Case

An assessment reads: "Submit a five-paragraph essay explaining the water-cycle model," with the reading provided only as a PDF and the topic fixed for everyone. It looks rigorous. But ask what the task is supposed to measure: understanding of the water-cycle mechanisms. The five-paragraph timed essay and the PDF-only input are not the construct; they are one arbitrary means fused to it. A multilingual learner, a learner using a screen reader, or a learner who reasons better in diagrams now faces barriers that have nothing to do with whether they understand the water cycle.

## Core Concept

UDL rests on a single design discipline: separate the **firm goal** (the construct, what every learner must demonstrate) from the **flexible means** (how they access, engage with, and express it). Barriers that block the means without testing the goal are *construct-irrelevant* and should be removed; flexibility that would let a learner skip the goal is not allowed.

The framework organizes flexibility along three principles, each tied to a brain network and a design question:

| Principle | Network | Design question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Multiple means of representation | Recognition ("what") | Can every learner access the content? |
| Multiple means of action and expression | Strategic ("how") | Can every learner demonstrate the goal in a workable form? |
| Multiple means of engagement | Affective ("why") | Is the task relevant and sustainable for varied motivations? |

The crucial distinction is **construct-relevant versus construct-irrelevant variance**. If the goal is "explain the mechanisms," then the modality of explanation (written, spoken, diagrammed) is construct-irrelevant and should be flexible; the accuracy of the mechanisms is construct-relevant and stays fixed for all. UDL is not "let students do whatever they want." It is "vary everything except the construct."

## Worked Example

A thin claim treats options as generic generosity:

> I gave students more choices.

A UDL-grounded claim ties each option to a removed barrier while holding the construct:

> The construct is "explain the water-cycle mechanisms." I kept that fixed and added options that are construct-irrelevant: the reading in PDF, tagged HTML, and narrated diagram (access), and submission as essay, recorded walkthrough, or annotated diagram (expression). The rubric still scores mechanism accuracy, so the goal is unchanged.

The improvement is that the predictable variability is anticipated in the design, so options exist for everyone rather than being requested later by a few.

### Annotated Before/After: Designing for the Margins

Here is one assessment task in a graduate module, revised against the three UDL principles.

**Before.** Task: "Submit a five-paragraph essay explaining the water-cycle model." The reading is provided as a single PDF. The topic is fixed for all learners. Reader-action note: "write the essay."

The diagnosis: the construct being assessed is "explain the model," but the design fuses that construct to one rigid means (a timed five-paragraph essay) and one input format (PDF), which adds barriers unrelated to the goal.

**After (firm goal, flexible means).** Construct kept firm: explain the water-cycle model with correct mechanisms.

1. Representation: the reading is offered as PDF, tagged HTML with adjustable text, and a 4-minute narrated diagram, so access does not gate understanding.
2. Action and expression: learners may submit a written explanation, a recorded 3-minute walkthrough, or an annotated diagram with captions. The scoring rubric judges mechanism accuracy, not format.
3. Engagement: learners choose one of three authentic contexts (a farm, a city storm drain, a mountain watershed), and the relevance of the task is stated up front.

**Rejected suggestion.** A reviewer proposed "just let students pick any topic they want" to maximize choice. Rejected: that changes the firm goal and makes the construct unassessable. Choice is offered within the goal, not instead of it.

The improvement is that the predictable variability is anticipated in the design, so options exist for everyone rather than being requested later by a few.

## Decision Rule

Hold the construct, vary the rest, and test each option against the goal:

1. **Name the construct precisely.** Write the one thing every learner must demonstrate. Everything not in that sentence is a candidate for flexibility.
2. **Offer options where variance is construct-irrelevant.** Access format, expression mode, and context are usually irrelevant to the goal and should flex.
3. **Refuse flexibility that changes the construct.** If an option lets a learner skip the goal, it is not a UDL option; it is a different assessment.

If an "option" makes the rubric unable to judge the goal, you have varied the construct, not the means.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading equates UDL with maximal choice; unbounded choice (any topic, any goal) makes the construct unassessable and helps no one. The second treats UDL as retrofitted accommodations for a few; the point is options built in for everyone, so no learner has to disclose a need to get access. The third confuses surface variety with access: three file formats that are all inaccessible to a screen reader add clutter, not representation.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Run the essay task through the firm-goal test. The construct is mechanism accuracy. Ask of each design element: does it test the construct, or does it just happen to be how we have always done it? The five-paragraph form does not test mechanisms; it tests essay composition, a different construct, so it becomes optional. The PDF-only input does not test mechanisms; it tests PDF access, so alternate formats join it. The fixed topic does not test mechanisms; it tests interest in that one scenario, so authentic-context choice joins it. Each addition is licensed because the variance it admits is construct-irrelevant. The rejected "any topic" option fails the same test from the other side: it admits construct-relevant variance and breaks the rubric. The walkthrough shows UDL as disciplined subtraction of irrelevant barriers, not generic generosity.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

Read every task by first naming the construct in one sentence, then auditing each design element for whether it tests that construct or an unrelated one. A strong reader can label each option construct-relevant or construct-irrelevant and justify why it is fixed or flexible. Keep a two-column note: design element on the left, construct-relevant or not on the right.

A strong reader of learner variability and UDL can answer four questions:

1. What is the one construct every learner must demonstrate?
2. Which design elements test the construct, and which test something irrelevant?
3. Where can means flex without touching the goal?
4. Which proposed option would secretly change the construct?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The artifact is strong when the construct is stated in one sentence and every option is justified as construct-irrelevant, with the rubric scoring only the construct. "Added choices" is weak; "construct is mechanism accuracy; format and context flex because they are construct-irrelevant; rubric scores mechanisms only" is strong. If an option's relation to the construct is unstated, mark the gap and ask BigAL to test the option against the rubric rather than to define UDL.

A useful final entry would read: "I held the mechanism-accuracy construct and flexed access format, expression mode, and context because each is construct-irrelevant. I rejected free topic choice because it would make the construct unassessable."

## Transfer Case

Move the task into a licensure exam where the construct must be demonstrated under standardized conditions for legal defensibility. Expression flexibility narrows, because the scoring must be comparable across thousands of candidates, but representation flexibility (accessible formats, assistive technology) becomes a legal requirement, not a nicety. The firm-goal discipline is unchanged; the room for flexible means shrinks on expression and expands on access because the context changed the stakes. A designer who reasoned from the construct can re-derive what flexes; one who only "added choices" cannot tell which choices to keep.
