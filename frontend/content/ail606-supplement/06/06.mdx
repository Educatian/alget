## Core Concept

The core idea in this section is that **Peer Scholarly Critique** should be treated as a relationship among purpose, constraint, evidence, and revision. Purpose names what the learner or audience needs to accomplish. Constraint names what limits the decision. Evidence names the source that makes the decision defensible. Revision shows what changed after the evidence was considered.

Three course anchors shape the reasoning here:

- **Mayer multimedia learning** helps you decide what counts as a meaningful learning or performance demand.
- **cognitive load theory** helps you notice when a design or technology choice creates risk, burden, or unsupported assumptions.
- **UDL learner variability** helps you check whether the artifact works for varied learners, audiences, or use contexts.

Use these anchors as lenses, not as names to drop into a reflection. A useful lens changes what you notice, what you question, and what you revise.

## Worked Example

Start with a thin claim that many students write on a first pass:

> I made the capstone defense evidence board with limitations and transfer claims clearer.

That claim is too thin because "clearer" does not identify the audience, the problem, or the evidence. A stronger claim would read:

> I revised the capstone defense evidence board with limitations and transfer claims so that the intended audience can see the decision, the constraint, and the evidence source. The revision is justified by **cognitive load theory**, and I can point to the exact part of the artifact that changed.

The stronger version treats the capstone defense evidence board with limitations and transfer claims as evidence. It names what changed, links the change to **cognitive load theory**, and leaves a reader able to audit the decision.

## Decision Rule

Use the learning goal first, the media choice second, and the software feature last. A feature is justified only when it reduces unnecessary processing, strengthens useful processing, or makes learner action easier to observe.

Before submitting the capstone defense evidence board with limitations and transfer claims, slow down and check whether the trace can answer three questions:

1. **Purpose check:** What should the audience be able to do after reading or using the artifact?
2. **Evidence check:** Which exact source, observation, annotation, rubric line, data pattern, or policy boundary justifies the revision?
3. **Revision check:** What changed in the artifact, and what suggestion did you reject or modify?

If you cannot answer all three questions, the artifact may be complete, but it is not yet ready as evidence of learning.

## Common Misreadings

A common mistake is to treat multimedia quality as visual polish. A cleaner screen can still increase extraneous load if the learner action is unclear.

The trace should show what cognitive demand was noticed, what support was requested, and which change improved the learner's path.
