## Why This Section Matters

"Great module, really clear, 5 out of 5" is not a critique; it is applause. Scholarly critique is a distinct genre with rules borrowed from academic peer review: every claim about the work must be anchored to a stated criterion, separated from personal preference, and phrased as something the author can act on. The skill cuts both ways. As a reviewer you learn to produce feedback that improves a peer's work; as an author you learn to receive criticism and respond to each point with a reasoned disposition rather than defensiveness or blanket compliance.

This matters because design is a social, reviewed practice. Real instructional products pass through stakeholder review, SME critique, and accessibility audit before launch, and a designer who cannot give actionable feedback or respond to it with judgment will struggle in any of those rooms. The capstone peer critique is a rehearsal for that professional reality, which is why both roles are graded.

## Learning Targets

- Convert praise or preference into criterion-anchored, actionable peer critique using the element-problem-criterion frame.
- Respond to each critique point with a reasoned disposition (accept, modify, or reject), including at least one reasoned rejection.
- Verify an AI-drafted critique against the actual artifact before adopting any of its points.
- Use annotations, support requests, and revision notes to decide what help or design change should come next.

## Opening Case

A reviewer writes, "Great module, really clear and engaging. I would have used a different color for the buttons though. 5 out of 5." The author cannot use a word of it. The praise names no criterion the author could try to preserve, and the one suggestion is the reviewer's personal taste presented as if it were a quality judgment. The review is simultaneously too kind to be useful and too arbitrary to be acted on.

A scholarly version of the same review would anchor each point to a criterion the author already accepts, an objective-to-assessment alignment gap, a missing caption, and would flag the button color explicitly as preference, scoring it on nothing. The author can then act on the criterion-based points and reasonably set the preference aside. That separation, criterion from taste, is the whole craft.

## Core Concept

Scholarly critique is **criterion-anchored, actionable feedback**, and it has a structure on the giving side and a structure on the receiving side. Both are assessable, because a critique only improves an artifact if the author can metabolize it.

On the giving side, each point has three parts:

- **Element:** the specific screen, item, or component being critiqued, so the author knows where to look.
- **Problem:** the observable issue with that element.
- **Criterion:** the standard the problem violates (alignment, accessibility, signaling), which converts an opinion into a reviewable claim.

Match each move in the critique exchange to the role and purpose it serves. The giving side names problems against criteria; the receiving side owes each point a reasoned disposition.

The non-negotiable move is separating criterion from preference. A reviewer who dislikes a color may say so, but must label it as preference and not score it, because preferences are not violations. This is the same discipline a journal reviewer uses when distinguishing "this method is inappropriate for the question" (a criterion-based objection) from "I would have framed the introduction differently" (a stylistic preference the author may ignore).

On the receiving side, the author owes each point a **disposition**: accept (with the change made), reject (with a reason), or modify. Blanket acceptance of all feedback is as weak as blanket rejection; both skip the judgment that the response is meant to demonstrate. A reasoned reject ("the current color already meets the contrast standard, so I keep it") is stronger evidence of design maturity than an automatic accept, because it shows the author evaluated the point against a criterion rather than deferring to the reviewer's authority. UDL and Mayer's principles supply the shared criteria that let reviewer and author argue on common ground instead of trading tastes.

## Worked Example

Here is one peer comment on a classmate's prototype, written two ways. Read the before version, then the annotated after version.

**Before (weak critique):**

> Great module, really clear and engaging. I would have used a different color for the buttons though. 5/5.

That is praise plus a personal preference. The author cannot act on it because no criterion is named. Now the same reviewer's feedback rebuilt as scholarly critique:

**After (annotated critique, with the author's disposition):**

1. **Element + problem + criterion:** Module 2 states an "evaluate" objective, but its only activity is a matching item that asks learners to recall definitions. This fails the objective-to-assessment alignment criterion.
2. **Element + problem + criterion:** The narrated video on screen 3 has no captions, which fails the accessibility criterion for time-based media.
3. **Preference flagged as preference:** The button color is a personal taste, not a criterion, so I am noting it separately and not scoring it.

The author then logs a disposition for each point:

- Point 1: **Accept.** Replaced the matching item with a scenario-judgment task that requires evaluation.
- Point 2: **Accept.** Added captions to screen 3.
- Point 3: **Reject.** The current color meets the contrast standard, so I keep it.

The after version names elements, problems, and criteria the author can act on, separates preference from criterion, and shows reasoned judgment on each point rather than blanket acceptance.

## Decision Rule

Test feedback from both sides before you trust the exchange. A critique only counts if it is actionable; a response only counts if it shows judgment.

1. **Actionability test (giving):** Could the author act on each point without asking what you meant? If a point has no element or no criterion, it is not yet usable.
2. **Separation test (giving):** Is every preference labeled as preference and excluded from scoring?
3. **Disposition test (receiving):** Does each point get an accept, reject, or modify with a reason, rather than a blanket response?

If any test fails, the exchange is applause or compliance, not scholarly critique. Add the missing element, criterion, or disposition.

## Common Misreadings

The most common error is the praise sandwich performed for politeness, where the criterion-based problem is buried so gently between compliments that the author misses it. Kindness is not the goal; actionability is. State the problem and its criterion plainly, and let the criterion, not the softening, carry the respect.

A second error, on the author's side, is treating every reviewer point as a command. Accepting all feedback uncritically produces a Frankenstein artifact that satisfies each reviewer and serves no learner, and it forfeits the judgment the response is meant to show. The strongest responses include at least one reasoned reject, because declining a point on a criterion proves you are evaluating feedback rather than obeying it.

## Textbook Walkthrough

When you review a peer, hold the rubric beside their artifact and force every comment through the element-problem-criterion frame. If you cannot name the criterion a comment violates, you have a preference, and you either drop it or label it as preference and move on. Aim for a small number of criterion-anchored points the author can act on rather than a long list that mixes substance with taste.

When you receive a review, resist the urge to defend or to comply. Take each point and ask what criterion it invokes, then decide: does my artifact actually violate that criterion? If yes, accept and make the change. If no, reject with the criterion that shows you already meet the standard. Write the disposition next to the point so a committee can see not just what you changed but what you declined and why. The walkthrough ends with a critique that improved the work and a response log that demonstrates judgment in both directions.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, practice both roles on a real example: rewrite one piece of praise you have received into a criterion-anchored point, and draft a reasoned reject for one suggestion you disagree with. Strong reading here means recognizing that the hardest move in critique is the honest reject, because it requires you to name the criterion that justifies keeping your choice.

A strong reader of peer scholarly critique can do four things:

1. Anchor every critique point to an element and a criterion.
2. Separate preference from criterion and score only the latter.
3. Respond to each point with an accept, reject, or modify and a reason.
4. Produce at least one reasoned reject rather than blanket compliance.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A strong critique exchange is judged by whether the feedback was actionable and the response showed judgment. On the giving side, the standard fails when a review is praise plus taste; on the receiving side, it fails when every point is accepted without evaluation. The artifact you submit is the round trip: the critique you gave, the dispositions you logged, and the criterion-based reasoning on both. When you are unsure whether a point is criterion or preference, annotate it and request a comparison between a criterion-anchored critique and a preference-driven one.

A final paragraph for the artifact would read like this: "I gave criterion-anchored, actionable feedback and labeled my preferences as preferences. When I received feedback I logged a disposition for every point, accepting the alignment and accessibility fixes and rejecting one point with the criterion that showed I already met the standard."

## Transfer Case

Move the exchange into a workplace review with a power asymmetry, for example feedback from a client or a senior stakeholder rather than a peer. The criterion-anchoring discipline becomes more important, not less: when you must decline a powerful reviewer's suggestion, a reasoned reject grounded in an accessibility or alignment criterion is defensible in a way that "I disagree" is not. The transferable skill is responding to feedback with judgment when the reviewer outranks you, holding to a criterion rather than either capitulating or refusing, which is the situation most professional design review actually involves.
