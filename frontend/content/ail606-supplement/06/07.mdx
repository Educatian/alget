## Core Concept

The core idea in this section is that **Capstone Presentation Narrative** should be treated as a relationship among purpose, constraint, evidence, and revision. Purpose names what the learner or audience needs to accomplish. Constraint names what limits the decision. Evidence names the source that makes the decision defensible. Revision shows what changed after the evidence was considered.

Three course anchors shape the reasoning here:

- **Mayer multimedia learning** helps you decide what counts as a meaningful learning or performance demand.
- **cognitive load theory** helps you notice when a design or technology choice creates risk, burden, or unsupported assumptions.
- **UDL learner variability** helps you check whether the artifact works for varied learners, audiences, or use contexts.

The anchors should do work in your artifact. If Mayer multimedia learning or cognitive load theory does not change a decision, it is only a citation rather than evidence of judgment.

## Worked Example

Here is the opening of a capstone talk written two ways. Read the before version, then the annotated after version.

**Before (feature tour):**

> This is my home screen. Next is the menu. Here is Module 1. Here is the quiz. Here are the results. As you can see, it is engaging and clean.

That is a walkthrough in interface order with one unsupported claim at the end. Now the same project narrated as an argument:

**After (annotated narrative beats):**

1. **Problem beat:** New hires were failing the safety checklist because the existing PDF buried the critical step on page 4. (Slide shows the old PDF.)
2. **Decision beat:** I moved the critical step to its own gated screen with a required action. (Slide shows the new screen only, no script text.)
3. **Evidence beat:** In 3 tryouts, all 3 learners completed the gated step before proceeding; in the old PDF, 2 of 3 had skipped it. (Slide shows the two completion numbers.)
4. **Transfer beat:** The same gating pattern would apply to any sequence with a must-not-skip step, such as consent or calibration. (Spoken, not on the slide.)

The after version moves from problem to decision to evidence to transfer. Each effectiveness claim has an evidence beat the committee can probe, and the slides carry visual evidence while the spoken narrative carries the argument, so the channels are not redundant.

## Decision Rule

Use the learning goal first, the media choice second, and the software feature last. A feature is justified only when it reduces unnecessary processing, strengthens useful processing, or makes learner action easier to observe.

Use the following check when the artifact looks finished but the reasoning may still be hidden:

1. **Purpose check:** What should the audience be able to do after reading or using the artifact?
2. **Evidence check:** Which exact source, observation, annotation, rubric line, data pattern, or policy boundary justifies the revision?
3. **Revision check:** What changed in the artifact, and what suggestion did you reject or modify?

If you cannot answer all three questions, the artifact may be complete, but it is not yet ready as evidence of learning.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Read this section as a small professional decision cycle, not as a list of terms. First, locate the work product: **capstone defense evidence board with limitations and transfer claims**. Then ask what problem the artifact is supposed to solve inside capstone defense. A strong reader can explain the artifact in plain language before naming any theory: who will use it, what decision it supports, and what would count as evidence that it improved.

Next, connect the artifact to the course anchors: **Mayer multimedia learning**, **cognitive load theory**, **UDL learner variability**. The point is not to cite every anchor. The point is to let one anchor change the artifact. If the anchor does not change wording, layout, evidence, timing, or feedback, it has not yet become design reasoning.

Finally, inspect the revision trail. A weak submission says the artifact was improved. A strong submission shows the earlier version, the issue noticed, the support requested, the decision accepted or rejected, and the reason the final version is better for the intended learner or audience.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: **claim** on the left, **evidence** on the right. The claim is the decision you think the section is teaching. The evidence is the exact phrase, example, annotation, support move, or artifact revision that makes the claim inspectable. By the end of AIL 606 06.07, your notes should make it possible to reconstruct your reasoning without asking you to explain it again.

A strong reader of Capstone Presentation Narrative can answer four questions:

1. What is the real decision hidden inside this topic?
2. Which constraint makes the decision difficult?
3. What evidence would persuade a skeptical reviewer?
4. What would you revise if the audience, learner, classroom, or tool changed?
