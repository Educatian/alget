## Why This Section Matters

A feature tour, "here is the home screen, here is the menu, here is the quiz," walks a committee through your interface in click order and proves nothing about whether the design works. A capstone presentation narrative does the opposite: it organizes the talk around an argument, problem to decision to evidence to transfer, and uses the interface only to illustrate that argument. The committee is not evaluating whether your software runs; they assume it does. They are evaluating whether you can explain why it is the right design, which is a rhetorical task, not a demo.

There is also a multimedia-design dimension specific to a live talk. You are presenting two channels at once, slides and speech, and the same principles you applied to your learners apply to your audience. Putting your full script on the slide and reading it aloud violates the redundancy principle and splits the committee's attention exactly when you need it. The presentation is itself an instructional-design artifact, and treating it as one is part of the demonstration.

## Opening Case

A student opens: "This is my home screen. Next is the menu. Here is Module 1. Here is the quiz. Here are the results. As you can see, it is engaging and clean." The committee has now seen the product and learned nothing about the reasoning. The talk is ordered by the interface, not by an argument, and it ends on an unsupported claim ("engaging and clean") with no evidence attached.

The argument-driven version reorders everything around a problem. It opens with the failure the design solves (new hires skipping a critical safety step buried on page 4 of a PDF), states the decision that addresses it (a gated screen with a required action), presents the evidence that it worked (completion rose from one of three to three of three), and closes with where the pattern transfers. Same prototype, entirely different talk, because the spine is an argument rather than a click path.

## Core Concept

A capstone presentation narrative is an **evidence-based design argument delivered across two channels**. It has a structure and a media discipline, and both are graded because both are instructional-design competencies.

The structure is a four-beat argument:

| Beat | What it does | What the committee learns |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Problem | Names the learner failure the design targets | Why this design needed to exist |
| Decision | States the design move that addresses it | What you did, specifically |
| Evidence | Shows the data that the move worked | Whether it actually improved learning |
| Transfer | Generalizes the pattern to other contexts | That you understand the principle, not just the instance |

The four beats form the spine of the talk, and the order is the argument: name the failure, state the move, show the data, then generalize. Put them in sequence.

<sequence-builder mode="order" items='["Problem: name the learner failure the design targets", "Decision: state the specific design move that addresses it", "Evidence: show the data that the move worked", "Transfer: generalize the pattern to other contexts"]'></sequence-builder>

The evidence beat is the one a feature tour never has, and it is the one the committee will probe hardest. Every effectiveness claim ("it is engaging") must be replaced by a beat that shows a measurement ("completion of the gated step rose from one of three to three of three"). A claim without an evidence beat is the spoken equivalent of an unverified iteration entry.

The media discipline is where multimedia principles apply to your own talk. The temporal and redundancy principles say the two channels should carry different, complementary information: the slide carries the visual evidence (the old PDF, the new screen, the two completion numbers) while your voice carries the argument that connects them. Reading the slide aloud collapses two channels into one redundant stream and wastes the committee's working memory. Signaling applies too: a slide should show one thing, cued clearly, not a dense board the audience must search while you talk over it. Designing the talk this way is itself evidence that you can apply the course's theory to a real communication problem.

## Worked Example

Here is the opening of a capstone talk written two ways. Read the before version, then the annotated after version.

**Before (feature tour):**

> This is my home screen. Next is the menu. Here is Module 1. Here is the quiz. Here are the results. As you can see, it is engaging and clean.

That is a walkthrough in interface order with one unsupported claim at the end. Now the same project narrated as an argument:

**After (annotated narrative beats):**

1. **Problem beat:** New hires were failing the safety checklist because the existing PDF buried the critical step on page 4. (Slide shows the old PDF.)
2. **Decision beat:** I moved the critical step to its own gated screen with a required action. (Slide shows the new screen only, no script text.)
3. **Evidence beat:** In 3 tryouts, all 3 learners completed the gated step before proceeding; in the old PDF, 2 of 3 had skipped it. (Slide shows the two completion numbers.)
4. **Transfer beat:** The same gating pattern would apply to any sequence with a must-not-skip step, such as consent or calibration. (Spoken, not on the slide.)

The after version moves from problem to decision to evidence to transfer. Each effectiveness claim has an evidence beat the committee can probe, and the slides carry visual evidence while the spoken narrative carries the argument, so the channels are not redundant.

## Decision Rule

Audit the talk for argument and for channel separation before you rehearse it. A talk ordered by interface, or one that reads its slides, is not yet a defense.

1. **Spine test:** Is the talk ordered by problem-decision-evidence-transfer, not by the order of your screens?
2. **Evidence test:** Does every effectiveness claim have a beat that shows a measurement, not an adjective?
3. **Channel test:** Do the slides carry visual evidence while your voice carries the argument, so neither repeats the other?

If any test fails, you have a demo, not a narrative. Re-spine it around the problem and move the words off the slides.

## Common Misreadings

The most common error is believing the committee wants to see the whole product. They do not; they want the argument, and an exhaustive tour buries it. Show only the screens that serve a beat, and skip the rest even if they took the most work, because effort is not the same as relevance.

A second error is putting the script on the slides as a safety net. Text-heavy slides feel reassuring to a nervous presenter but force the committee to choose between reading and listening, which is the redundancy effect working against you. Trust your voice with the argument and let the slide hold a single piece of evidence; the discipline that improves your learners' experience improves your committee's experience too.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Outline the talk in argument order before you open your slide tool. Write the problem in one sentence, the decision in one, the evidence in one with a number in it, and the transfer in one. If you cannot state the evidence with a measurement, that beat is not ready and no slide will rescue it; go get the number or qualify the claim.

Then build each slide to carry exactly one piece of visual evidence for its beat, and write your spoken line to do the connecting that the slide does not show. Rehearse by talking through the argument with the slides advancing behind you, and listen for the moment you start reading the slide; that is where the channels have collapsed and you should cut the slide text. The walkthrough ends when you can deliver the four beats from the visuals alone, with the argument living in your voice.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, convert your own most click-heavy demo moment into a single argument beat with one supporting visual. Strong reading here means noticing which screens you want to show because you are proud of them versus which screens actually advance the argument, and being willing to cut the former.

A strong reader of a capstone presentation narrative can do four things:

1. Order a talk by problem-decision-evidence-transfer rather than by interface.
2. Replace every effectiveness adjective with an evidence beat that carries a measurement.
3. Split the two channels so slides show evidence and speech carries the argument.
4. Cut a screen that does not serve a beat, regardless of effort spent on it.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A strong presentation narrative is judged by whether a committee could reconstruct your design argument from the talk alone, without re-reading your report. That requires an argument spine, an evidence beat behind every claim, and clean channel separation. The standard fails when the talk is a tour, because a tour conveys the product but not the reasoning the defense is meant to assess. When you are unsure whether a slide carries evidence or just decoration, annotate it and request a comparison between an evidence beat and a feature-tour beat.

A final paragraph for the artifact would read like this: "I structured the talk as a problem-decision-evidence-transfer argument, attached a measurement to every effectiveness claim, and kept the slides carrying evidence while my voice carried the argument. I cut the screens that did not serve a beat even though they were the most polished."

## Transfer Case

Move the talk to an asynchronous format, a recorded video walkthrough for stakeholders who will not be in the room to ask questions. The argument spine survives, but the channel discipline tightens further: with no live questioning, every claim must carry its evidence on first pass, and the redundancy risk grows because viewers cannot interrupt to resolve a confusing slide. The transferable skill is preserving the problem-decision-evidence-transfer argument and the two-channel separation when the audience cannot push back, which is the condition most real design communication, from a client deck to a launch memo, actually faces.
