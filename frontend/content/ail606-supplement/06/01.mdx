# Evidence-of-Iteration Documentation

![Evidence-of-Iteration Documentation textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/ail606-supplement/06-01-evidence-of-iteration-documentation.png)

## Why This Section Matters

A capstone committee almost never disputes that your prototype changed between versions. What they probe is whether the change was driven by evidence or by taste. Evidence-of-iteration documentation is the genre of writing that answers that probe in advance. It is the difference between a portfolio that shows three screenshots of "v1, v2, v3" and a portfolio that shows, for each version, the specific observation that triggered the revision, the design principle that interpreted that observation, and the alternative you considered and declined. In design research this is the distinction between a product log and a design rationale: the product log records what the artifact looks like, while the rationale records why it looks that way and why it does not look the other way.

This matters because instructional design is a defensible-decision profession, not a deliverable-production profession. Your storyboard frame sequence with narration, signaling, and reader-action notes is the deliverable; the iteration record is the evidence that the deliverable is the result of reasoning a peer could reproduce.

## Opening Case

A student submits a four-frame onboarding storyboard with a one-line changelog: "Tightened the visuals in v2." The committee asks a single question: "What told you the visuals needed tightening?" The student has no answer, because the change came from a vague sense that the screen felt crowded. The screen may genuinely be better, but the student cannot demonstrate that it is better, and in a defense, an undemonstrable improvement reads as luck.

Contrast that with a student who can point to a tryout note: three of three testers skipped the lower half of Frame 3. Now "tightening" has a cause, and the revision becomes inspectable. This section is about producing the second kind of record by default, so the design logic behind your storyboard is never hidden inside your own head.

## Core Concept

Evidence-of-iteration documentation is a structured **design rationale**: a record that makes each revision traceable from a triggering observation to a justified change. A defensible iteration entry contains five linked elements, and the linkage is what carries the meaning. Drop any one element and the entry collapses back into a changelog.

| Element | Question it answers | Failure mode if missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Triggering observation | What did you actually see a learner do? | "Felt crowded" (unfalsifiable intuition) |
| Interpretive principle | Which theory explains why that is a problem? | Change with no diagnosis (treats symptom, not cause) |
| Change made | What concretely differs in the artifact? | Vague "improved" with no inspectable delta |
| Result | Did the change move the observed behavior? | Unverified change (no evidence it worked) |
| Rejected alternative | What did you consider and decline, and why? | Hides that you reasoned about trade-offs |

The five elements only carry meaning in order: an observation comes before its interpreting principle, the change follows the diagnosis, the result tests the change, and the rejected alternative defends the path taken. Reconstruct that chain so the linkage is explicit.

Two theory anchors do the interpretive work in this genre. Mayer's multimedia principles tell you whether an observation reflects a real learning demand (for example, learners not encoding a step because the diagram and narration competed for attention). Cognitive load theory names the mechanism (split attention, redundancy, transient information) so the revision targets the cause rather than the surface. The discipline rule is strict: if a cited theory did not change a wording, a layout, a timing, or a sequence, it is decoration, not rationale. The "rejected alternative" line is the single most diagnostic field, because it is the only one that proves you weighed options instead of accepting the first idea.

## Worked Example

Here is one row from a real iteration log for a four-frame onboarding storyboard. Read the before version, then the annotated after version.

**Before (weak log entry):**

> Frame 3: cleaned it up in v2, looks much better now.

That entry cannot be reviewed. It names no problem, no source, and no rejected option. Now the same change, documented as evidence of iteration:

**After (annotated decision):**

1. **Problem observed:** In a 3-learner tryout, all 3 skipped the second half of Frame 3. The frame held 84 words of narration plus a diagram with no pause point.
2. **Source:** Cognitive load theory (split-attention and segmenting). The learner had to hold narration and diagram in working memory at once.
3. **Change made:** Split Frame 3 into 3a (diagram + 38-word narration) and 3b (28-word narration + a "predict the next step" reader-action prompt).
4. **Result:** In the re-test, 3 of 3 learners completed both subframes and 2 attempted the prompt.
5. **Rejected alternative:** I considered only shortening the narration to 50 words in one frame. I rejected it because the segmenting evidence said the pause point, not just word count, was the lever.

The after version is not longer for its own sake. Each numbered line is a thing a reviewer can audit: a problem, a source, a change, a result, and a path not taken.

## Decision Rule

When you finish an iteration entry, run it through a falsifiability test before you trust it: could a skeptical peer disagree with this entry using your own evidence? If the entry only contains praise of the new version, there is nothing to disagree with, which means there is nothing to verify.

1. **Observation test:** Does the entry quote a learner behavior or a measured value, not a feeling about the screen?
2. **Mechanism test:** Does a named principle explain why that behavior is a problem, so the fix targets the cause?
3. **Counterfactual test:** Is there a rejected alternative that shows you chose among options rather than accepting the first?

If any test fails, the artifact may be finished, but the iteration record is not yet evidence. Add the missing line before you move on.

## Common Misreadings

The most common error is treating iteration documentation as a writing-up step performed after the design is done. Reconstructed rationale is weaker than recorded rationale, because memory smooths over the rejected alternatives that make the record honest. Capture the triggering observation in the moment you see it, even as a one-line annotation, and write the full entry while the tryout is fresh.

A second misreading is to let an AI assistant polish the prose of the storyboard while the design logic stays undocumented. A more fluent caption is not a justified caption. Keep the tool on the surface layer and keep the rationale in your own observations.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Trace one revision end to end on your own storyboard. Pick a single frame and ask what a learner is supposed to do on it, not what it is supposed to show. Run a small tryout, even with two or three readers, and write down the first place a reader hesitates, backtracks, or skips. That hesitation is your triggering observation.

Now reach for one principle, not three. If the reader skipped narration while studying a diagram, the split-attention principle is the relevant lens; reach for it and let it dictate the fix (integrate the label into the diagram, or segment the narration). Make the change, re-test the same frame, and record whether the hesitation disappeared. Finally, write the alternative you did not choose. The walkthrough is complete only when someone who never watched your tryout could read the entry and agree the change was warranted.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read this section, keep your own iteration log open and tag each design move you have already made with the five elements above. The diagnostic question is not "is my storyboard good" but "could I defend this specific frame against a committee member who liked the previous version." Where you cannot, you have found the frame whose rationale is still missing.

A strong reader of evidence-of-iteration documentation can do four things:

1. State the triggering observation for any given revision without re-watching the tryout.
2. Name the principle that turned that observation into a diagnosis.
3. Produce the rejected alternative on request.
4. Explain what they would log differently if they ran the tryout again.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A strong iteration record is judged by reproducibility, not by length. A peer should be able to read your entry, look at the two versions, and arrive at the same change you made. That standard fails the moment an entry asserts an improvement the evidence does not show, so treat every "better" as a claim that needs an observation attached. If a frame has visible polish but no triggering observation, mark it with an annotation and request a targeted before/after comparison rather than a general critique.

A final paragraph for the artifact would read like this: "I changed the storyboard frame sequence with narration, signaling, and reader-action notes because a tryout showed learners skipping content I assumed they were reading, and a named principle told me why. I declined a competing fix because it would have changed the surface without addressing the cause the evidence identified."

## Transfer Case

Move the same storyboard into a setting with no tryout learners available, say a tight enterprise timeline where you cannot recruit testers before the deadline. The five-element structure does not disappear; the triggering observation simply changes source. Instead of an observed skip, you might rely on a documented heuristic violation or an analytics pattern from a comparable module. The test of whether you understand iteration documentation is whether you can keep the rationale honest when direct observation is unavailable, substituting the best evidence you can get rather than dropping the evidence requirement entirely.

ALGET uses your annotations and artifact trace to choose support:

- If your note makes a **connection**, show where the connection changes the artifact.

## Final Check

Before you move on, make sure each iteration entry links a triggering observation, an interpretive principle, the concrete change, the verified result, and a rejected alternative. That five-part chain is what turns a changelog into evidence of design judgment.
