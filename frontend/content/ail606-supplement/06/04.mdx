# Final Prototype Quality Review

![Final Prototype Quality Review textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/ail606-supplement/06-04-final-prototype-quality-review.png)

## Why This Section Matters

A single overall score, "9 out of 10, ready to defend," is the least useful thing a quality review can produce, because it hides which problems block the defense behind problems that are merely cosmetic. The purpose of a final prototype quality review is to replace a holistic verdict with a criterion-referenced audit: a fixed set of dimensions, each scored against observable evidence, each tagged with how badly its failure would hurt. This is the difference between summative impression and analytic rubric scoring, and in capstone work the analytic approach is the only one that tells you what to fix first.

The stakes are about triage. A prototype almost always has more flaws than time, so the review's real job is to rank them. A misaligned objective and a slightly inconsistent button position are not equally urgent, and a review that scores them on the same flat scale, or worse, averages them into one number, destroys exactly the information you need to prioritize before the defense.

## Opening Case

A reviewer writes "Looks great, overall quality 9 out of 10, ready to defend." Two weeks later the committee finds that the Module 4 quiz only tests recall while the objective says "analyze," and that two narrated frames have no captions. Both were present when the 9 was awarded; the holistic score simply could not see them, because high visual polish dominated the impression. The 9 was not wrong about polish; it was wrong about what polish measures.

A criterion-referenced review would have caught both. Scoring alignment, accessibility, navigation, and polish as separate dimensions forces the reviewer to look at each one on its own terms, and tagging severity would have flagged the alignment and caption failures as blockers while the polish score stayed high and irrelevant to the go/no-go decision.

## Core Concept

A final prototype quality review is **analytic, criterion-referenced scoring with severity classification**. Three design choices make it work, and abandoning any one returns you to a useless overall score.

First, the criteria are fixed and observable before review begins. You do not invent dimensions while scoring; you decide in advance what quality means for this artifact (objective-to-assessment alignment, accessibility, navigation consistency, visual polish) so the review measures the artifact, not the reviewer's mood.

Second, each score points to evidence. A score of 2 out of 5 on alignment is meaningless unless it names the screen and the mismatch ("Module 4 objective is analyze, quiz asks recall"). Evidence is what makes the score re-checkable by the committee.

Third, and most important, severity is independent of score. A criterion can score low and matter enormously (a captioning gap that blocks access) or score low and barely matter (one off-grid control). The review therefore carries two columns, a score and a severity, because they answer different questions: how good is it, and how much does it matter.

| Severity | Meaning | Effect on defense |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Blocker | Defeats the artifact's instructional purpose or excludes a learner | Must fix before defense |
| Major | Degrades the experience but does not defeat purpose | Fix if time allows; disclose if not |
| Cosmetic | Surface inconsistency with no learning impact | Optional; never blocks a defense |

Severity answers a different question than score, so match each severity tag to its effect on the go/no-go decision before you triage.

The two course anchors supply the criteria themselves. Mayer's principles define what instructional alignment and signaling quality look like; UDL defines the accessibility criteria (perceivable media, multiple means of representation) whose failure tends to register as a blocker. The error the whole method exists to prevent is letting visual polish, the most salient and least consequential dimension, stand in for instructional quality.

## Worked Example

Here is one prototype quality review written two ways. Read the before version, then the annotated after version.

**Before (weak review):**

> Looks great. Overall quality: 9/10. Ready to defend.

That review is a verdict with no criteria, no evidence, and no severity. Now the same prototype reviewed against a rubric:

**After (annotated quality review):**

| Criterion | Score | Evidence | Severity if failing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Objectives-to-assessment alignment | 2/5 | Module 4 objective is "analyze," but the quiz only asks recall | High blocker |
| Navigation consistency | 4/5 | Next control is bottom-right on 7 of 8 screens; screen 5 places it top-left | Medium |
| Accessibility (captions) | 3/5 | 2 of 5 narrated frames lack captions | High blocker |
| Visual polish | 5/5 | Consistent type and spacing throughout | Cosmetic |

Two findings (alignment and captions) are high-severity blockers that the original 9/10 hid behind visual polish. The review now separates what blocks the defense from what is cosmetic, and each score points to a criterion and screen evidence a committee can re-check.

## Decision Rule

When the review is done, prioritize by severity, not by score. The lowest score is not necessarily the first thing to fix; the highest-severity finding is.

1. **Criterion test:** Was each dimension defined before scoring, so the criteria measure the artifact rather than the reviewer's preference?
2. **Evidence test:** Does every non-perfect score name a screen and a specific observation that the committee could re-verify?
3. **Severity test:** Is severity scored separately from quality, so a low-impact flaw cannot masquerade as a blocker and a real blocker cannot hide behind high polish?

If any test fails, the review is still an impression, not an audit. Re-score against fixed criteria with evidence and severity attached.

## Common Misreadings

The most common error is averaging criteria into a single number. Averaging is exactly the operation that lets a perfect polish score (5) lift a failing alignment score (2) into a comfortable composite, which is how blockers disappear. Report the criteria as a profile, never as a mean.

A second error is conflating low score with high severity. A dimension can score poorly and not matter (cosmetic) or score moderately and matter enormously (a partial accessibility gap that still excludes some learners). Keeping the two judgments in separate columns is what makes the review a triage tool rather than a grade.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Write your criteria before you open the prototype. List the four or five dimensions that define quality for this specific artifact and decide what evidence would justify each score level. Locking the rubric first is what prevents you from quietly grading the dimensions the artifact happens to do well.

Then score one criterion at a time, in isolation, recording the screen and observation behind each score. Resist forming an overall impression; the method works because it forbids it. When all criteria are scored, assign severity independently, asking of each failing criterion not "how bad is the score" but "what happens at the defense if this stays." Order your fix list by that severity column. The walkthrough ends with a ranked list of revisions, the alignment and accessibility blockers at the top, the cosmetic items at the bottom or deferred.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, build the criteria list for your own prototype and predict which dimension your visual polish is currently masking. Strong reading here means noticing that the dimension you are proudest of (often polish) is the one most likely to inflate a holistic judgment, and deliberately scoring the unglamorous dimensions (alignment, captions) first.

A strong reader of a final prototype quality review can do four things:

1. Define quality as a fixed set of observable criteria, set before review.
2. Attach screen-level evidence to every score.
3. Score severity independently of quality.
4. Order revisions by severity rather than by lowest score.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A strong quality review is judged by whether someone else could re-run it and reach the same profile. That requires fixed criteria, located evidence, and a severity column that survives scrutiny. The standard fails the instant the review collapses into a single number, because a number cannot be re-derived from the artifact and cannot be triaged. When you are unsure whether a finding is a blocker, annotate the screen and request a comparison between a blocker-level and a cosmetic-level instance of the same criterion.

A final paragraph for the artifact would read like this: "I scored the prototype against fixed criteria with screen-level evidence, kept severity separate from quality, and ordered my revisions by severity. The high polish score did not move the go/no-go decision, because the blockers were an alignment gap and a captioning gap, not the surface."

## Transfer Case

Move the review to a context with a fixed external standard, for example a corporate course that must pass a WCAG accessibility audit before launch. The structure holds, but the severity scale is no longer your judgment alone; an accessibility failure is a hard blocker by policy regardless of how minor it feels, and a polish flaw remains optional regardless of how prominent it looks. The transferable skill is mapping your internal severity column onto whatever external standard governs the deployment, so the same artifact can be triaged correctly under a different definition of "blocker."
