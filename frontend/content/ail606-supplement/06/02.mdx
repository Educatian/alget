# Toolchain Disclosure and AI-USE.md

![Toolchain Disclosure and AI-USE.md textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/ail606-supplement/06-02-toolchain-disclosure-and-ai-use-md.png)

## Why This Section Matters

A blanket statement such as "AI tools were used throughout this project" is technically true and academically useless. It satisfies a disclosure box without letting anyone evaluate what the human actually contributed. Toolchain disclosure exists to solve a specific authorship problem: when generative tools can produce objectives, feedback text, images, and even rationale, a committee needs to know which decisions are yours. The AI-USE.md file is the instrument that answers this, and it answers it at the granularity of individual contributions, not at the granularity of the whole project.

The deeper purpose is calibrating credit and responsibility. If an AI drafted your assessment items and you kept them unchanged, you are responsible for their alignment to your objectives whether or not you wrote them. If you rejected most of an AI's output, that rejection is itself evidence of expertise and belongs in the record. Disclosure is therefore not a compliance afterthought; it is part of the argument that the design judgment in the capstone is your own.

## Opening Case

A reviewer opens two capstones. The first contains the sentence "ChatGPT, Claude, and Midjourney were used to assist this project." The reviewer learns nothing about authorship and has to treat the entire artifact as potentially AI-authored. The second contains a table: ChatGPT drafted Module 3 objectives (kept two, rejected three), Claude drafted quiz feedback (rewrote four of six because the explanations contradicted the key), Midjourney produced a decorative banner (used as-is, flagged as non-instructional). The reviewer can now see exactly where human judgment overrode the tool, and the second capstone reads as more rigorous precisely because it discloses more, not less.

This inversion is the point. Specific, located disclosure builds trust; vague disclosure spends it.

## Core Concept

An AI-USE.md is a **per-contribution provenance record**. Its unit of analysis is the discrete output, not the tool and not the project. Each row makes three things auditable: where the tool acted in the artifact, what it produced, and what human judgment followed. The third column is where authorship is established, because the same tool output can represent very different levels of human contribution.

A useful frame is the gradient of human involvement, adapted from authorship and attribution norms:

| Disposition | What it means | What it signals to a committee |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Generated and kept as-is | Tool output used unchanged | Lowest human contribution; acceptable for low-stakes or decorative content if flagged |
| Generated and edited | Tool output revised by the designer | Human curation; show what changed and why |
| Generated and rejected | Tool output considered and declined | Highest evidence of expertise; the rejection is the contribution |
| Tool-assisted process | Tool used to brainstorm or critique, not to produce final text | Discloses influence on thinking, not on the artifact surface |

Match each disposition to the signal it sends a committee. The same tool output can sit at very different points on this gradient depending on what you did next.

Two norms govern the record. First, locate every contribution: "used throughout" is a non-disclosure because it cannot be checked against any specific element. Second, disclose rejections, not just acceptances; an entry showing you discarded three of five AI-drafted objectives is stronger evidence of instructional expertise than an entry showing you wrote them alone, because it demonstrates you can recognize a misaligned objective when a fluent tool produces one. The danger the file guards against is automation bias: accepting plausible-sounding output (a quiz explanation that contradicts the answer key) because it reads smoothly. The disclosure of an override is the trace that you resisted that bias.

## Worked Example

Here is the same AI-USE.md disclosure written two ways. Read the before version, then the annotated after version.

**Before (weak disclosure):**

> AI tools (ChatGPT, Claude, Midjourney) were used throughout this capstone to support design and writing.

A reviewer cannot locate a single contribution in that sentence. Now the same project disclosed as an auditable table:

**After (annotated disclosure entries):**

| Tool | Where it acted | What I did with the output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ChatGPT | Draft of Module 3 learning objectives | Kept 2 of 5; rejected 3 because they targeted recall, not the analyze-level goal in my rubric |
| Claude | First pass of Module 2 quiz feedback text | Rewrote 4 of 6 items; the AI explanations contradicted the answer key |
| Midjourney | Banner image for the welcome screen | Used as-is; logged the prompt and flagged it as decorative, not instructional |

Notice the three columns: tool, location, and the human judgment that followed. The decorative-image row is honest about a low-stakes use. The two text rows show where I overrode the tool and why. A defense committee can now audit each AI contribution to a specific place in the work, which is exactly what a blanket "used throughout" statement hides.

## Decision Rule

Before you finalize the AI-USE.md, audit it for locatability and for honest rejections. A record that lists only tools and only accepted outputs is hiding the two things a reviewer most wants to see.

1. **Locatability:** Can a reviewer point to the exact screen, item, or asset each entry refers to? If an entry says "throughout," split it.
2. **Disposition clarity:** Does each entry state kept, edited, or rejected, with the reasoning for edits and rejections?
3. **Responsibility match:** For anything kept as-is, are you prepared to defend it as if you authored it, since in attribution terms you now own it?

If you cannot answer all three, the disclosure is not yet defensible, even if every tool is named.

## Common Misreadings

The most damaging misreading is that more disclosure looks worse. Designers sometimes minimize AI mentions to seem more independent, which backfires: an under-disclosed capstone that a committee later suspects of AI authorship is far weaker than one that openly documents and bounds its tool use. Transparency about a rejection is a credential, not a confession.

A second misreading is treating disclosure as separate from the prototype README traceability matrix connecting theory to screen changes. The two documents should agree.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Build the file by walking your own production history, not your final artifact. Open each component (objectives, assessments, feedback, media, narration) and ask one question per component: did a tool touch this, and if so, what did I do next? Write the answer as a row even when the answer is "no tool." The act of asking it for every component is what prevents the silent, undocumented use that erodes trust.

Then test one row against the responsibility standard. Take a row marked "kept as-is" and imagine defending that content's quality to a committee. If you can, the row is fine. If you cannot, that is a signal you outsourced a decision you have not yet evaluated, and the right move is to evaluate it now, edit if needed, and update the disposition. The walkthrough turns disclosure from a clerical list into a final quality pass.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, map every entry to a place where you can defend or revise the human contribution. The test is adversarial: assume the committee suspects every fluent sentence was AI-generated, and check whether your file gives them the evidence to rule that out where it is false and confirm it where it is true.

A strong reader of toolchain disclosure can do four things:

1. Point any disclosure entry to a specific element in the artifact.
2. State the disposition (kept, edited, rejected) and the reason for each non-trivial entry.
3. Defend any kept-as-is output as if they authored it.
4. Show that the disclosure and the design rationale tell the same story.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A strong AI-USE.md is judged by whether a reviewer could reconstruct your authorship boundary from it alone. That boundary is the line between what you decided and what a tool produced. The standard fails when the file is a list of tool names with no locations or dispositions, because such a list moves the authorship question to the reviewer instead of answering it. When an entry's disposition is unclear, annotate the underlying element and request a comparison of a defensible versus an under-disclosed entry rather than a generic explanation of citation.

A final paragraph for the artifact would read like this: "I disclosed each AI contribution to a specific element and stated whether I kept, edited, or rejected it. I am prepared to defend every kept-as-is output as my own, and the rejections I logged are part of my evidence of design judgment."

## Transfer Case

Move the disclosure requirement into a workplace with a strict client confidentiality policy that forbids naming external AI services in deliverables. The provenance principle survives, but the format adapts: instead of a public AI-USE.md, you maintain an internal provenance log and disclose at the level of process ("portions of draft copy were machine-generated and human-reviewed against the alignment rubric") in the deliverable itself. The test of understanding is whether you can preserve locatability and disposition under a constraint that limits how openly you can name the tool, rather than abandoning provenance because the public format is unavailable.
