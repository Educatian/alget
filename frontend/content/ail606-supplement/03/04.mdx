# Narration and Modality Decisions

![Narration and Modality Decisions textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/ail606-supplement/03-04-narration-and-modality-decisions.png)

The single most common multimedia mistake is also the most well-intentioned: a designer adds narration that reads the on-screen text aloud, "so the lesson is multimedia." This duplicates the same words in two channels and, by the redundancy principle, usually depresses learning rather than helping it. The modality decision, whether words should arrive as on-screen text or as narration, is not a production preference; it is a load-management decision grounded in the dual-channel model of working memory, and it has conditions, limits, and an accessibility cost that designers routinely ignore.

This section gives you the actual rule, not the slogan. The modality principle ("present words as speech rather than on-screen text when they accompany a graphic") holds only under specific conditions, transient material, system-paced presentation, words paired with a visual the eyes must watch, and it carries an obligation, because audio that helps a sighted, hearing learner excludes a deaf learner unless an equivalent is provided. Knowing when the principle does not apply is as important as knowing when it does.

## Opening Case

A designer animates the four phases of mitosis, then adds narration that reads aloud, word for word, the full explanatory paragraph already printed on the frame. She is pleased: it now has motion, text, and voice. But a learner watching this frame must split the visual channel between the animation and the identical printed paragraph, while the ear receives nothing new. The eyes lose the animation to re-read words the ear is already delivering. The "richer" frame teaches less than a version with brief labels and audio explanation.

The frame looks more complete because it has more media, and that appearance is the problem. This section asks you to expose the modality logic the extra voiceover hides: more channels are not better; the right words in the right channel, with an access equivalent, is better.

## Core Concept

The foundation is the **dual-channel assumption**: working memory processes visual and auditory information through partly separate channels, each with limited capacity. When a graphic must be watched and words must be processed at the same time, putting the words on screen forces both into the visual channel and overloads it; speaking the words instead spreads the load across both channels. That is the **modality principle**.

But the principle is conditional, and the conditions are where the judgment lives:

- **Transience.** It applies to transient, system-paced material (animation, video). For static text the learner can reread at will, on-screen text is fine, and narration may even hurt because the learner cannot scan back through speech.
- **Redundancy.** Narration must complement the visual, not duplicate on-screen text. Identical words in both channels (the redundancy principle) overload rather than offload; the fix is brief labels on screen plus distinct spoken explanation.
- **Complexity and pacing.** For dense or unfamiliar content, even well-placed narration can outrun the learner; segmenting and learner control become the safety valve.

UDL learner variability supplies the constraint the principle ignores: audio that helps a hearing learner excludes deaf and hard-of-hearing learners, and audio-only delivery fails in sound-off contexts. So a modality decision is never finished until it specifies the **access equivalent** (synchronized captions, a transcript). Cognitive load theory frames the whole choice: the goal is to keep extraneous load down (no redundancy) while keeping germane processing reachable across channels.

## Worked Example: A Modality Decision on One Process Frame

The frame teaches the four phases of mitosis using a short animation. Below is the before/after of the narration and modality choice.

**Before (redundant and overloaded):**

> Frame 3. The animation plays. The full explanatory paragraph is printed on screen, and the narrator reads that paragraph word for word. Caption: added narration so it is multimedia.

Problems: on-screen text and narration are identical (redundancy), and the eyes must split between watching the animation and reading the same words. The visual channel is overloaded.

**After (words offloaded to audio, equivalent provided):**

> Frame 3. The animation plays with brief on-screen labels only (phase names). The narration explains the transitions in concise, learner-paced segments, distinct from the labels. A synchronized caption track carries the narration text for access.

Annotated decision trail:

1. Removed the verbatim paragraph: kept short labels on screen, moved the explanation to narration so the eyes can follow the animation (modality offloads words to audio).
2. Checked the redundancy limit: narration now complements the visual instead of duplicating printed text.
3. Scoped the modality claim: this is transient explanatory content paired with a graphic, the case where narration helps; a reference table on a later frame was left as text.
4. Added an equivalent: captions and a transcript so deaf or hard-of-hearing learners, and those in audio-off settings, are not excluded.
5. Rejected suggestion: the AI offered to keep the full paragraph and also narrate it for emphasis. Rejected, because that restores the redundancy the revision removed.

The improvement is a modality decision with a stated limit and an access equivalent, not just an added soundtrack.

## Decision Rule

Decide modality by interrogating the frame, not by habit. For each block of words, ask the three diagnostic questions:

1. **Is the visual transient and must the eyes watch it?** If yes (animation, a process unfolding), speak the words so the eyes stay on the visual. If the visual is static and rereadable, on-screen text is usually fine.
2. **Are these words already on screen?** If you are about to narrate text the learner can also read, stop, that is redundancy. Reduce the on-screen text to brief labels and let narration carry the distinct explanation.
3. **What is the access equivalent?** Any narration decision is incomplete until you name the caption track or transcript that delivers the same content without sound.

A defensible modality choice names the condition that triggered it (transient visual, paired graphic) and the equivalent that protects access. If you cannot name both, you have a preference, not a decision.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is "narration is always better than text." It is conditional; for static, learner-paced reference material, on-screen text can beat narration, and narrating it adds nothing the learner cannot already control. The second misreading treats captions as optional polish for the modality decision. They are not; once you offload words to audio for load reasons, you have created an access gap, and the captions or transcript are the part of the decision that closes it. A modality choice without an equivalent is an incomplete decision, not a finished one with a nice-to-have missing.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Walk a mixed frame. An onboarding module has two parts: a thirty-second animation of a checkout flow, and a static reference table of return-policy rules. A designer wants one consistent treatment, narrate both. Apply the diagnostics. The animation is transient and the eyes must watch it, so narrate the steps with only short labels on screen (modality applies). The policy table is static, dense, and learner-paced; narrating it would force a linear pace on material learners need to scan and compare, so leave it as on-screen text and do not narrate it (modality does not apply). One frame, two opposite decisions, each tied to the transience condition.

Now close the access gap created by the animation's narration: add a synchronized caption track and a transcript, so a deaf learner gets the step explanations and an audio-off learner is not blocked. The walkthrough shows the principle is not a global setting; it is decided block by block, and every audio decision pulls an accessibility decision behind it.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

Read every "added narration" as a hypothesis with conditions, and check the conditions. For each spoken block in the chapter's examples, verify it is transient and paired with a visual, and confirm the on-screen text is not the same words. Then look for the access equivalent and flag its absence. A strong reader notices both failure modes: the redundant voiceover that duplicates text, and the bare audio with no caption. Keep a note of where the chapter narrates and where it deliberately does not, and recover the condition behind each.

A strong reader of modality decisions can, for any block of words, name the channel chosen, the condition that justifies it, the redundancy it avoids, and the equivalent that keeps it accessible.

On the later reference table I kept text and added no narration, because that content is static and learner-paced. I rejected the suggestion to keep and also narrate the paragraph, which would restore the redundancy I removed."

## Transfer Case

Move the decision to a low-bandwidth or offline mobile context where reliable audio playback cannot be assumed. The modality principle's preference for narration on transient visuals now collides with a delivery constraint: if audio may not play, words offloaded to speech can simply vanish. The transfer test is whether you can keep the dual-channel reasoning while treating the transcript or on-screen text as the primary, not the fallback, channel, perhaps converting an animation to a stepped, learner-paced sequence of static frames so the words can be read. If your modality skill assumes headphones and stable streaming, it does not transfer; the durable skill is choosing channels against the actual delivery conditions, not the ideal ones.
