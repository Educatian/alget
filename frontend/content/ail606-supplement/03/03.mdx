## Core Concept

The core idea in this section is that **Image Generation for Instruction** should be treated as a relationship among purpose, constraint, evidence, and revision. Purpose names what the learner or audience needs to accomplish. Constraint names what limits the decision. Evidence names the source that makes the decision defensible. Revision shows what changed after the evidence was considered.

Three course anchors shape the reasoning here:

- **Mayer multimedia learning** helps you decide what counts as a meaningful learning or performance demand.
- **cognitive load theory** helps you notice when a design or technology choice creates risk, burden, or unsupported assumptions.
- **UDL learner variability** helps you check whether the artifact works for varied learners, audiences, or use contexts.

Use these anchors as lenses, not as names to drop into a reflection. A useful lens changes what you notice, what you question, and what you revise.

## Worked Example: Choosing and Verifying a Generated Instructional Image

The lesson teaches how a lever multiplies force. Below is the before/after of one image decision.

**Before (first generated image, accepted on looks):**

> Prompt: photorealistic woodworking studio, dramatic light, a crowbar resting on a workbench among many tools. The render is striking. Caption: looks great, students will love it.

Problems: the scene is decorative. The fulcrum, load, and effort are not distinguishable, the crowbar sits among competing detail (seductive detail), and nothing was verified against the lever model.

**After (re-prompted toward the learning function, then verified):**

> Learning function named first: the learner must see that moving the fulcrum closer to the load increases mechanical advantage. Prompt: clean labeled line diagram, one lever, fulcrum-load-effort marked, two effort-arm lengths shown side by side. Caption: shows the relationship, then I checked it.

Annotated decision trail:

1. Started from the learning function (fulcrum position changes advantage), not from a style prompt.
2. Cut decorative and seductive detail: removed the studio scene so attention stays on the lever geometry.
3. Verified accuracy: confirmed the diagram labels effort, load, and fulcrum correctly and that the longer effort arm is drawn on the higher-advantage side. The first generated draft had swapped load and effort labels; that was corrected.
4. Rejected suggestion: the AI proposed adding a person pushing the lever for relatability. Rejected, because the figure added detail without clarifying the distance relationship the lesson depends on.

The improvement is not a prettier image. It is an image chosen for its instructional function and verified against the content.

## Decision Rule

Use the learning goal first, the media choice second, and the software feature last. A feature is justified only when it reduces unnecessary processing, strengthens useful processing, or makes learner action easier to observe.

Before submitting the storyboard frame sequence with narration, signaling, and reader-action notes, slow down and check whether the trace can answer three questions:

1. **Purpose check:** What should the audience be able to do after reading or using the artifact?
2. **Evidence check:** Which exact source, observation, annotation, rubric line, data pattern, or policy boundary justifies the revision?
3. **Revision check:** What changed in the artifact, and what suggestion did you reject or modify?

If you cannot answer all three questions, the artifact may be complete, but it is not yet ready as evidence of learning.

## Common Misreadings

A common mistake is to treat multimedia quality as visual polish. A cleaner screen can still increase extraneous load if the learner action is unclear.

The trace should show what cognitive demand was noticed, what support was requested, and which change improved the learner's path.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Read this section as a small professional decision cycle, not as a list of terms. First, locate the work product: **storyboard frame sequence with narration, signaling, and reader-action notes**. Then ask what problem the artifact is supposed to solve inside storyboarding and AI asset generation. A strong reader can explain the artifact in plain language before naming any theory: who will use it, what decision it supports, and what would count as evidence that it improved.

Next, connect the artifact to the course anchors: **Mayer multimedia learning**, **cognitive load theory**, **UDL learner variability**. The point is not to cite every anchor. The point is to let one anchor change the artifact. If the anchor does not change wording, layout, evidence, timing, or feedback, it has not yet become design reasoning.

Finally, inspect the revision trail. A weak submission says the artifact was improved. A strong submission shows the earlier version, the issue noticed, the support requested, the decision accepted or rejected, and the reason the final version is better for the intended learner or audience.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: **claim** on the left, **evidence** on the right. The claim is the decision you think the section is teaching. The evidence is the exact phrase, example, annotation, support move, or artifact revision that makes the claim inspectable. By the end of AIL 606 03.03, your notes should make it possible to reconstruct your reasoning without asking you to explain it again.

A strong reader of Image Generation for Instruction can answer four questions:

1. What is the real decision hidden inside this topic?
2. Which constraint makes the decision difficult?
3. What evidence would persuade a skeptical reviewer?
4. What would you revise if the audience, learner, classroom, or tool changed?
