## Core Concept

The core idea in this section is that **Peer Review of Storyboards** should be treated as a relationship among purpose, constraint, evidence, and revision. Purpose names what the learner or audience needs to accomplish. Constraint names what limits the decision. Evidence names the source that makes the decision defensible. Revision shows what changed after the evidence was considered.

Three course anchors shape the reasoning here:

- **Mayer multimedia learning** helps you decide what counts as a meaningful learning or performance demand.
- **cognitive load theory** helps you notice when a design or technology choice creates risk, burden, or unsupported assumptions.
- **UDL learner variability** helps you check whether the artifact works for varied learners, audiences, or use contexts.

The anchors should do work in your artifact. If Mayer multimedia learning or cognitive load theory does not change a decision, it is only a citation rather than evidence of judgment.

## Worked Example: Rewriting a Peer Comment Into Real Review

A classmate shares a storyboard whose Frame 4 packs three diagrams and a paragraph onto one screen. Below is the before/after of the peer comment.

**Before (praise and taste, not review):**

> "Looks great! I love the colors. I would have used a blue background though."

Problems: general praise plus a personal-preference suggestion. Nothing names a principle, points to the real issue (Frame 4 is overloaded), or gives the author a decision to make.

**After (anchored, actionable, authorship preserved):**

> "Frame 4 may exceed the coherence and segmenting principles: three diagrams plus a full paragraph compete for the visual channel. The learning objective for this frame is to compare two diagrams, so the third diagram and the paragraph may be extraneous. Option: move the paragraph to narration and split the third diagram to its own frame. Your call on which fits your sequence."

Annotated decision trail:

1. Replaced praise with a specific observation tied to a named principle (coherence, segmenting) and the frame's objective.
2. Dropped the personal-taste color comment, which the shared criteria do not support.
3. Offered an option but left the revision choice to the author, so review surfaces the issue without seizing authorship.
4. Author's response recorded: accepted moving the paragraph to narration, rejected splitting the diagram (the comparison needs both side by side), and noted why. That accept/reject record is the design evidence.

The improvement is feedback that is actionable and criteria-anchored, and an authoring decision the author owns.

## Decision Rule

Use the learning goal first, the media choice second, and the software feature last. A feature is justified only when it reduces unnecessary processing, strengthens useful processing, or makes learner action easier to observe.

Use the following check when the artifact looks finished but the reasoning may still be hidden:

1. **Purpose check:** What should the audience be able to do after reading or using the artifact?
2. **Evidence check:** Which exact source, observation, annotation, rubric line, data pattern, or policy boundary justifies the revision?
3. **Revision check:** What changed in the artifact, and what suggestion did you reject or modify?

If you cannot answer all three questions, the artifact may be complete, but it is not yet ready as evidence of learning.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Read this section as a small professional decision cycle, not as a list of terms. First, locate the work product: **storyboard frame sequence with narration, signaling, and reader-action notes**. Then ask what problem the artifact is supposed to solve inside storyboarding and AI asset generation. A strong reader can explain the artifact in plain language before naming any theory: who will use it, what decision it supports, and what would count as evidence that it improved.

Next, connect the artifact to the course anchors: **Mayer multimedia learning**, **cognitive load theory**, **UDL learner variability**. The point is not to cite every anchor. The point is to let one anchor change the artifact. If the anchor does not change wording, layout, evidence, timing, or feedback, it has not yet become design reasoning.

Finally, inspect the revision trail. A weak submission says the artifact was improved. A strong submission shows the earlier version, the issue noticed, the support requested, the decision accepted or rejected, and the reason the final version is better for the intended learner or audience.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: **claim** on the left, **evidence** on the right. The claim is the decision you think the section is teaching. The evidence is the exact phrase, example, annotation, support move, or artifact revision that makes the claim inspectable. By the end of AIL 606 03.07, your notes should make it possible to reconstruct your reasoning without asking you to explain it again.

A strong reader of Peer Review of Storyboards can answer four questions:

1. What is the real decision hidden inside this topic?
2. Which constraint makes the decision difficult?
3. What evidence would persuade a skeptical reviewer?
4. What would you revise if the audience, learner, classroom, or tool changed?
