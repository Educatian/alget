# Peer Review of Storyboards

![Peer Review of Storyboards textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/ail606-supplement/03-07-peer-review-of-storyboards.png)

Most peer feedback on storyboards is useless, and predictably so. Left unstructured, reviewers default to two failure modes: vague praise ("looks great, nice colors") that gives the author nothing to act on, and personal taste ("I would have used blue") that the shared design criteria do not support. Neither tells the author whether a frame teaches, and both quietly assert the reviewer's preferences as if they were standards. Effective peer review is a learnable discipline with a structure, not a personality trait of generous people.

This section treats peer review as criteria-anchored, actionable, and authorship-preserving. Anchored means every comment cites a shared principle (a Mayer principle, the frame's stated objective, a load concern) rather than the reviewer's preference. Actionable means the author is left with a concrete decision, not a compliment. Authorship-preserving means the reviewer surfaces the issue and may offer an option, but the revision choice, and the accountability for it, stays with the author. The design evidence produced is not the comment alone; it is the author's recorded accept-or-reject decision and the reason behind it.

## Learning Targets

- Diagnose why a storyboard peer comment fails by identifying which of the three properties (anchored, actionable, authorship-preserving) is missing.
- Rewrite a praise or taste comment into a three-part review (observation, anchor, option) that cites a Mayer principle or the frame objective.
- Explain why the author's documented accept-or-reject response, not the comment itself, is the design evidence, and why a principled rejection can be stronger than an acceptance.
- Maintain anchored, authorship-preserving review across a power gradient, as in the senior-to-junior transfer case.

## Opening Case

A classmate shares a storyboard whose Frame 4 packs three diagrams and a full paragraph onto one screen. The first reviewer writes "Looks great! I love the colors, though I'd have used a blue background." The author reads it, feels mildly complimented, and changes nothing meaningful, because there is nothing to change: the comment names no problem, cites no principle, and offers only a preference the assignment criteria do not support.

The real issue, that Frame 4 is overloaded and competes for the visual channel, went unnamed. The comment was friendly and worthless. This section asks you to expose what separates that comment from a review: a review names the principle at stake, points to the specific frame and objective, and hands the author a decision rather than a verdict on taste.

## Core Concept

A useful storyboard review comment has three properties, and dropping any one of them produces a recognizable failure.

- **Anchored.** It cites a shared standard, a Mayer principle, the frame's own stated objective, or a load concern, not the reviewer's preference. Anchoring is what gives the author grounds to weigh the comment instead of obeying or dismissing it. The opposite, taste ("use blue"), has no warrant.
- **Actionable.** It points to a specific frame and names a concrete change the author could make ("move the paragraph to narration; split the third diagram to its own frame"). The opposite, vague praise, gives nothing to do.
- **Authorship-preserving.** It surfaces the issue and may offer options, but leaves the revision decision to the author. The opposite, prescription ("change it to this"), seizes the author's design ownership and short-circuits their judgment.

The deeper move is recognizing what counts as the design evidence. It is not the reviewer's comment; it is the **author's documented response**, the accept-or-reject decision and its reason. A comment that is accepted without reasoning, or rejected with "I disagree," produces no evidence. A comment that the author rejects with a principled reason ("I kept both diagrams side by side because the objective is to compare them") produces strong evidence of judgment. These map to the course anchors directly: Mayer's coherence and segmenting principles and [cognitive load theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_load) supply the standards a comment anchors to, and UDL learner variability is often the basis for an option a reviewer offers ("this works for fluent readers; what about the rest?"). The anchor must change a decision; a comment that names a principle but would not alter the frame is decoration, not review.

## Worked Example: Rewriting a Peer Comment Into Real Review

A classmate shares a storyboard whose Frame 4 packs three diagrams and a paragraph onto one screen. Below is the before/after of the peer comment.

**Before (praise and taste, not review):**

> "Looks great! I love the colors. I would have used a blue background though."

Problems: general praise plus a personal-preference suggestion. Nothing names a principle, points to the real issue (Frame 4 is overloaded), or gives the author a decision to make.

**After (anchored, actionable, authorship preserved):**

> "Frame 4 may exceed the coherence and segmenting principles: three diagrams plus a full paragraph compete for the visual channel. The learning objective for this frame is to compare two diagrams, so the third diagram and the paragraph may be extraneous. Option: move the paragraph to narration and split the third diagram to its own frame. Your call on which fits your sequence."

Annotated decision trail:

1. Replaced praise with a specific observation tied to a named principle (coherence, segmenting) and the frame's objective.
2. Dropped the personal-taste color comment, which the shared criteria do not support.
3. Offered an option but left the revision choice to the author, so review surfaces the issue without seizing authorship.
4. Author's response recorded: accepted moving the paragraph to narration, rejected splitting the diagram (the comparison needs both side by side), and noted why. That accept/reject record is the design evidence.

The improvement is feedback that is actionable and criteria-anchored, and an authoring decision the author owns.

## Decision Rule

Write every review comment in a fixed three-part shape, observation, anchor, option, and refuse to send one that is missing a part:

1. **Observation:** State what you see on a specific frame, factually ("Frame 4 has three diagrams and a full paragraph on one screen").
2. **Anchor:** Tie it to a shared standard ("this may exceed the coherence and segmenting principles, and the frame's objective is only to compare two diagrams").
3. **Option, not order:** Offer a possible move and return the decision ("you could move the paragraph to narration; your call on what fits the sequence").

Strip anything that is pure taste ("I'd use blue") unless the criteria support it. Then, as the author receiving review, complete the loop: record each comment as accepted or rejected with a reason, because that response, not the comment, is the design evidence the assignment is really collecting.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is that good review means being either nice or harsh. Tone is not the variable; anchoring is. A warm comment with no anchor is as useless as a blunt one, and a blunt comment that cites the objective is valuable. The second misreading is that the author should act on every comment. Accepting all feedback is not responsiveness; it is abdication. A strong author rejects comments that conflict with the objective and records why, and that principled rejection is stronger evidence of design judgment than a quiet acceptance.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Walk the overloaded Frame 4 through the shape. Observation: "Frame 4 carries three diagrams and a paragraph competing for the visual channel." Anchor: "the objective is to compare two diagrams, so the third diagram and the paragraph may be extraneous (coherence, segmenting)." Option: "you could move the paragraph to narration and split the third diagram onto its own frame, but choose what fits your sequence." Now the author's response becomes the evidence: she accepts moving the paragraph to narration (it was duplicating the diagram's point) and rejects splitting the diagrams (the comparison needs both side by side, which is the objective), and records both reasons.

Notice the division of labor. The reviewer surfaced a real, anchored problem and offered a route; the author made and owned two opposite decisions, one accept and one reject, each justified by the objective. The walkthrough shows that a single well-formed comment can produce two pieces of design evidence, and that the author's reasoned rejection is the stronger of the two.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

Read peer comments by testing each for the three parts and by asking "what could the author actually do with this?" For each comment in the chapter's examples, identify the observation, the anchor, and whether it preserves authorship or seizes it. Then look past the comment to the author's response and judge the reasoning, not the choice; an accept and a reject can both be excellent if both cite the objective. A strong reader can spot the comment that is really a preference wearing the costume of feedback.

A strong reader of peer review can, for any exchange, name the standard the comment anchors to, the decision it leaves to the author, and the reason behind the author's accept-or-reject response.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The artifact reaches the stronger standard when it pairs anchored, option-style comments with the author's documented responses, including at least one comment the author accepted and one the author rejected, each with a reason tied to the frame's objective. The trace should show a comment being declined on principle, because a review record with only acceptances suggests the author was complying, not deciding. Pure-taste comments, if present, are marked as out of scope against the criteria.

A strong closing note reads: "On Frame 4 I received an anchored comment about coherence overload. I accepted moving the paragraph to narration because it duplicated the diagram's point, and I rejected splitting the two diagrams because the objective is to compare them side by side. I logged both decisions with reasons; the rejection is the part that shows I weighed the comment against the objective rather than just obeying it."

## Transfer Case

Move peer review from a graduate cohort of near-equals to a workplace where a senior designer reviews a junior's storyboard. The three-part shape still holds, but the power gradient changes the authorship dynamic: an "option" from a senior reads as an order unless the senior is explicit that the junior owns the call, and a junior's principled rejection now carries professional risk. The transfer test is whether you can keep review anchored and authorship-preserving when the reviewer outranks the author, which usually means stating the anchor more, not less, explicitly and inviting the reasoned rejection on the record. If your review skill only works among equals, it collapses into compliance the moment a hierarchy is introduced; the durable skill is keeping the comment about the objective rather than about who outranks whom.
