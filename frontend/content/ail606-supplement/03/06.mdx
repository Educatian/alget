# Prompt Logs as Design Evidence

![Prompt Logs as Design Evidence textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/ail606-supplement/03-06-prompt-logs-as-design-evidence.png)

When AI tools generate the assets, the visible artifact stops being good evidence of the designer's thinking, because anyone can produce a polished image or paragraph in one click. What distinguishes a designer who made principled choices from one who accepted the first output is no longer in the deliverable; it is in the record of how the deliverable was reached. The prompt log is where that record lives, and it is increasingly the part of a portfolio that demonstrates competence rather than mere access to a tool.

This section reframes the prompt log from a raw chat transcript into a curated argument for the final asset. A transcript is a passive byproduct; a design log is a deliberate document that shows intent, alternatives considered, suggestions rejected with reasons, and verification of the result. The shift matters because reviewers, instructors, and future-you cannot inspect taste, but they can inspect a log that says "I tried X, it failed because Y, so I changed to Z and confirmed it against the source."

## Opening Case

Two students submit the identical AI-generated rock-cycle diagram. The first attaches a one-line log: "Prompt: make a rock cycle diagram. Kept it." The second attaches a three-version log showing a baseline that lacked the transformation arrows, a rejection with the reason (the diagram missed the cyclic process, which is the learning point), a revised prompt that fixed it, and a final verification against the textbook. The deliverable is the same; the demonstrated competence is not.

The first log proves the student can operate the tool. The second proves the student exercised design judgment, knew what the image had to teach, recognized when an output failed it, and could say why the final version is right. This section asks you to make your log do the second job, because a clean transcript of the winning prompt hides exactly the reasoning a reviewer needs to see.

## Core Concept

A design log differs from a transcript on four dimensions, and each is a deliberate editorial act.

| Dimension | Raw transcript | Curated design log |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Intent | implicit; just the prompt text | each kept entry states why it was tried |
| Alternatives | deleted to look tidy | rejected attempts kept, with the reason |
| Outcome link | none; output floats free | each prompt tied to what changed and why |
| Verification | absent | final asset checked against a source |

The most counterintuitive rule is that **rejections are the most valuable entries**. A kept-and-explained failure ("v1 had no transformation arrows, which is the learning point, so I rejected it") shows the judgment that a clean log of only the winner erases. Pruning the failures to look efficient destroys the evidence.

Two cautions keep a log honest. **Length is not rigor**: a log of fifteen near-identical prompts demonstrates flailing, not judgment, so curate to the entries that carry a decision. And **verification is non-negotiable** when AI generated content, because the tool is fluent but not truthful, so the log must record where the final asset was checked against the authoritative source. These connect to the course anchors as the locus of intent: a log entry earns its place when its intent cites the learning function the asset must serve (a Mayer principle, a load concern, an access need), so the reader can see the prompt was driven by a learning goal, not by trial and error toward something that merely looked good.

## Worked Example: Turning a Chat History Into a Prompt Log

The task was to generate a diagram of the rock cycle. Below is the before/after of the prompt log itself.

**Before (raw transcript, only the winner kept):**

> Prompt: "make a rock cycle diagram." Output kept. Everything else deleted to keep it tidy.

Problems: this is a passive transcript trimmed to one line. It shows no intent, no rejected attempts, and no link between any prompt and what changed in the output.

**After (curated evidence with intent, a rejection, and outcomes):**

> v1 "make a rock cycle diagram." Intent: get a baseline. Outcome: looked nice but only three rock types and no arrows showing transformation. Rejected: missing the cyclic process, which is the learning point.
>
> v2 "labeled rock cycle: igneous, sedimentary, metamorphic, with arrows for melting, weathering, heat and pressure." Intent: foreground the transformations. Outcome: arrows appeared but weathering and erosion were merged. Kept with a note to verify against the source.
>
> v3 (final) added "show weathering and erosion as separate steps." Outcome: matched the textbook process. Verified labels against the source.

Annotated decision trail:

1. Replaced the transcript with curated entries: each kept prompt states intent and the change it produced.
2. Preserved a rejected attempt (v1) with the reason, since the rejection carries the design judgment.
3. Linked every revision to an outcome and to the learning goal (showing the cycle), so length is not mistaken for rigor.
4. Recorded verification against the content source at the final step.

The improvement is a log that argues for the final prompt, not a tidy list that hides how the decision was made.

## Decision Rule

Curate the log as an argument for the final asset. For each entry you keep, require three fields, intent, outcome, and disposition, and prune anything that carries none of them:

1. **Intent:** Why did you try this prompt? Name the learning function it was meant to serve.
2. **Outcome:** What did the output actually do, and where did it fall short?
3. **Disposition:** Kept, rejected, or revised, and the reason. Keep at least one informative rejection.

Then add the two integrity checks: a **verification entry** at the final step ("checked the labels against the source, corrected one") and a **curation pass** that removes redundant near-duplicate prompts so length signals decisions, not flailing. A log passes when a reader who never saw your screen can reconstruct why the final asset is the right one.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading is "a complete log means keeping everything." Completeness is the enemy here; an unedited transcript buries the three or four decisions that matter under dozens of trivial retries, and the reader cannot find the judgment. The second misreading is treating the log as a record of effort rather than of reasoning. Twenty prompts prove you spent time; they do not prove you made a defensible choice. The valuable log is short, edited, and built around rejections and verification, not a timestamped dump of activity.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Walk a real curation. The raw history for a rock-cycle diagram has eight prompts, mostly cosmetic retries about colors and fonts. Curate. Entry 1 (kept as a rejection): "make a rock cycle diagram. Intent: baseline. Outcome: three rock types but no arrows, so it shows types, not the cyclic process, which is the learning point. Rejected." Entry 2 (kept, revised): "labeled rock cycle with arrows for melting, weathering, heat and pressure. Intent: foreground the transformations. Outcome: arrows appeared but weathering and erosion were merged. Kept with a note to verify." Entry 3 (final): "show weathering and erosion as separate steps. Outcome: matched the textbook process. Verified labels against the source." The five color-and-font retries are dropped, with a one-line note that they were cosmetic.

The curated log is three entries and argues for the final diagram: it names what the asset had to teach (the cycle), preserves the rejection that carries the judgment, links each prompt to a change, and records verification. The walkthrough shows curation is subtraction plus annotation, not transcription.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

Read any prompt log by hunting for its rejections and its verification step first, because that is where the evidence lives. For each kept entry, check that it states intent, outcome, and disposition; flag entries that are just prompt text with no reasoning. A strong reader can tell a curated argument from a tidied transcript at a glance: the argument explains a failure, the transcript only shows successes. Notice when a long log is actually flailing and when a short log is actually decisive.

A strong reader of design logs can, for any log, point to the most informative rejection, the link between a prompt and the change it produced, and the place where the final asset was verified.

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The artifact reaches the stronger standard when the log reads as a curated case for the final asset: every kept entry carries intent, outcome, and disposition; at least one rejection is preserved with its reason tied to the learning function; redundant retries are pruned with a note; and the final step records verification against a source. A reviewer should be able to reconstruct the design decision without the original chat window.

A strong closing note reads: "I cut five cosmetic retries and kept three entries that carry decisions. I preserved the v1 rejection because its failure, no transformation arrows, is exactly the learning point, which is the judgment a clean log would hide. Each kept prompt is tied to the change it produced, and I verified the final labels against the textbook and corrected one merged step."

## Transfer Case

Move the logging discipline from solo asset generation to a team workflow where several designers prompt the same tool against a shared storyboard. The curation rule survives, but ownership and provenance now matter: the log must attribute who tried what, so a rejection's reasoning can be traced to the person who made the call, and a shared verification step prevents two designers from each assuming the other checked the facts. The transfer test is whether you can keep the intent-outcome-disposition structure while adding attribution and a single accountable verification, rather than letting the team log decay into an anonymous merged transcript. If your logging habit only works when one person remembers their own reasoning, it does not scale to the collaborative case where reasoning must be legible to others.
