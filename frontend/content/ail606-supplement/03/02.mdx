## Core Concept

The core idea in this section is that **Signaling and Segmenting in Practice** should be treated as a relationship among purpose, constraint, evidence, and revision. Purpose names what the learner or audience needs to accomplish. Constraint names what limits the decision. Evidence names the source that makes the decision defensible. Revision shows what changed after the evidence was considered.

Three course anchors shape the reasoning here:

- **Mayer multimedia learning** helps you decide what counts as a meaningful learning or performance demand.
- **cognitive load theory** helps you notice when a design or technology choice creates risk, burden, or unsupported assumptions.
- **UDL learner variability** helps you check whether the artifact works for varied learners, audiences, or use contexts.

Read the anchors as competing checks on quality. One may highlight the learning demand, another may expose a risk, and another may help you test transfer.

## Worked Example: Fixing Over-Cuing and Adding Real Segmenting

Here is a single frame from a four-step lab-safety procedure, shown before and after a signaling-and-segmenting revision.

**Before (over-cued, auto-advancing):**

> Frame 2. All four step headings are bold red, each diagram sits in a yellow box, two arrows pulse, and the whole sequence auto-plays end to end with no learner control. Caption: made it pop so students notice the steps.

Two problems. First, contrast has collapsed: when every step is cued, none reads as the one to attend to now. Second, there is no segmenting; the learner cannot pace the four steps.

**After (one cue, learner-paced segments):**

> Frame 2. Only the step currently in focus is bold (one cue, by contrast). The other three headings are plain gray until reached. A Continue control ends each step, so the learner advances one meaningful unit at a time. Caption: signal the active step; let the learner set the pace.

Annotated decision trail:

1. Signaling fix: reduced from four simultaneous cues to one active-step cue. Warrant: signaling works by contrast, so a single salient element directs limited attention.
2. Load check: removed the pulsing arrows. The arrow motion competed with reading the step it was meant to mark, spending load without adding guidance.
3. Segmenting fix: replaced auto-play with a Continue control at each conceptual seam (between steps), not mid-step.
4. Rejected suggestion: an AI tool proposed color-coding all four steps for consistency. Rejected, because consistent color across all steps reintroduces the over-cuing the revision just removed.

The improvement is not more polish. It is less cuing used more precisely, plus learner control placed at the conceptual seams.

## Decision Rule

Use the learning goal first, the media choice second, and the software feature last. A feature is justified only when it reduces unnecessary processing, strengthens useful processing, or makes learner action easier to observe.

A strong submission should survive three quick tests:

1. **Purpose check:** What should the audience be able to do after reading or using the artifact?
2. **Evidence check:** Which exact source, observation, annotation, rubric line, data pattern, or policy boundary justifies the revision?
3. **Revision check:** What changed in the artifact, and what suggestion did you reject or modify?

If you cannot answer all three questions, the artifact may be complete, but it is not yet ready as evidence of learning.
