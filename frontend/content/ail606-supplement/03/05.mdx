## Core Concept

The core idea in this section is that **Alt Text and Accessibility** should be treated as a relationship among purpose, constraint, evidence, and revision. Purpose names what the learner or audience needs to accomplish. Constraint names what limits the decision. Evidence names the source that makes the decision defensible. Revision shows what changed after the evidence was considered.

Three course anchors shape the reasoning here:

- **Mayer multimedia learning** helps you decide what counts as a meaningful learning or performance demand.
- **cognitive load theory** helps you notice when a design or technology choice creates risk, burden, or unsupported assumptions.
- **UDL learner variability** helps you check whether the artifact works for varied learners, audiences, or use contexts.

Read the anchors as competing checks on quality. One may highlight the learning demand, another may expose a risk, and another may help you test transfer.

## Worked Example: Rewriting Alt Text for a Bar Chart

A frame presents a bar chart comparing test scores across three teaching methods. Below is the before/after of its accessibility treatment.

**Before (appearance, not function):**

> alt = "A colorful bar chart with blue, green, and orange bars on a white background." The series are distinguished by color only. No transcript.

Problems: the alt text describes pixels, not the comparison the chart teaches. A screen-reader user learns nothing about the result, and a color-blind learner cannot tell the series apart.

**After (function carried in words, color not load-bearing):**

> alt = "Bar chart: average score was highest for inquiry (82), then lecture (74), then lab (70); inquiry led by 8 points." Each bar is also labeled with its method name and value, so color is decorative, not the only cue. A short data table sits beside the chart.

Annotated decision trail:

1. Rewrote alt to convey the instructional function (the ranking and the 8-point gap), not the colors.
2. Removed color-only coding: added on-bar labels so the distinction survives color blindness and grayscale.
3. Classified the image as meaningful, so it earns content-bearing alt, unlike the page's decorative header rule, which takes empty alt.
4. Handled complexity: kept the alt brief and added a nearby data table for learners who need every value.
5. Rejected suggestion: the AI proposed a 200-word alt attribute listing all values. Rejected, because alt should stay brief; the full data belongs in the adjacent table.

The improvement is access carried by structure, brief functional alt plus a readable equivalent, not a longer color description.

## Decision Rule

Use the learning goal first, the media choice second, and the software feature last. A feature is justified only when it reduces unnecessary processing, strengthens useful processing, or makes learner action easier to observe.

A strong submission should survive three quick tests:

1. **Purpose check:** What should the audience be able to do after reading or using the artifact?
2. **Evidence check:** Which exact source, observation, annotation, rubric line, data pattern, or policy boundary justifies the revision?
3. **Revision check:** What changed in the artifact, and what suggestion did you reject or modify?

If you cannot answer all three questions, the artifact may be complete, but it is not yet ready as evidence of learning.

Next, connect the artifact to the course anchors: **Mayer multimedia learning**, **cognitive load theory**, **UDL learner variability**. The point is not to cite every anchor. The point is to let one anchor change the artifact. If the anchor does not change wording, layout, evidence, timing, or feedback, it has not yet become design reasoning.

Finally, inspect the revision trail. A weak submission says the artifact was improved. A strong submission shows the earlier version, the issue noticed, the support requested, the decision accepted or rejected, and the reason the final version is better for the intended learner or audience.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: **claim** on the left, **evidence** on the right. The claim is the decision you think the section is teaching. The evidence is the exact phrase, example, annotation, support move, or artifact revision that makes the claim inspectable. By the end of AIL 606 03.05, your notes should make it possible to reconstruct your reasoning without asking you to explain it again.

A strong reader of Alt Text and Accessibility can answer four questions:

1. What is the real decision hidden inside this topic?
2. Which constraint makes the decision difficult?
3. What evidence would persuade a skeptical reviewer?
4. What would you revise if the audience, learner, classroom, or tool changed?
