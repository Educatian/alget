## Why This Section Matters

By the end of Module 4 you have a problem statement, a traceability matrix, annotated wireframes, a load diagnosis, an accessibility pass, an authoring record, and a disclosure. The clinic is where these stop being seven separate deliverables and become one coherent revision plan. The hard skill here is not producing more fixes; it is prioritizing among the fixes you already have evidence for, ranking them by their impact on the one outcome the problem statement named. A revision plan that lists "change the colors, add animations, bigger font" has lost the thread; a plan that ranks "segment screen 3" above "fix body contrast" because the load diagnosis and pilot data say so has found it.

This section teaches you to synthesize the module's artifacts into a prioritized, evidence-ranked revision plan. You should leave able to filter generic AI critique down to the one point you can substantiate, and able to defend why a high-impact revision outranks a quick cosmetic one.

## Learning Targets

- Synthesize the module's artifacts (problem statement, load diagnosis, accessibility pass, traceability) into one plan.
- Rank revisions by evidenced impact on the problem statement's named outcome.
- Filter generic AI critique down to claims you can substantiate against your own evidence.
- Defend a prioritization, not just a list of changes.

## Opening Case

A student arrives at the clinic with a revision list: change the color scheme, add more animations, make the font bigger, and "implement all ten suggestions the AI gave me." Every item is either a preference or an unfiltered verdict, and none connects to the module's problem statement (novices reach first correct action in under 90 seconds). The facilitator's first question is not "which color?" but "which of these moves the 90-second outcome, and how do you know?" The list collapses, because it was never anchored to evidence or to the goal the whole module was built to serve.

The case shows that a revision plan is only as good as its anchor. Untethered from the problem statement and the module's own evidence, even a long list of changes is noise.

## Core Concept

Synthesis means reading the module's artifacts as a connected argument rather than a stack. The problem statement supplies the target outcome; the load diagnosis, accessibility pass, and pilot data supply the evidence; the traceability matrix supplies the principle behind each candidate change. A clinic-ready plan ranks revisions by evidenced impact on the target outcome, which requires two judgments per item: how much it moves the named outcome, and how strong the evidence for that impact is. A high-impact change backed by load-diagnosis and pilot data (segment the standard-deviation intro) outranks a low-impact change even when the low-impact one is easier (though must-do, low-effort accessibility fixes still get done because their cost is trivial).

Filtering AI critique is the second discipline. A generative critique produces ten plausible suggestions, but plausible is not the same as grounded in your learners. The move is to treat each suggestion as a hypothesis to verify against your own evidence, keeping only the ones your artifacts substantiate and rejecting the rest with a reason. This is the same accept-or-reject-with-reason logic from the authoring section, now applied to critique rather than code.

| Revision candidate | Impact on the 90-second outcome | Evidence backing it | Priority |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Segment screen 3 intro | High (the main stall) | Load diagnosis + 2-min pilot stall | 1 |
| Per-item quiz feedback | Medium | Testers could not tell which item they missed | 2 |
| Body-text contrast fix | Low impact, but must-do | Computed 2.8:1, fails WCAG AA | 3 (low effort) |
| Nine AI cosmetic suggestions | Unverified | Not grounded in these learners | Rejected |

The reason prioritization is the real work is that revision time is finite. A plan that does not rank cannot tell you where to spend it, so it defaults to spending it on whatever is easiest or loudest (the AI's ten suggestions), which is rarely where the outcome lives.

Order the four candidates the way the clinic plan should read: highest evidenced impact on the 90-second outcome first, with low-effort must-dos kept in and unverified critique dropped last.

## Worked Example

Work through one annotated before/after revision of a Module 4 design-clinic revision plan, the document you bring to the clinic table.

**Before (revision plan a student brings to clinic):**

> Things to fix:
> - Change the color scheme
> - Add more animations
> - Make the font bigger
> - Implement all ten suggestions the AI gave me

**Annotations a clinic facilitator would leave:**

1. Every item is a surface or preference change; none ties back to the Module 4 problem statement or a learner outcome.
2. "Implement all ten AI suggestions" treats a generic critique as a verdict rather than evidence to verify.
3. The list has no priority, so it cannot guide where to spend limited revision time.

**After (revised, prioritized plan):**

> Problem statement target: novices reach first correct action in under 90 seconds.
> Prioritized revisions, ranked by evidenced impact on that target:
> 1. Screen 3 (highest impact): segment the standard-deviation intro into 3 steps. Evidence: load diagnosis flagged high intrinsic load; pilot testers stalled here for 2+ minutes.
> 2. Quiz feedback (medium): move to per-item feedback. Evidence: testers could not tell which item they missed.
> 3. Contrast fix on body text (must-do, low effort): #999 fails WCAG AA at 2.8:1.
> AI critique: of ten suggestions, I kept only "shorten the intro narration," which I verified against the load diagnosis; I rejected the rest as not grounded in my learners.

**Why the after version is stronger:** each revision is tied to the original problem statement, ranked by evidenced impact on the named learner outcome, and integrates the module's own artifacts (load diagnosis, accessibility pass, pilot evidence). The AI critique is filtered to the one point the author could substantiate. The plan now reasons across the module rather than listing unrelated fixes.

## Decision Rule

Anchor every revision to the outcome and rank by evidenced impact. Before the clinic, run the plan through three tests:

1. **Anchor check:** Does each revision tie back to the problem statement's named outcome?
2. **Ranking check:** Are revisions ordered by evidenced impact on that outcome, with the evidence cited?
3. **Filter check:** Has each AI suggestion been kept only if your own evidence substantiates it, and rejected with a reason otherwise?

If a revision cannot name the outcome it serves and the evidence behind it, it does not belong on the prioritized list.

## Common Misreadings

The first error is volume mistaken for rigor: a long list of changes feels thorough but, unranked and unanchored, cannot guide a single decision. The second is treating AI critique as a verdict to implement rather than hypotheses to verify, which lets the tool set your priorities. The third is prioritizing by effort or preference (the easy cosmetic fix) instead of by impact on the outcome the module exists to improve.

A clinic plan is a prioritized argument anchored to the outcome, not an exhaustive to-do list.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Start from the problem statement and write its target outcome at the top of the plan. Everything below answers to that line; a revision that does not serve it is either a trivial must-do or a distraction. This single anchor is what converts a pile of fixes into a plan.

Then pull evidence from each module artifact and attach it to candidate revisions. The load diagnosis nominates the high-intrinsic-load screen, the pilot data nominates the stall, the accessibility pass nominates the contrast fix, the traceability matrix names the principle. Each candidate now carries its own evidence, which makes ranking possible.

Finally, rank by evidenced impact and filter the AI critique through the same evidence. Order the candidates by how much they move the outcome and how strong the evidence is, keeping low-effort must-dos in regardless. Then take the ten AI suggestions and discard any you cannot tie to your learners, keeping only the verified one. The plan you carry to the clinic is now a defensible prioritization, not a wish list.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: **claim** on the left, **evidence** on the right. The claim is a revision's priority; the evidence is the artifact (load diagnosis, pilot stall, contrast measurement) that justifies that ranking. By the end of AIL 606 04.08, your notes should let a facilitator see why each revision sits where it does in the order.

A strong reader of a clinic plan can answer four questions:

1. What outcome does the whole plan serve, and is every item anchored to it?
2. Why is the top-ranked revision ranked first, in evidence terms?
3. Which AI suggestions were rejected, and why?
4. How would the ranking change if the pilot data were different?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The artifact is strong when the plan is anchored to the problem statement, ranked by evidenced impact, and shows the AI critique filtered to substantiated claims. Treat a long unranked list as the weakest plan. Where you cannot tell which of two revisions matters more, mark it with the annotation tools and ask BigAL to compare an effort-ranked plan with an impact-ranked plan rather than for a generic checklist.

A useful final paragraph for the artifact would sound like this: "I ranked the segmenting of screen 3 first because the load diagnosis and the two-minute pilot stall both point to it as the main barrier to the 90-second outcome. I rejected nine of the ten AI suggestions because none was grounded in my learners' actual stall."

## Transfer Case

Move the clinic from your own draft to peer review, where you are now prioritizing revisions for a classmate's prototype whose problem statement and evidence are not yours. The synthesis discipline holds, but you must read their artifacts to find their anchor before you can rank anything, and you must resist imposing your priorities or the AI's. Transfer means applying the same anchor-then-rank-by-evidence method to an unfamiliar artifact, not reusing your own ranking. If you understand synthesis, reviewing someone else's draft forces you to locate their outcome and their evidence before proposing a single change.
