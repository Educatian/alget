## Core Concept

The core idea in this section is that **Module 4 Design Draft Clinic** should be treated as a relationship among purpose, constraint, evidence, and revision. Purpose names what the learner or audience needs to accomplish. Constraint names what limits the decision. Evidence names the source that makes the decision defensible. Revision shows what changed after the evidence was considered.

Three course anchors shape the reasoning here:

- **Mayer multimedia learning** helps you decide what counts as a meaningful learning or performance demand.
- **cognitive load theory** helps you notice when a design or technology choice creates risk, burden, or unsupported assumptions.
- **UDL learner variability** helps you check whether the artifact works for varied learners, audiences, or use contexts.

Use these anchors as lenses, not as names to drop into a reflection. A useful lens changes what you notice, what you question, and what you revise.

## Worked Example

Work through one annotated before/after revision of a Module 4 design-clinic revision plan, the document you bring to the clinic table.

**Before (revision plan a student brings to clinic):**

> Things to fix:
> - Change the color scheme
> - Add more animations
> - Make the font bigger
> - Implement all ten suggestions the AI gave me

**Annotations a clinic facilitator would leave:**

1. Every item is a surface or preference change; none ties back to the Module 4 problem statement or a learner outcome.
2. "Implement all ten AI suggestions" treats a generic critique as a verdict rather than evidence to verify.
3. The list has no priority, so it cannot guide where to spend limited revision time.

**After (revised, prioritized plan):**

> Problem statement target: novices reach first correct action in under 90 seconds.
> Prioritized revisions, ranked by evidenced impact on that target:
> 1. Screen 3 (highest impact): segment the standard-deviation intro into 3 steps. Evidence: load diagnosis flagged high intrinsic load; pilot testers stalled here for 2+ minutes.
> 2. Quiz feedback (medium): move to per-item feedback. Evidence: testers could not tell which item they missed.
> 3. Contrast fix on body text (must-do, low effort): #999 fails WCAG AA at 2.8:1.
> AI critique: of ten suggestions, I kept only "shorten the intro narration," which I verified against the load diagnosis; I rejected the rest as not grounded in my learners.

**Why the after version is stronger:** each revision is tied to the original problem statement, ranked by evidenced impact on the named learner outcome, and integrates the module's own artifacts (load diagnosis, accessibility pass, pilot evidence). The AI critique is filtered to the one point the author could substantiate. The plan now reasons across the module rather than listing unrelated fixes.

## Decision Rule

Use the learning goal first, the media choice second, and the software feature last. A feature is justified only when it reduces unnecessary processing, strengthens useful processing, or makes learner action easier to observe.

Before submitting the storyboard frame sequence with narration, signaling, and reader-action notes, slow down and check whether the trace can answer three questions:

1. **Purpose check:** What should the audience be able to do after reading or using the artifact?
2. **Evidence check:** Which exact source, observation, annotation, rubric line, data pattern, or policy boundary justifies the revision?
3. **Revision check:** What changed in the artifact, and what suggestion did you reject or modify?

If you cannot answer all three questions, the artifact may be complete, but it is not yet ready as evidence of learning.

## Common Misreadings

A common mistake is to treat multimedia quality as visual polish. A cleaner screen can still increase extraneous load if the learner action is unclear.

The trace should show what cognitive demand was noticed, what support was requested, and which change improved the learner's path.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Read this section as a small professional decision cycle, not as a list of terms. First, locate the work product: **storyboard frame sequence with narration, signaling, and reader-action notes**. Then ask what problem the artifact is supposed to solve inside prototype authoring. A strong reader can explain the artifact in plain language before naming any theory: who will use it, what decision it supports, and what would count as evidence that it improved.

Next, connect the artifact to the course anchors: **Mayer multimedia learning**, **cognitive load theory**, **UDL learner variability**. The point is not to cite every anchor. The point is to let one anchor change the artifact. If the anchor does not change wording, layout, evidence, timing, or feedback, it has not yet become design reasoning.

Finally, inspect the revision trail. A weak submission says the artifact was improved. A strong submission shows the earlier version, the issue noticed, the support requested, the decision accepted or rejected, and the reason the final version is better for the intended learner or audience.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: **claim** on the left, **evidence** on the right. The claim is the decision you think the section is teaching. The evidence is the exact phrase, example, annotation, support move, or artifact revision that makes the claim inspectable. By the end of AIL 606 04.08, your notes should make it possible to reconstruct your reasoning without asking you to explain it again.

A strong reader of Module 4 Design Draft Clinic can answer four questions:

1. What is the real decision hidden inside this topic?
2. Which constraint makes the decision difficult?
3. What evidence would persuade a skeptical reviewer?
4. What would you revise if the audience, learner, classroom, or tool changed?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

For this section, the artifact is strong when a reviewer can connect the design choice to theory, learner need, and visible evidence in the prototype. Treat visual polish as the last layer. The earlier layers are purpose, constraint, evidence, and revision.
