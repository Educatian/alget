# Principle-to-Feature Traceability

![Principle-to-Feature Traceability textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/ail606-supplement/04-02-principle-to-feature-traceability.png)

## Why This Section Matters

Designers love to cite principles, but a cited principle and an applied principle look completely different in a prototype. Principle-to-feature traceability is the practice of proving that a named learning principle actually produced a specific feature, and that the feature reduces or strengthens a specific processing demand. Without traceability, a storyboard becomes a pile of nice features each justified after the fact by whichever principle sounds plausible. AIL 606 asks you to build a matrix that survives the reverse question: pick any feature, and you can name the one principle that controls it and the demand it addresses.

This section teaches you to write traceability rows that resist post-hoc justification. You should leave able to spot the tell of a cosmetic change wearing four principles, and able to rewrite a row so it names one controlling principle, the processing demand it targets, and the feature mechanism.

## Learning Targets

- Map one controlling principle to one feature, with the processing demand named explicitly.
- Detect post-hoc justification (many principles attached to one cosmetic change).
- Describe features as mechanisms that act on a demand, not as decoration.
- Defend any feature by tracing it forward from principle to demand to implementation.

## Opening Case

In a design crit, a peer presents a matrix where one row reads "Signaling, Coherence, Modality, Segmenting" all pointing at "added a colored highlight and cleaned up the slide." The crit goes quiet, because everyone recognizes the pattern: the highlight was added first because it looked good, and four principles were stapled on afterward to make it sound rigorous. The fix is not more principles; it is one principle, honestly chosen, with the demand it reduces stated out loud.

The case shows why traceability runs in both directions. A forward trace (principle to feature) is easy to fake; the reverse trace (here is the feature, name its one controlling principle and the demand) is the one that exposes whether design reasoning actually happened.

## Core Concept

Traceability is the documented relationship between an instructional principle, the cognitive demand it acts on, and the feature that implements it. The core move is the one-to-one discipline: each feature should be controlled by a single principle. Mayer's principles are not interchangeable adjectives; each names a distinct processing problem. Signaling solves "the learner cannot tell which element matters." Coherence solves "interesting but irrelevant material is stealing capacity." Modality solves "the visual channel is overloaded because text and graphics compete." Redundancy solves "identical words are presented in two channels at once." When you attach four of these to one change, at least three are decorative.

A feature must be written as a mechanism, not an appearance. "Made it cleaner" is appearance; "a yellow highlight appears on exactly the term the narration is speaking, synced to the audio, and clears when narration moves on" is a mechanism, because it states what the feature does to the learner's attention. Only a mechanism can be tested against the demand it claims to reduce.

| Principle | The processing problem it solves | A mechanism that solves it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Signaling | Learner cannot locate the relevant element | Cue (highlight, arrow, bold) on exactly the active element |
| Coherence | Extraneous material consumes capacity | Remove decorative images and background music |
| Modality | Visual channel overloaded by competing text and graphic | Move on-screen text to narration |
| Segmenting | Continuous presentation outpaces processing | Learner-paced chunks with continue control |

The reason one-to-one matters is auditability. A reviewer who can isolate the controlling principle can run a clean test: does this mechanism reduce that demand? With four principles fused, no clean test exists, so the claim is unfalsifiable and therefore worthless as evidence of judgment.

## Worked Example

Work through one annotated before/after revision of a single traceability row, the kind you would defend in a design crit.

**Before (one row of the traceability matrix):**

| Principle | Feature in the storyboard |
| --- | --- |
| Signaling, Coherence, Modality, Segmenting | Added a colored highlight to the key term and made the slide cleaner. |

**Annotations a reviewer would leave:**

1. Four principles point at one cosmetic change. That density is a tell that the highlight was chosen first and the principles were attached afterward.
2. "Made the slide cleaner" describes appearance, not a change to what the learner processes.
3. No row says what demand the feature reduces, so the mapping cannot be tested.

**After (revised row):**

| Principle (controlling) | Demand it addresses | Feature mechanism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Signaling | Novices scan the whole frame and cannot tell which term the narration is on. | A yellow highlight appears on exactly the term the narration is speaking, synced to the audio, and clears when the narration moves on. |

**Why the after version is stronger:** it names one controlling principle, states the processing demand the feature reduces, and describes the feature as a mechanism (a synced highlight tied to the narration) rather than as decoration. The trace now runs principle and demand to feature, so a reviewer can test whether the highlight actually directs attention. The change is a better argument, not a longer table.

## Decision Rule

Build the matrix so it survives the reverse trace. Before the storyboard ships, audit every row with three tests:

1. **One-principle check:** Can you cut the row to a single controlling principle? If three drop away with no loss, they were decorative.
2. **Mechanism check:** Does the feature column say what the feature does to attention or processing, not how it looks?
3. **Demand check:** Does the row name the specific demand the feature reduces or strengthens, so the claim can be tested?

If a row fails any test, it is a justification, not a trace.

## Common Misreadings

The frequent error is principle inflation: attaching every plausible principle to a feature so the matrix looks thorough. Thoroughness here is a liability, because it signals that the principles were chosen to flatter the feature rather than to control it. A related error is describing features by appearance ("cleaner," "more modern"), which can never be traced to a demand because appearance is not a cognitive event.

A matrix is strong when each row is sparse and falsifiable, not when each row is crowded with citations.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Start from a finished feature and try to delete principles. Take the row, and one at a time, remove each principle and ask whether the feature still makes sense without it. The principle you cannot remove is the controlling one; the ones that fall away without consequence were decorative. This subtractive method is faster and more honest than trying to justify additions.

Next, rewrite the feature cell as a mechanism. Replace any appearance word with a verb describing what happens to the learner: directs attention to, removes from the channel, paces, separates. If you cannot write the verb, you do not yet understand what the feature does, which is the real finding.

Finally, name the demand in the learner's terms, not the designer's. "Reduces clutter" is the designer's view; "novices cannot tell which term the narration is on" is the learner's processing problem. The demand cell is where the row becomes testable, because a demand stated from the learner's side can be checked in user testing.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: **claim** on the left, **evidence** on the right. The claim is the single controlling principle you believe governs a feature; the evidence is the mechanism and the demand that make the mapping testable. By the end of AIL 606 04.02, your notes should let a reviewer audit any row by running the reverse trace.

A strong reader of a traceability matrix can answer four questions:

1. For this feature, which one principle survives the subtraction test?
2. Is the feature written as a mechanism or as an appearance?
3. What demand, stated from the learner's side, does the row claim to reduce?
4. How would you test in user data whether the mechanism worked?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The artifact is strong when every row is sparse (one controlling principle), mechanistic (a verb acting on attention), and falsifiable (a named demand a reviewer could test). Treat a crowded, citation-heavy matrix as a warning sign, not a strength. Where a row still fuses principles, mark it with the annotation tools and ask BigAL to compare a one-principle row against a four-principle row rather than for a generic definition of signaling.

A useful final paragraph for the artifact would sound like this: "I rewrote the signaling row to name one principle, the demand it reduces, and the synced-highlight mechanism. I dropped Coherence, Modality, and Segmenting from that row because removing them changed nothing about why the highlight exists."

## Transfer Case

Move the same matrix from a narrated slide deck to a text-only print job aid where there is no audio channel. The signaling row may survive (a bold cue still directs attention), but the Modality and Redundancy rows lose their basis, because there is no second channel to balance or duplicate. Transfer means re-deriving which principles even apply in the new channel mix, not copying the rows. If you understand traceability, removing the audio channel visibly prunes the matrix.
