# Accessibility and WCAG Checks

![Accessibility and WCAG Checks textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/ail606-supplement/04-05-accessibility-and-wcag-checks.png)

## Why This Section Matters

Accessibility is the place where "looks fine to me" is most often wrong, because the designer is rarely the person the design fails. A learner using a screen reader, a learner with low vision, and a learner who cannot distinguish red from green each experience a screen the sighted designer never sees. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines exist precisely to replace personal taste with verifiable criteria: a contrast ratio is a number you can compute, not an opinion. AIL 606 treats accessibility as a checkable revision, where every fix names the WCAG criterion it satisfies and can be confirmed by anyone, not asserted by the author.

This section teaches you to audit a draft screen against specific WCAG 2.2 AA success criteria (WCAG 2.2 is the current W3C Recommendation as of 2026; WCAG 3.0 remains a Working Draft, so audits should target 2.2 AA today) and to write fixes that are verifiable rather than tasteful. You should leave able to catch the three most common failures (insufficient contrast, useless alt text, and meaning carried by color alone) and to state which criterion each fix meets.

## Learning Targets

- Audit a screen against specific WCAG 2.2 AA success criteria, not general "looks accessible" impressions.
- Compute and check color contrast against the 4.5:1 threshold for normal text.
- Write alt text that conveys the information an image carries, not the word "image."
- Ensure no status or meaning is communicated by color alone.

## Opening Case

A quiz-feedback screen looks clean and professional. The instructions are in light gray, a workflow diagram sits at the top, and correct and incorrect answers are marked with a green check and a red cross. Three different learners hit three different walls: the low-vision learner cannot read gray text at roughly 2.8:1 contrast, the screen-reader user hears only "image" where the workflow should be described, and the color-blind learner sees two identical-looking marks because the only difference is hue. None of these learners is unusual, and none of them is served by a screen that "looks fine."

The case shows why accessibility cannot be self-certified by the designer. Each failure is invisible to the person who built it and obvious the moment you apply the right criterion.

## Core Concept

WCAG is organized under four principles (perceivable, operable, understandable, robust) and expressed as testable success criteria at levels A, AA, and AAA. AA is the standard most institutions and Section 508 contexts require. The value of criteria over impressions is that each one is checkable by a third party. Three criteria account for the majority of instructional-media failures.

Contrast (criterion 1.4.3) requires a luminance contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for normal text (3:1 for large text). The ratio is computed from the relative luminance of the two colors; gray text at #999 on white computes to about 2.8:1 and fails, while #333 on white clears the threshold. This is arithmetic, not preference. Text alternatives (criterion 1.1.1) require that non-text content carry an equivalent text alternative; alt="image" satisfies the attribute but conveys nothing, so it fails the intent. Alt text must carry the information the image carries, which means a workflow diagram needs its steps and flow described, not its existence announced. Use of color (criterion 1.4.1) requires that color never be the only means of conveying information; a green check and red cross that differ only in hue fail, and pairing each with a word (Correct, Incorrect) or a distinct shape passes.

| WCAG criterion | Failure to catch | Verifiable fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum) | Body text below 4.5:1 | Darken text until the computed ratio clears 4.5:1 |
| 1.1.1 Non-text Content | alt="image" or empty alt on informative graphics | Describe what the image communicates |
| 1.4.1 Use of Color | Status shown by color only | Add a word or shape alongside the color |

The reason these are revision checks and not aesthetic preferences is that each one yields a pass or fail that a reviewer can reproduce. "I made it more accessible" is an assertion; "body text now computes at 4.6:1, clearing 1.4.3" is evidence.

Contrast is arithmetic, not taste. Given the relative luminance of the text (\$L_t\$) and the background (\$L_b\$), the ratio is \$(L_{light} + 0.05) / (L_{dark} + 0.05)\$. With white text on a white-ish page the denominator stays near the background, so the ratio is \$(L_b + 0.05)/(L_t + 0.05)\$ for dark text on a light field. Drive the text luminance down and watch the ratio cross the 4.5:1 AA threshold (gray #999 sits near \$L_t = 0.32\$; #333 near \$L_t = 0.03\$).

## Worked Example

Work through one annotated before/after accessibility revision of a single quiz-feedback screen against WCAG 2.2 AA (the three criteria used here, 1.4.3, 1.1.1, and 1.4.1, are unchanged from WCAG 2.1).

**Before (feedback screen markup):**

```html
<p style="color:#999">Read each item and choose the best answer.</p>
<img src="workflow.png" alt="image">
<span style="color:green">&#10003;</span>  <!-- correct -->
<span style="color:red">&#10007;</span>    <!-- incorrect -->
```

**Annotations a reviewer would leave:**

1. Body text at #999 on white is roughly 2.8:1 contrast, below the 4.5:1 AA threshold.
2. The diagram's alt text says "image," which conveys none of the workflow it depicts.
3. Correct and incorrect differ only by color (green vs red), so a color-blind learner cannot tell the status apart.

**After (revised markup):**

```html
<p style="color:#333">Read each item and choose the best answer.</p>
<img src="workflow.png"
     alt="Three-step intake workflow: request, review, then approve, with review looping back on rejection">
<span style="color:#1a7f37">&#10003; Correct</span>
<span style="color:#b42318">&#10007; Incorrect</span>
```

**Why the after version is stronger:** the body color #333 on white clears 4.5:1, the alt text now carries the workflow's actual information for screen-reader users, and each status pairs its color with a word so meaning no longer depends on color discrimination. Each fix is checked against a specific WCAG criterion, so the revision is verifiable, not a matter of taste.

Match each common failure to the named WCAG criterion it violates and the verifiable fix that resolves it. Naming the criterion is what turns "looks fine" into reproducible evidence.

## Decision Rule

Replace "looks accessible" with a criterion and a result. For every accessibility revision:

1. **Criterion check:** Which named WCAG success criterion does this fix address?
2. **Measurement check:** Can a reviewer reproduce the result (compute the contrast, read the alt text, confirm a non-color cue)?
3. **Coverage check:** Did you test for the failures invisible to you, not just the ones you happened to notice?

If a fix cannot be stated as a criterion plus a reproducible result, it is decoration, not an accessibility revision.

## Common Misreadings

The first error is self-certification: declaring a screen accessible because it looks readable to the designer, who is exactly the wrong judge. The second is treating alt text as a label to satisfy a validator (alt="image") rather than an information equivalent for someone who cannot see the graphic. The third is assuming bright, saturated color is sufficient signaling, when color alone fails any learner who cannot distinguish the hues.

Accessibility is conformance to verifiable criteria for the people the design excludes, not a sighted designer's impression of readability.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Start by listing who the design might exclude: low vision, no vision, color vision deficiency, motor constraints. Each audience maps to specific criteria, which converts a vague goal ("be accessible") into a finite checklist you can actually run.

Then test, do not eyeball. Run text colors through a contrast formula or checker against 4.5:1. Read every informative image's alt text aloud and ask whether a person who cannot see it would now know what it shows. View the screen in grayscale to expose any meaning that lives only in hue. Testing is what turns an impression into evidence.

Finally, record each fix as criterion plus result. "Darkened body text to #333, computed 5.7:1, satisfies 1.4.3" is an audit trail a reviewer can verify, and it is the form your disclosure and traceability artifacts will reuse. Accessibility done this way produces evidence, not just compliance.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: **claim** on the left, **evidence** on the right. The claim is the WCAG criterion you believe a feature violates or satisfies; the evidence is the measured result (the ratio, the alt-text content, the non-color cue). By the end of AIL 606 04.05, your notes should let a reviewer reproduce every accessibility judgment.

A strong reader of an accessibility pass can answer four questions:

1. Which named criterion does each fix satisfy?
2. What is the measured contrast ratio, and does it clear 4.5:1?
3. Does the alt text carry the image's information or just its existence?
4. Which failures were invisible to the designer until tested?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

The artifact is strong when every accessibility change cites a WCAG criterion and a reproducible result, and when the audit covered the failures the designer could not personally perceive. Treat "looks fine" as an unverified claim. Where you are unsure a fix conforms, mark it with the annotation tools and ask BigAL to compare a failing example against a passing one for that exact criterion rather than for general accessibility advice.

A useful final paragraph for the artifact would sound like this: "I darkened the body text to clear 1.4.3 at 5.7:1, rewrote the diagram alt text to describe the workflow for 1.1.1, and added Correct and Incorrect labels for 1.4.1. I rejected the suggestion to brighten the green check, because color alone would still fail color-blind learners."

## Transfer Case

Move the same screen from a desktop web prototype to a printed, black-and-white job aid. Contrast and color-only concerns change shape: the green and red marks become indistinguishable grays in print, so the word labels you added now carry the entire status, and the alt-text criterion no longer applies but a figure caption must do the same information work. Transfer means re-deriving which criteria govern the new medium, not copying the HTML fixes. If you understand WCAG, changing the medium changes which criteria are even in play while the underlying obligation (perceivable to everyone) persists.
