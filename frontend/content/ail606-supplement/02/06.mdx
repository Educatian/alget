## Core Concept

The core idea in this section is that **LXD Optimizer Critical Use** should be treated as a relationship among purpose, constraint, evidence, and revision. Purpose names what the learner or audience needs to accomplish. Constraint names what limits the decision. Evidence names the source that makes the decision defensible. Revision shows what changed after the evidence was considered.

Three course anchors shape the reasoning here:

- **Mayer multimedia learning** helps you decide what counts as a meaningful learning or performance demand.
- **cognitive load theory** helps you notice when a design or technology choice creates risk, burden, or unsupported assumptions.
- **UDL learner variability** helps you check whether the artifact works for varied learners, audiences, or use contexts.

Read the anchors as competing checks on quality. One may highlight the learning demand, another may expose a risk, and another may help you test transfer.

## Worked Example: Triaging an Optimizer Report

An LXD optimizer scanned one module and returned four suggestions. Here is the before-and-after of how a critical user handled them.

**Before (accept-all framing)**

The designer's first instinct: "The tool raised every metric, so apply all four edits."

**The decision point.** Read against the learning goal, the four suggestions split apart:

| Optimizer suggestion | Reported gain | Verdict and reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shorten the worked example from 6 steps to 2 | +14% completion | Reject. Cutting the steps removes the modeled reasoning the example exists to teach. |
| Move the self-check above the reading | +9% time-on-page | Reject. Higher time-on-page reflects confusion, not engagement, when the check precedes the content. |
| Add alt text to two unlabeled diagrams | +3% completion | Accept. Serves accessibility and learning; the gain is a side effect, not the reason. |
| Remove the rarely played audio-description track | +6% efficiency | Reject. The track is an accessibility provision the usage metric cannot value. |

**After (critical-use framing)**

The designer applied one of four edits, kept a one-line reason for each verdict, and added a transfer quiz before and after the accepted change to confirm understanding did not drop.

Designer note (decision level): "I refused to treat the optimizer's proxies as learning. I accepted only the edit that served both a learner need and the goal, and I logged why I rejected the three that moved a metric without serving learning, including one that would have cut an accessibility feature."

The revision is defensible because each optimizer suggestion was judged against the learning goal and learner variability, not accepted as a batch because the dashboard turned green.

## Decision Rule

Use the learning goal first, the media choice second, and the software feature last. A feature is justified only when it reduces unnecessary processing, strengthens useful processing, or makes learner action easier to observe.

A strong submission should survive three quick tests:

1. **Purpose check:** What should the audience be able to do after reading or using the artifact?
2. **Evidence check:** Which exact source, observation, annotation, rubric line, data pattern, or policy boundary justifies the revision?
3. **Revision check:** What changed in the artifact, and what suggestion did you reject or modify?

If you cannot answer all three questions, the artifact may be complete, but it is not yet ready as evidence of learning.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Read this section as a small professional decision cycle, not as a list of terms. First, locate the work product: **storyboard frame sequence with narration, signaling, and reader-action notes**. Then ask what problem the artifact is supposed to solve inside learning-experience design tools. A strong reader can explain the artifact in plain language before naming any theory: who will use it, what decision it supports, and what would count as evidence that it improved.

Next, connect the artifact to the course anchors: **Mayer multimedia learning**, **cognitive load theory**, **UDL learner variability**. The point is not to cite every anchor. The point is to let one anchor change the artifact. If the anchor does not change wording, layout, evidence, timing, or feedback, it has not yet become design reasoning.

Finally, inspect the revision trail. A weak submission says the artifact was improved. A strong submission shows the earlier version, the issue noticed, the support requested, the decision accepted or rejected, and the reason the final version is better for the intended learner or audience.
