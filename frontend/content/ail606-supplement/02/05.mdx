# AI Tutor Boundaries

![AI Tutor Boundaries textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/ail606-supplement/02-05-ai-tutor-boundaries.png)

## Why This Section Matters

When an AI tutor helps you design, it can occupy one of several roles: a generator that produces the artifact, an advisor that proposes options, a critic that questions your reasoning, or an oracle whose claims you accept as true. The boundary you draw around that role determines whether the AI accelerates your judgment or replaces it. This section is about drawing those boundaries on purpose and recording them, so that "I used AI" becomes an account a reviewer can audit rather than a vague admission.

The governing distinction is between the AI making a *proposal* and the AI making a *decision*. A proposal enters your reasoning as one option among others, still subject to your evidence and your goal; a decision is what you alone are accountable for. Boundaries also cover *verification* (who checks the AI's factual claims against course evidence) and *disclosure* (a record specific enough that another person could re-trace what the AI touched and what you did with it). A blanket "I used AI to improve my storyboard" violates all three: it hides which decision the AI touched, whether anyone verified its claims, and where the human judgment was.

## Opening Case

An AI tutor reviews a designer's onboarding flow and suggests merging Frames 2 and 3 to "reduce clicks." It sounds authoritative and the rationale is plausible. The designer almost accepts it, then checks the pilot drop-off data and finds that Frame 3 is exactly where five of twelve learners first interact with the interface. Merging the frames would bury that action cue. The tutor was confidently wrong, not maliciously but because it optimized a proxy (clicks) it could see while blind to the learning evidence it could not.

The case shows why the boundary matters more than the suggestion. The danger was never that the AI made a proposal; proposals are useful. The danger was the gravitational pull to treat the proposal as a verdict because it arrived fluent and certain. The designer held the verification boundary, checked the claim against course evidence, and recorded the rejection. The disclosure of that exchange, not the polish of the final flow, is what makes the work defensible.

## Core Concept

An AI tutor boundary has three components, and a defensible disclosure makes all three visible. The **role boundary** fixes whether the AI proposes or decides; in learning design the AI should stay a proposer and a critic, never the final authority on what serves the learner. The **verification boundary** fixes who is accountable for the truth of the AI's claims; because generative tools produce fluent text regardless of accuracy, every factual or empirical claim ("this cuts three clicks," "learners prefer X") must be checked against course evidence before it can justify a decision. The **disclosure boundary** fixes the granularity of the record; an auditable disclosure names the one decision the AI touched, what it proposed, what evidence you checked it against, and what you accepted or rejected and why.

Why insist on a *bounded* disclosure tied to a single decision rather than a blanket statement? Because accountability is only legible at the level of a specific choice. "I used AI to improve my storyboard" cannot be evaluated; "the tutor proposed merging Frames 2 and 3, I checked it against drop-off data showing Frame 3 is the first interaction point, and I rejected it" can be evaluated, challenged, and learned from.

The course anchors do real work in setting these boundaries:

- **Cognitive load theory** is the standard a proposal must meet: a suggestion to "reduce clicks" is only good if the clicks were extraneous load, not the germane interaction the frame exists to create.
- **UDL learner variability** flags when an AI's efficiency optimization quietly removes an accessibility provision, since usage-based proposals systematically undervalue features used by a minority of learners.
- **Mayer's principles** give you the evidence base to push back on a confident but wrong suggestion, replacing "the AI said so" with "the redundancy principle says otherwise."

## Worked Example: An Annotated AI-Use Disclosure

Here is a before-and-after of one AI-use disclosure excerpt tied to a single bounded design-support decision.

**Before (blanket disclosure)**

> I used AI to help improve my storyboard.

This is not auditable. It does not say which decision the AI touched, what it proposed, what evidence the learner checked it against, or what was accepted or rejected.

**The decision point.** The tutor had suggested merging onboarding Frames 2 and 3 to "reduce clicks." Pilot drop-off data showed that Frame 3 was where 5 of 12 learners first acted on the interface. Merging would have buried that action cue.

**After (bounded, auditable disclosure)**

> AI-use note: I asked the tutor for one way to shorten my onboarding flow. It proposed merging Frames 2 and 3 to cut a click. I checked this against my pilot drop-off data, which showed Frame 3 is where 5 of 12 learners first interacted. I rejected the merge because it would hide that action cue, and I verified the tutor's claim about click count against the actual frame map rather than taking it as authoritative.

Notice the boundaries this disclosure makes visible: the tutor offered a proposal, not a verdict (it did not get to decide); the learner held the verification boundary (checking the claim against course evidence); and the disclosure names the one bounded decision, the evidence, and the reject reason. That is what turns "I used AI" into an account a reviewer can audit.

## Decision Rule

Treat every AI suggestion as a proposal to be tested against your learning goal and your evidence, never as a verdict to be applied. The tool may speak first, but the human verifies and decides last.

Before you accept an AI tutor's suggestion or finalize your disclosure, run these checks:

1. **Role:** Did the AI propose, or did you let it decide? If you cannot name the alternative you chose between, the AI decided for you.
2. **Verification:** Did you check the AI's factual or empirical claim against course evidence, or accept its fluency as authority?
3. **Goal fit:** Does the suggestion serve the learning goal, or only a proxy the tool can measure (clicks, time, completion)?
4. **Disclosure granularity:** Does your record name the one decision, the proposal, the evidence, and the accept/reject reason, so a reviewer could re-trace it?

A disclosure that passes is auditable. "I used AI to help" fails every check and tells a reviewer nothing about where your judgment was.

## Common Misreadings

The first misreading treats disclosure as a confession of guilt, so the goal becomes minimizing what you admit ("I only used it a little"). Disclosure is not an apology; it is documentation of where a tool entered your reasoning and how your judgment governed it. A precise disclosure of substantial, well-verified AI use is more professional than a vague denial.

The second misreading equates the AI's fluency with authority, so a confident suggestion feels like a finding. Generative tools produce equally fluent text whether they are right or wrong; fluency carries no information about accuracy. The boundary that protects you is verification against course evidence, which is exactly what the worked example's designer did when checking the click-count claim against the actual frame map.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Read the two disclosures in the worked example side by side as a study in granularity. The blanket version ("I used AI to help improve my storyboard") is not false; it is simply unauditable, because nothing in it can be checked or challenged. The bounded version names the single decision (shortening the onboarding flow), the proposal (merge Frames 2 and 3), the evidence the designer checked it against (drop-off data showing Frame 3 is the first interaction point for five of twelve learners), and the reasoned rejection. The second is longer not because it confesses more but because it makes the human judgment legible.

Trace where each boundary appears in that disclosure. The role boundary is visible in the phrase "it proposed," which keeps the AI a proposer rather than a decider. The verification boundary is visible in "I verified the tutor's claim about click count against the actual frame map rather than taking it as authoritative." The disclosure boundary is the structure itself: one decision, one proposal, one evidence check, one verdict. Together they convert a vague admission into an account a reviewer can audit.

The walkthrough's lesson is that good AI use and good disclosure are the same skill seen from two sides. You cannot write a bounded disclosure unless you actually maintained the boundaries while working, and maintaining the boundaries while working is what produces a defensible design.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a running log of every AI suggestion you receive in your own work and tag each as proposed-and-verified, proposed-and-rejected, or accepted-without-checking. The third category is the one to watch: it is where the AI quietly crossed from advisor to decider. Most boundary failures are not dramatic; they are small, unverified acceptances that accumulate until the design is no longer yours.

A strong reader of AI tutor boundaries can answer four questions about any AI-assisted decision:

1. Did the AI propose or decide, and can I name the alternative I chose between?
2. What course evidence did I check the AI's claim against before acting on it?
3. Did the suggestion serve the learning goal or only a proxy the tool could measure?
4. Could a reviewer re-trace this exact exchange from my disclosure alone?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A strong artifact for this section is a disclosure tied to exactly one bounded decision, naming the proposal, the evidence check, and the accept-or-reject reason. The strongest disclosures include a rejected suggestion, because a record that only shows accepted AI input cannot demonstrate that the human held the boundary; the rejection is the proof of judgment.

When you use BigAL here, use it deliberately as the proposer so you can practice the boundary in real time, then disclose the exchange. A useful move is: "Propose one change to this onboarding flow and state the assumption your proposal depends on." Asking for the underlying assumption gives you the exact thing to verify against your evidence, and it makes the role boundary explicit from the first turn.

## Transfer Case

Move the same boundary practice from a graduate design course, where disclosure is mostly about academic integrity, to a workplace where you ship learning products under a client contract and a privacy regulation. The role and verification boundaries hold, but the stakes and the verification sources change. In the workplace, an AI suggestion to "personalize using learner data" is not just possibly wrong about learning; it may violate the contract or the privacy rule, so the verification boundary now includes legal and policy evidence, not only pedagogical evidence. A disclosure that satisfied a professor ("I checked it against drop-off data") may be insufficient for a client who needs to know whether learner data left the system. If you can explain why the same three boundaries require a wider evidence base in the regulated workplace, you understand AI tutor boundaries as a transferable accountability discipline rather than a course formality.
