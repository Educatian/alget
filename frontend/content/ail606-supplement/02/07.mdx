## Core Concept

The core idea in this section is that **Comparative Software Audit** should be treated as a relationship among purpose, constraint, evidence, and revision. Purpose names what the learner or audience needs to accomplish. Constraint names what limits the decision. Evidence names the source that makes the decision defensible. Revision shows what changed after the evidence was considered.

Three course anchors shape the reasoning here:

- **Mayer multimedia learning** helps you decide what counts as a meaningful learning or performance demand.
- **cognitive load theory** helps you notice when a design or technology choice creates risk, burden, or unsupported assumptions.
- **UDL learner variability** helps you check whether the artifact works for varied learners, audiences, or use contexts.

Use these anchors as lenses, not as names to drop into a reflection. A useful lens changes what you notice, what you question, and what you revise.

## Worked Example: Building a Comparative Audit Matrix

Here is a before-and-after of an audit comparing three authoring tools for a task defined as "let novice designers build a branching scenario with screen-reader support, on a fixed budget."

**Before (unsound comparison)**

The draft praised each tool on a different axis: "Tool A is cheap, Tool B has the most templates, Tool C is the newest." It then picked Tool B for its template count. No two tools were judged on the same dimension.

**The decision point.** The audit was rebuilt around five shared criteria, scored 0 to 2 for every tool, with accessibility marked as a non-negotiable gate.

| Criterion (gate marked *) | Tool A | Tool B | Tool C |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Branching authoring | 2 | 2 | 1 |
| Screen-reader support * | 2 | 0 | 2 |
| Novice learnability | 2 | 1 | 1 |
| Cost within budget | 2 | 2 | 0 |
| Export to LMS | 1 | 2 | 2 |
| Total | 9 | 7 | 6 |

**After (defensible recommendation)**

Tool B had the most templates but scored 0 on the accessibility gate, which disqualifies it for a cohort that includes screen-reader users. Tool A wins on both total score and the gate.

Audit note (decision level): "I scored all three tools on one shared criteria set rather than praising each on its own strength. Accessibility is a gate, not a weight, so Tool B's template lead cannot rescue a 0 there. The recommendation holds for novice designers on this budget with screen-reader users; if that context changed, Tool B could re-enter."

The audit is defensible because the verdict rests on common criteria, an explicit gate, and a named context, not on a feature count or a per-tool compliment.

## Decision Rule

Use the learning goal first, the media choice second, and the software feature last. A feature is justified only when it reduces unnecessary processing, strengthens useful processing, or makes learner action easier to observe.

Before submitting the storyboard frame sequence with narration, signaling, and reader-action notes, slow down and check whether the trace can answer three questions:

1. **Purpose check:** What should the audience be able to do after reading or using the artifact?
2. **Evidence check:** Which exact source, observation, annotation, rubric line, data pattern, or policy boundary justifies the revision?
3. **Revision check:** What changed in the artifact, and what suggestion did you reject or modify?

If you cannot answer all three questions, the artifact may be complete, but it is not yet ready as evidence of learning.

## Common Misreadings

A common mistake is to treat multimedia quality as visual polish. A cleaner screen can still increase extraneous load if the learner action is unclear.

The trace should show what cognitive demand was noticed, what support was requested, and which change improved the learner's path.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Read this section as a small professional decision cycle, not as a list of terms. First, locate the work product: **storyboard frame sequence with narration, signaling, and reader-action notes**. Then ask what problem the artifact is supposed to solve inside learning-experience design tools. A strong reader can explain the artifact in plain language before naming any theory: who will use it, what decision it supports, and what would count as evidence that it improved.

Next, connect the artifact to the course anchors: **Mayer multimedia learning**, **cognitive load theory**, **UDL learner variability**. The point is not to cite every anchor. The point is to let one anchor change the artifact. If the anchor does not change wording, layout, evidence, timing, or feedback, it has not yet become design reasoning.

Finally, inspect the revision trail. A weak submission says the artifact was improved. A strong submission shows the earlier version, the issue noticed, the support requested, the decision accepted or rejected, and the reason the final version is better for the intended learner or audience.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, keep a two-column note: **claim** on the left, **evidence** on the right. The claim is the decision you think the section is teaching. The evidence is the exact phrase, example, annotation, support move, or artifact revision that makes the claim inspectable. By the end of AIL 606 02.07, your notes should make it possible to reconstruct your reasoning without asking you to explain it again.

A strong reader of Comparative Software Audit can answer four questions:

1. What is the real decision hidden inside this topic?
2. Which constraint makes the decision difficult?
3. What evidence would persuade a skeptical reviewer?
4. What would you revise if the audience, learner, classroom, or tool changed?
