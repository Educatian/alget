# Interaction Patterns for Learning Software

![Interaction Patterns for Learning Software textbook visual](/course-art/deep-exemplars/ail606-supplement/02-03-interaction-patterns-for-learning-software.png)

## Why This Section Matters

An interaction pattern is a reusable solution to a recurring interaction problem: the tabbed comparison, the drag-to-sort, the branching scenario, the spaced-retrieval flashcard, the guided hotspot. Experienced designers do not invent interactions from scratch for every screen; they recognize the problem type and reach for a pattern that has worked before, the way a programmer reaches for a known algorithm. This section is about choosing patterns deliberately, because the wrong pattern can defeat a perfectly good learning goal while looking impressive.

The trap this section disarms is novelty bias: the instinct that a richer, more immersive, more modern interaction must be a better one. A rotatable 3D model feels more advanced than two static tabs, but if the goal is comparison, the 3D interaction can spend the learner's attention on manipulating the object instead of comparing objects. The right question is never "which interaction is most engaging?" but "which pattern's structure mirrors the cognitive operation the learner must perform?"

## Opening Case

A designer shows a stakeholder two prototypes of the same chemistry lesson. One uses a slick, rotatable 3D molecule the learner can spin with a finger; the other uses two plain tabs and a side-by-side overlay. The stakeholder's instinct is immediate: "The 3D one is obviously better, it feels like real software." Then the pilot data arrives. Learners on the 3D version spent forty percent of their time rotating models and only one in six actually placed the molecules side by side to compare them, which was the entire point of the lesson. The impressive interaction had crowded out the cognitive operation it was supposed to support.

The case shows why interaction choices cannot be settled by how the screen feels in a demo. The stakeholder's "obviously better" was a judgment about production value, not about learning. The storyboard's job is to record which cognitive operation the lesson requires (here, comparison) and to justify the chosen pattern against that operation, so that "it feels modern" never gets to win the argument by default.

## Core Concept

The central principle is **interaction-cognition fit**: the structure of an interaction pattern should mirror the cognitive operation the learner must perform. If the operation is *compare*, the pattern should hold both items in view at once and make differences salient (a side-by-side or overlay), because comparison requires simultaneous access. If the operation is *sequence*, a drag-to-order pattern externalizes the ordering the learner is building. If the operation is *decide under consequences*, a branching scenario lets a choice produce a downstream result. When the pattern's structure matches the operation, the interface disappears and the learner's effort lands on the content. When it mismatches, the interface itself becomes a task.

Three failure modes recur, and each maps to an anchor:

- **Split attention** (from cognitive load theory): the pattern forces the learner to hold information from one place while acting in another, as when a 3D model must be rotated to a remembered orientation before it can be compared. The fix is spatial contiguity, putting the to-be-related elements together.
- **Seductive detail** (from Mayer's coherence principle): an interaction adds motion, sound, or interactivity that is engaging but irrelevant to the goal, drawing attention away from the operation. Rotation that is not needed for comparison is seductive detail.
- **Interaction cost asymmetry** (from UDL learner variability): a pattern that is trivial for one learner (precise mouse dragging) is a barrier for another (motor impairment, touchscreen, screen reader). The fix is choosing patterns with low and equitable interaction cost, or offering an equivalent path.

Use these as a triage. Before adopting a pattern because it looks good, ask which of the three failure modes it risks, and whether the cognitive operation actually needs the richness you are about to add.

## Worked Example: Choosing an Interaction Pattern

Here is a before-and-after decision about one interaction in a chemistry module whose goal is "compare two molecular shapes and predict which bonds more readily."

**Before (pattern v1)**

The frame used an animated, rotatable 3D model of each molecule. The designer note read: "3D feels immersive and modern."

**The decision point.** Pilot eye-tracking on 6 learners showed:

1. Learners spent about 40 percent of their time rotating the models.
2. Only 1 of 6 placed the two molecules side by side to compare them.
3. The closing prediction question was answered correctly by 2 of 6.

The interaction produced motion and engagement but not the comparison the learning goal required.

**After (pattern v2)**

The 3D model was replaced with a familiar tabbed comparison: two tabs, each showing a labeled static shape, plus a third tab holding a side-by-side overlay. A drop-to-rank step then asks learners to place "bonds more readily" against "bonds less readily," and the drop closes the loop by revealing a one-line rationale.

Designer note (decision level): "I cut the 3D rotation because the **signaling** and split-attention costs pulled effort away from comparison. The tabbed pattern is familiar, so attention goes to the molecules, not the interface, and the ranking step gives the interaction a visible consequence."

The revision is defensible because the new pattern is justified by evidence (eye-tracking, the 2-of-6 result), the cognitive demand of the task (comparison), and a closed loop (the ranking reveals a rationale), not by which pattern looked more impressive.

## Decision Rule

Name the cognitive operation first, then pick the pattern whose structure matches it, then add richness only if the operation requires it. Richness for its own sake is a liability, not a feature.

Before you lock an interaction pattern, run it through these tests:

1. **Operation fit:** What cognitive operation must the learner perform, and does the pattern's structure mirror it (compare needs simultaneity, sequence needs ordering, decide needs consequence)?
2. **Failure-mode scan:** Does the pattern risk split attention, seductive detail, or interaction-cost asymmetry?
3. **Closed loop:** Does the learner's action produce a visible consequence (a revealed rationale, a downstream branch), or does it just register a click?
4. **Equity:** Can a keyboard or screen-reader learner perform the same operation at comparable cost?

A pattern that passes is justified by the operation it serves. A pattern that fails the first test is decoration, however polished it looks in a demo.

## Common Misreadings

The most common misreading is that interactivity equals learning, so adding more interactions makes a screen more educational. Interactivity helps only when each interaction enacts the cognitive operation the goal requires; an interaction that enacts something else (rotating when the goal is comparing) competes with learning rather than supporting it. Count operation-relevant interactions, not interactions.

A second misreading is that a familiar pattern is a boring one to be avoided. In the worked example, the tabbed comparison "wins" precisely because it is familiar: the learner's attention does not have to be spent learning the interface, so it can go to the molecules. Novelty in the interaction is a cost the learner pays in attention. Spend that cost only when the operation genuinely needs a pattern the learner has not seen.

## Textbook Walkthrough

Replay the worked example as a diagnosis. The eye-tracking data is the symptom: forty percent of time on rotation, one of six placing molecules side by side, two of six answering correctly. The designer reads those numbers through interaction-cognition fit and reaches a precise diagnosis: the 3D pattern enacts *manipulate the object*, but the goal requires *compare two objects*, so the pattern and the operation are mismatched. The mismatch, not the difficulty of chemistry, explains the low scores.

Watch how the replacement pattern is engineered to fit. The tabbed comparison with a side-by-side overlay gives simultaneous access, which is what comparison needs, and being familiar it costs the learner almost no attention to operate. Then the drop-to-rank step is added so the interaction has a consequence: ranking "bonds more readily" against "bonds less readily" forces the comparison to produce a commitment, and revealing a one-line rationale closes the loop. Each design move is traceable to a named principle (spatial contiguity, low interaction cost, closed feedback), not to which prototype looked more impressive.

The lesson of the walkthrough is that interaction quality is measurable behaviorally. You do not have to argue about whether 3D is "better"; you watch where attention goes and whether the operation happens. A designer who collects that evidence can defend a plain pattern over a flashy one to any stakeholder.

## What Strong Reading Looks Like

As you read, build a small catalog: for each interaction pattern you meet, write the cognitive operation it best serves and the failure mode it most easily slips into. Tabbed comparison serves *compare* and rarely fails; carousel serves *browse* but risks hiding items learners need to compare; auto-playing animation serves *observe a process* but courts seductive detail. Over time this catalog becomes the professional judgment that lets you pick a pattern in seconds and defend it.

A strong reader of interaction patterns can answer four questions about any learning interaction:

1. What cognitive operation does this pattern's structure actually enact?
2. Does that match the operation the learning goal requires?
3. Which of the three failure modes (split attention, seductive detail, cost asymmetry) is this pattern most likely to trigger?
4. What behavioral evidence (time-on-task, completion of the operation, transfer) would confirm the pattern is working?

## Stronger Artifact Standard

A strong artifact for this section names the cognitive operation explicitly in the storyboard and justifies the chosen pattern against it, ideally with at least one rejected pattern and the failure mode that ruled it out. The strongest versions cite behavioral evidence (real or projected) for why the chosen pattern lets the operation actually happen, rather than asserting that it "engages" learners.

When you consult BigAL, ask it to argue the opposite pattern. A productive prompt is: "My goal requires the learner to compare two items, and I chose a tabbed side-by-side pattern. Make the strongest case for an alternative pattern, and tell me what learner behavior would prove my choice wrong." Forcing the tool to defend the alternative surfaces failure modes you are too close to see.

## Transfer Case

Keep the cognitive operation (compare two molecular shapes) but change the goal from "predict which bonds more readily" to "explain step by step why one bonds more readily." The operation has shifted from compare to compare-then-explain-a-process, and that should change the pattern. The static side-by-side that served pure comparison may now be too still, because the new goal requires the learner to trace a sequence of interactions over time. Here a controlled, learner-paced animation with the two structures contiguous might earn the richness that the original goal would have rejected as seductive detail. The same content and the same pair of molecules, read against a different operation, can flip your earlier verdict on animation. If you can explain why animation is seductive detail for "predict" but germane for "explain the process," you understand interaction-cognition fit rather than a fixed list of good and bad patterns.
